# Executive summary

## RESPONSE – integRatEd Solutions for POsitive eNergy and reSilient CitiEs

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** RESPONSE – integRatEd Solutions for POsitive eNergy and reSilient CitiEs
- **Alternative Names:** RESPONSE Project, RESPONSE – Positive Energy Neighbourhoods
- **Location:** Dijon (France); Turku (Finland); plus Fellow Cities: Brussels (Belgium), Zaragoza (Spain), Botosani (Romania), Ptolemaida (Greece), Gabrovo (Bulgaria), Severodonetsk (Ukraine)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** 
  - Dijon: 47.3220° N, 5.0415° E
  - Turku: 60.4518° N, 22.2666° E
- **Timeline:** October 2020 – September 2025 (ongoing); with phased implementation, demonstration, monitoring, and replication milestones[^1][^2].
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Covers multiple districts within the two Lighthouse Cities, targeting over 11.2 GWh/year of local renewable energy generation, 3,090 MWh/year energy savings, and 9,799 tons CO₂eq/year emission reduction[^3][^4].
  - 53 partners from 13 countries[^1].
- **Leading Organization(s):** Coordinated by EIFER (European Institute for Energy Research); large consortium of public, private, and research partners[^1].
- **Funding Sources:** EU Horizon 2020; total budget not publicly specified but typical for H2020 lighthouse projects is €15–€25 million; blend of public, private, and municipal co-financing[^1][^5].

#### **Executive Summary**
RESPONSE is a pioneering European initiative under the Horizon 2020 framework to deliver **positive energy districts**—urban neighborhoods that generate more renewable energy than they consume—by integrating advanced digital technologies, smart grids, and citizen engagement. Centered in Dijon and Turku (the “Lighthouse Cities”), with replication in six additional “Fellow Cities,” the project deploys a suite of 86 innovative elements across ten integrated solution packages, ranging from digital twins and real-time analytics to renewable energy systems and e-mobility infrastructure[^3][^1][^2][^4]. RESPONSE’s ambition is not only technical: it seeks to address the “energy trilemma” of security, affordability, and environmental sustainability through a participatory and systemic approach, involving local authorities, residents, and businesses in scenario modeling, demand response, and co-creation of solutions[^2][^4].

By 2025, RESPONSE aims to demonstrate the feasibility and replicability of energy-positive districts, achieve deep carbon reductions, empower citizens, and establish validated roadmaps for other cities. The project’s digital platform enables transparent monitoring and knowledge sharing, while its business models and governance innovations are designed for scalability and long-term financial sustainability[^3][^4]. RESPONSE is thus a flagship model for climate-neutral urban transformation, balancing technology, policy, and social innovation.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**
- **Dijon, France:** Mid-sized city (pop. ~156,000), regional capital of Bourgogne-Franche-Comté, known for its rich architectural heritage and as a university and administrative center. Dijon has ambitious climate and innovation policies, positioning itself as a smart city leader.
- **Turku, Finland:** Pop. ~195,000, key port and university city, historically significant, and a Finnish climate action front-runner. Turku’s urban policy emphasizes sustainability, digitalization, and citizen well-being.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
- Both demonstration areas are mixed-use urban districts with a blend of residential, commercial, and public buildings.
  - **Historical development:** Both cities developed around historic cores, with growth in the 19th–20th centuries leading to current mixed-density, mixed-use neighborhoods.
  - **Land use:** Dense urban fabric with residential blocks, public buildings, and some industrial legacy sites.
  - **Demographics:** Diverse, with a mix of ages, moderate-to-high employment, and a range of income groups; some districts with higher shares of elderly and/or low-income populations.
  - **Infrastructure:** Well-established but varied (aging buildings, legacy heating systems in Dijon; district heating and modern infrastructure in Turku).
  - **Environmental conditions:** Both face climate change risks (heat waves, flooding), urban air quality concerns, and a need for energy transition[^6][^4].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High energy demand and carbon emissions from buildings.
  - Reliance on fossil fuels for heating and electricity.
  - Fragmented energy management systems.
  - Limited citizen awareness or engagement in energy transition.
  - Urban air quality, social equity, and economic resilience concerns[^6][^2][^4].

- **Triggering Events:**
  - EU Green Deal and Horizon 2020 policy push for climate-neutral cities.
  - Local climate emergencies declared in both cities.
  - Community aspirations for improved urban quality of life[^3][^2].

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - City governments (policy, planning, service delivery).
  - Energy utilities and grid operators.
  - Technology providers and research institutions.
  - Citizens and local business associations[^1][^4].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**
  - City governments as project leaders, with multi-level governance involving regional and national authorities.
  - Strong alignment with EU energy, climate, and digitalization directives.

- **Relevant Policies:**
  - EU Horizon 2020 Smart Cities and Communities.
  - National and local climate action plans (e.g. Dijon’s “Smart City” strategy; Turku’s climate neutrality plan).
  - Building codes, zoning, and renewable energy ordinances[^4].

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
  - Supportive EU funding and regulatory frameworks.
  - Some challenges around data privacy, procurement, and integration of new business models with legacy utilities[^4].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Achieve self-sufficient, **positive energy districts** (net annual energy surplus).
  - Demonstrate scalable, replicable models for smart, climate-neutral cities.
  - Empower citizens and stakeholders through digital tools and engagement[^3][^1][^4].

- **Vision Statement:**
  - “Establish a strategic vision for smart cities’ energy transition: climate-neutral cities by 2050, balancing energy security, affordability, and environmental sustainability”[^2][^5].

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**
  - Supports the European Green Deal, national energy transition roadmaps, and city-level “climate neutrality by 2050” commitments.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**
  - Retrofit and integrate buildings, deploy renewables and storage, implement digital twins, and engage citizens to achieve net positive energy and emissions reductions at district scale.
  - Leverage cross-sector digital platforms for optimization, monitoring, and business model innovation[^2][^4].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**
  - Residents (lower bills, better services, empowerment).
  - City governments (policy effectiveness, climate progress).
  - Local businesses (new markets, innovation).
  - Broader urban society (air quality, resilience, jobs).

- **Innovation Elements:**
  - Digital twins for scenario planning and real-time management.
  - Integrated energy systems (renewables, storage, smart grids).
  - Citizen co-creation apps and participatory governance.
  - New business models for energy-positive communities[^3][^1][^4].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** EU and city climate targets, city branding as smart/green leaders.
- **Economic:** Green job creation, economic revitalization, investment attraction.
- **Social:** Citizen well-being, community ownership, energy equity.
- **Environmental:** Decarbonization, adaptation to climate risks, improved air quality.
- **Technological:** Maturation of smart city tech, data analytics, IoT, and renewables[^2][^4].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**
  - Based on “Positive Energy District” (PED) reference frameworks: integrated, citizen-centric, technology-enabled, and systemic urban sustainability[^6][^4].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**
  - Retrofitting and new construction to PED standards.
  - Deployment of solar PV, batteries, heat pumps, and district heating/cooling upgrades.
  - Smart street lighting and e-mobility infrastructure.
- **Scope and Phasing:**
  - Phase 1: Planning, stakeholder engagement, and baseline data collection.
  - Phase 2: Infrastructure deployment and digital platform rollout.
  - Phase 3: Monitoring, optimization, and knowledge sharing.
  - Phase 4: Replication in Fellow Cities and post-project sustainability planning[^4].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Digital twins** for real-time simulation and management.
  - Smart sensors, IoT, and energy analytics platforms.
  - Advanced building management systems (BMS), demand response.
  - PV, wind, battery, and thermal storage systems.
  - E-mobility charging, grid flexibility, and peer-to-peer energy trading[^3][^4].

- **Innovation Type:** Technological (digital, energy), social (citizen engagement), financial (business models), governance (multi-level, participatory), process (co-creation, monitoring).

- **Integration Approach:**
  - Seamless interoperability between energy, mobility, and digital systems.
  - Open data platforms for transparency and replicability[^4].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**
  - Competitive EU and municipal tenders; multi-partner consortiums.
  - Involvement of local SMEs and start-ups where possible.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**
  - Co-design workshops, citizen focus groups, digital engagement tools.
  - Regular feedback loops with residents and businesses.
- **Participatory Elements:**
  - Citizen apps for monitoring, feedback, and behavioral nudging.
  - Crowdsourced scenario planning and community pilots[^4].
- **Project Management:**
  - Central coordination by EIFER; local project offices in each Lighthouse City.
  - Thematic work packages (technology, engagement, replication, etc.).

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Not fully public; estimated €15–25 million range for similar EU projects.
- **Funding Mix:** Mostly Horizon 2020, with contributions from cities, utilities, and private sector[^1][^4].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Energy savings, shared benefits from local energy sales, e-mobility services.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Emphasis on new business models (e.g., energy-as-a-service, community energy ownership); roadmap for post-project financing[^4].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Integration of legacy and new systems; digital twin calibration; scalability of ICT platforms; grid constraints[^4].
- **Financial:** Upfront investment requirements; aligning incentives for public/private partners; ensuring long-term returns.
- **Social and Political:** Engaging diverse communities; addressing digital divides and inclusivity[^1][^4].
- **Administrative:** Coordination across multiple agencies and countries; regulatory hurdles in energy markets.
- **Environmental:** Managing unforeseen impacts (e.g., construction disruption, site contamination).

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**
  - Iterative testing and adjustment of digital platforms.
  - Flexible business models tailored to local market realities.
- **Design Modifications:**
  - Adjusted phasing and timelines in response to COVID-19 and supply chain issues.
  - Enhanced focus on digital literacy and equity in citizen engagement[^4].
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**
  - Early and deep stakeholder involvement essential.
  - Need for robust monitoring and adaptive project management.
  - Importance of interoperable, open-source digital solutions.

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** Retrofitted and newly built positive energy buildings; smart grid and energy storage infrastructure; digital platforms operational in both cities[^4].
- **Service Delivery:** Real-time energy management, citizen dashboards, e-mobility charging, improved district heating/cooling.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Targeted 11.2 GWh/year renewable energy production, 3,090 MWh/year energy savings, and 9,799 tons CO₂eq/year emission reduction[^3][^2][^4].

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Enhanced comfort, lower energy bills, participatory urban governance.
- **Equity Outcomes:** Programs to ensure inclusion of low-income and digitally marginalized groups.
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** No significant displacement reported; active monitoring of equity outcomes.
- **Social Capital:** Increased awareness, engagement, and local capacity for sustainability.
- **Cultural Impacts:** Integration of sustainability with local identities and narratives.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Direct jobs in construction, technology, energy services; indirect jobs in supporting sectors.
- **Economic Activity:** Stimulated local business, attracted investment, promoted innovation ecosystems.
- **Property Values:** Likely uplift in project districts; monitored for affordability risks.
- **Cost Savings:** Lower utility bills, deferred infrastructure costs, improved operational efficiency.
- **Economic Inclusion:** Targeted business models for community ownership and benefit-sharing[^4].

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Significant reduction in district-level CO₂ emissions; enhanced climate resilience.
- **Resource Management:** Efficient water, energy, and material use; circular economy pilots.
- **Environmental Quality:** Improved air quality, noise reduction, increased green space.
- **Waste and Pollution:** Waste minimization in construction and operations.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Performance tracked against EU and local climate/energy targets[^4].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Strengthened local government expertise; new cross-sector governance models.
- **Policy Influence:** Informing future EU, national, and city policies on PEDs and smart cities.
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Public access to performance data via digital platforms.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** Progressing toward (and in some cases exceeding) technical and social KPIs; ongoing monitoring.
- **Unintended Consequences:** Greater-than-anticipated demand for digital literacy support; some delays due to local constraints.
- **Long-term Viability:** Robust post-project sustainability planning; roadmap for scaling and replication[^4].

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:** Some solutions tailored to specific legacy infrastructure and local regulations; core digital and engagement models broadly transferable.
- **Critical Success Factors:** Strong governance, early community engagement, open data, flexible business models, cross-sectoral integration[^6][^4].
- **Scalability:** Designed for district, city, and multi-city replication; active roadmap for scaling across Europe.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:** MAKING-CITY (NL, FI), SmartEnCity (DK, ES, EE), JPI Urban Europe PEDs.
- **Best Practices:** Digital twin deployment, citizen engagement, open innovation, blended financing.

- **Methodological Parallels:** Data-driven optimization, participatory co-design, smart grid integration.

- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Public-private-citizen partnerships, multi-level governance.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Comprehensive integration** of technology, policy, and social innovation.
- Strong focus on **citizen empowerment** and transparent performance monitoring.
- Demonstrated **replicability** and transferability across diverse European contexts.
- Robust **governance and partnership** structures[^1][^4].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Complex **coordination challenges** across partners and sectors.
- Risk of **digital exclusion** for non-tech-savvy residents.
- **Local legal and market barriers** may limit specific solution transferability.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Proactive inclusion measures, but ongoing need for monitoring and adaptation to ensure equitable access and benefit distribution.
- Early evidence suggests improved social cohesion and empowerment, but vigilance required to avoid unintended exclusion[^4].

#### **Future Outlook**

- Plans for **scaling up and replicating** solutions in other European cities.
- Ongoing work to **refine business models** and ensure financial sustainability.
- Anticipated evolution toward **integrated “climate-neutral city” platforms** beyond energy, including mobility, health, and resilience.
- Need for continued focus on **inclusion, adaptability, and policy learning** as urban contexts evolve[^4].
