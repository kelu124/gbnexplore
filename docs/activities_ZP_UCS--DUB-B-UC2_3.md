# Executive summary## IREN Living Lab: Energy Flexibility and Interoperability at the Residential Scale

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** IREN Living Lab: Energy Flexibility and Interoperability at the Residential Scale
- **Alternative Names:** IREN Living Lab; IREN Smart Energy Living Lab; Torino Living Lab (contextual overlap)
- **Location:** Turin Metropolitan Area, Piedmont Region, Northern Italy
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 45.0703° N, 7.6869° E (Turin city center)
- **Timeline:** Initiated 2018 (Living Lab platform); residential energy flexibility pilot launched 2021; ongoing in 2025[^1][^2]
- **Project Scale:** Pilot-scale; covers selected residential blocks (~hundreds of households); scalable to urban district level[^2]
- **Leading Organization(s):** IREN Group (multi-utility company); Turin Municipality; local research institutions
- **Funding Sources:** IREN internal R&D; EU Horizon 2020 support (PRELUDE project); municipal co-funding; private sector partnerships[^2][^3]

#### **Executive Summary**

The **IREN Living Lab** in Northern Italy stands at the forefront of urban experimentation in energy flexibility, aiming to revolutionize residential energy management. The initiative leverages digital platforms to integrate and control various household loads—including appliances, HVAC systems, inverters, and electric vehicle (EV) charging stations—in real time. By dynamically monitoring and managing these loads, the Lab seeks to optimize grid support, enhance user comfort, and facilitate the integration of renewable energy sources[^2][^4].

Key technological features include smart meters, controllable loads, EV charging management, and advanced data analytics for scenario testing and predictive planning. The initiative’s sustainability impact is evident in its improved demand response, reduced peak loads, and lower carbon emissions, with tangible benefits for both grid operators and residents—such as reduced energy bills and enhanced system resilience[^2][^4].

Scalability remains a challenge, as the transition from controlled lab environments to full urban districts requires adaptation in user behavior and infrastructure. Nonetheless, the IREN Living Lab offers a blueprint for European cities aiming for climate neutrality by 2030, providing evidence-based insights that inform city-wide energy policy and urban development strategies[^2][^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Turin, Italy’s fourth-largest city, has a population of ~870,000 (metropolitan area: ~2.2 million). Historically an industrial and automotive center, Turin is now repositioning itself as a leader in innovation, sustainability, and smart city development. The city’s economy is diversifying into ICT, green technologies, and advanced manufacturing[^1][^2].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  The Living Lab targets mixed-use residential districts typical of Turin’s post-war urban expansion—characterized by mid-rise apartment blocks, moderate income diversity, and aging infrastructure. The area features a blend of working-class and middle-income residents, with increasing ethnic diversity. Infrastructure is generally robust but faces modernization needs, especially in energy systems and digital connectivity. Air pollution and urban heat islands are persistent environmental constraints[^1][^2].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - High residential energy consumption and demand peaks  
  - Inadequate grid flexibility to accommodate distributed renewables  
  - Limited user engagement in sustainable energy practices  
  - Rising urban air pollution and carbon emissions

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Italy’s national climate commitments (EU Green Deal, Fit-for-55)  
  - Turin’s 2030 Climate Neutrality pledge  
  - Technological advances in smart metering and IoT

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - IREN Group (utility and project lead)  
  - Turin Municipality (policy and planning)  
  - Residents (energy users and test subjects)  
  - Academic partners (Politecnico di Torino, research support)  
  - Technology providers (smart hardware/software vendors)

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  Local government (Comune di Torino) oversees urban innovation, collaborating with utilities and research institutions.

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Turin City Lab platform for urban innovation[^2][^5]  
  - National/European directives on energy efficiency, renewable integration, and smart buildings (EPBD, SRI)[^3]

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Supportive legal framework for living labs and urban experimentation  
  - Regulatory challenges in scaling pilot innovations to commercial deployment (data privacy, grid codes)

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Demonstrate effective energy flexibility at the residential scale  
  - Enable integration of renewables and EV charging into urban grids  
  - Reduce energy costs and emissions  
  - Build evidence for city-wide energy policy reform

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To create resilient, flexible, and low-carbon urban energy systems that empower residents and support climate neutrality.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Fits within Turin’s 2030 climate and smart city agenda, Italy’s national decarbonization strategy, and EU urban climate initiatives (NetZeroCities, Horizon projects)[^2].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  By deploying digital control and data analytics to residential loads, the initiative enables real-time load shifting, better demand response, and seamless integration of renewables—resulting in lower costs, reduced emissions, and improved grid stability.

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Residents (lower bills, comfort)  
  - Grid operators (stability, resilience)  
  - Municipal government (policy evidence, progress toward climate targets)

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Real-time, user-centric load management  
  - Scenario-based control of EV charging  
  - Data-driven predictive analytics for planning

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  Local and national climate commitments; urban innovation leadership

- **Economic:**  
  Growing market for smart energy solutions; pressure to reduce municipal energy expenditure

- **Social:**  
  Demand for lower utility costs and improved urban health

- **Environmental:**  
  Need to cut urban emissions and improve air quality

- **Technological:**  
  Maturity of IoT, smart metering, and cloud analytics platforms[^2][^4]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Based on living lab principles: open innovation, real-world testing, and multi-stakeholder co-creation[^1][^5]. Emphasizes user involvement and iterative design.

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  Focus on retrofitting existing residential buildings with smart meters, sensors, and controlled EV charging stations. No major new construction, but significant upgrades to existing energy infrastructure.

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Phase 1: Pilot deployment (select households)  
  - Phase 2: Expansion to larger residential blocks  
  - Phase 3: Evaluation and scenario testing for district-wide scaling

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - **Smart meters** for granular energy data  
  - **Controllable loads** (appliances, HVAC, inverters)  
  - **EV charging management systems**  
  - **Digital platform** for real-time monitoring and control  
  - **Data analytics** for predictive and historical scenario analysis[^2][^4]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  Primarily technological; process and governance innovation via living lab methodology

- **Integration Approach:**  
  Technologies interfaced with existing utility infrastructure; digital platforms linked to user apps (e.g., IrenYou)[^4]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  Internal R&D by IREN, with external hardware/software vendors selected for interoperability and scalability

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  Residents recruited through community outreach; feedback integrated into system design

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  Workshops, surveys, and app-based engagement tools for user input and co-creation[^1][^4]

- **Project Management:**  
  Led by IREN’s innovation team, coordinated with municipal agencies and academic partners

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  Pilot phase investment estimated at several hundred thousand euros; full district scaling would require multimillion-euro funding

- **Funding Mix:**  
  Combination of IREN R&D budget, EU research grants (PRELUDE), and municipal co-investment[^2][^3]

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  Potential for service fees, data-driven value-added services, demand response incentives

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  Long-term viability depends on regulatory support and successful business model for smart energy services

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  Interoperability between legacy and new systems; ensuring cybersecurity and data privacy; scaling digital control to larger, more complex districts[^2][^4]

- **Financial:**  
  High upfront costs; uncertain revenue models for smart energy services

- **Social and Political:**  
  User adaptation and behavioral change; skepticism about data sharing; need for trust-building

- **Administrative:**  
  Coordination among multiple stakeholders; bureaucratic inertia in municipal processes

- **Environmental:**  
  Uncertainty in renewable generation profiles; integration with existing pollution abatement strategies

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  Iterative piloting; modular system architecture for phased scaling; stakeholder workshops to address concerns[^1][^4]

- **Design Modifications:**  
  Enhanced user interface for apps; improved data security protocols; flexible hardware choices for interoperability

- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
  Importance of user engagement; need for transparent data policies; value of scenario-based planning

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Demonstrated technical feasibility of energy flexibility at residential scale
- Strong partnership model (utility, municipality, research)
- Evidence-based insights for urban energy policy
- Positive social, economic, and environmental impacts

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Scalability challenges beyond pilot phase
- Need for deeper behavioral adaptation among residents
- Potential digital exclusion of less tech-savvy users
- Uncertainty in long-term financial sustainability

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Proactive inclusion efforts in participant selection
- Risks of digital divide and data privacy must be managed
- Minimal displacement risk at pilot scale; attention needed at expansion

#### **Future Outlook**

- Planned expansion to larger urban districts contingent on continued funding and regulatory adaptation
- Opportunities for integration with broader smart city and climate neutrality programs
- Ongoing challenges: scaling user engagement, adapting business models, maintaining technological interoperability
- Next phases may include integration with other urban systems (mobility, health, circular economy)[^1][^2][^4]
