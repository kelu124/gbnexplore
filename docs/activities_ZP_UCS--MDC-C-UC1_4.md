# Executive summary## EU-DREAM: Effective Uptake of Digital Services to Repower European Consumers and Communities as Active Participants in Energy Transition and Markets

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** EU-DREAM (Effective Uptake of Digital Services to Repower European Consumers and Communities as Active Participants in Energy Transition and Markets)
- **Alternative Names:** DREAM Project, DREAM Living Labs, EU-DREAM Horizon Europe Project
- **Location:** Six living labs across Europe, with prominent sites in Portugal and Denmark. Additional labs in Belgium, Italy, Ireland, and Greece[^1][^2][^3][^4].
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Not explicitly provided for all labs. Example: Porto, Portugal (41.1496° N, 8.6109° W); Aalborg, Denmark (57.0488° N, 9.9217° E)[^5].
- **Timeline:** Launched in 2023. The project is ongoing, with key milestones including living lab deployments and technology demonstrations scheduled through 2026[^1][^3].
- **Project Scale:** Six living labs; population and area affected vary by site (e.g., from single neighborhoods to multi-building residential communities). Involves 17 partners from 9 countries[^2][^4].
- **Leading Organization(s):** University of Porto (Portugal, coordinator), with VITO/EnergyVille (Belgium), Aalborg University (Denmark), EPRI Europe, and 13 additional industry and research partners[^1][^2][^3][^4].
- **Funding Sources:** European Union Horizon Europe Programme (Grant Agreement 101160614), supplemented by national and institutional co-funding[^1][^3][^6].

#### **Executive Summary**
The EU-DREAM project is a multi-country European initiative developed under the Horizon Europe framework, aiming to accelerate the digitalization of energy management in residential neighborhoods through the deployment of **AI-powered, digital-twin-enabled solutions**. The project operates six living labs, each testing different facets of smart energy communities. The Portuguese lab focuses on AI-driven energy optimization in a Mediterranean climate, while the Danish site integrates photovoltaic (PV), wind, batteries, and fuel cells to create a resilient smart home and microgrid environment[^1][^5][^2][^4].

At the core of EU-DREAM is the creation of **user-friendly digital platforms**, utilizing **IoT sensors, AI-based energy management, digital twins, and blockchain-enabled local energy marketplaces**. These technologies enable real-time optimization of energy flows, peer-to-peer trading, and dynamic engagement of residents. A key innovation is the use of an **AI assistant and natural language processing (NLP)** interface, making complex energy decisions accessible to ordinary consumers[^1][^2][^4].

EU-DREAM addresses technical, regulatory, and social challenges to empower consumers, reduce barriers to participation, and validate novel business models for energy communities. The project has demonstrated **up to 40% reduction in CO₂ emissions**, enhanced energy self-sufficiency, and increased resident comfort. By integrating technological, economic, and social innovation, EU-DREAM provides transferable lessons for the future of urban energy systems in Europe[^1][^2][^4][^7].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**
    - **Portugal (Porto):** A major city in northwest Portugal, population ~230,000 (metro ~1.7 million). Economic base: services, industry, logistics, and tourism. Porto is a regional hub with a commitment to sustainability and smart city initiatives.
    - **Denmark (Aalborg):** Northern Danish city, population ~120,000. Known for its technology sector, universities, and progressive energy policies. Aalborg is a leader in renewable integration and smart grid research[^5].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
    - **Portugal Living Lab:** Located in a mixed urban residential area, testing energy solutions in both new and retrofitted buildings. Area characterized by moderate- to middle-income households, Mediterranean climate, and varied building stock.
    - **Denmark Living Lab:** Suburban context with single-family homes, equipped with advanced distributed energy resources (DERs). Focus on tech-savvy and environmentally engaged residents[^5].

    - **Infrastructure:** Both sites feature modernized grid connections, high penetration of renewables, and robust digital communication infrastructure.
    - **Environmental Conditions:** Portugal faces high summer cooling loads, while Denmark must manage winter heating and wind intermittency.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
    - Low consumer engagement in energy transition (80% of Europeans not actively involved)[^1][^2].
    - Complexity and lack of transparency in energy management and market participation.
    - Need for reliable integration of distributed renewables, storage, and flexible demand.
    - Regulatory and interoperability barriers across EU countries.

- **Triggering Events:**
    - EU energy transition policy targets (Fit for 55, Green Deal).
    - Advances in AI and IoT technologies enabling new opportunities for energy management[^8][^9][^10].
    - Post-pandemic focus on resilience and energy affordability.

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
    - Residents (consumers, prosumers), utilities, local governments, technology providers, research institutions, and social innovation organizations[^1][^2][^3][^4].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**
    - EU-level: Horizon Europe, Digitalisation of the Energy System Action Plan, forthcoming AI Act for critical infrastructure[^2][^8][^10].
    - National/local: Smart grid initiatives, renewable integration targets, consumer protection laws.

- **Relevant Policies:**
    - Energy Communities Directive, EU Clean Energy Package, national energy and climate plans.

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
    - Regulation around peer-to-peer energy trading, data privacy, and grid access.
    - Need for regulatory sandboxes to test innovative models in real market conditions[^1][^2][^8].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
    - Empower consumers and communities to actively participate in the energy market.
    - Develop and validate AI-powered digital tools for seamless, user-friendly energy management.
    - Demonstrate scalable models for renewable-powered, resilient urban neighborhoods[^1][^2][^4][^7].

- **Vision Statement:**
    - “Bridge the digital divide in energy, enabling every citizen and community to become an active agent in the sustainable energy transition through accessible, intelligent, and secure digital tools.”[^4][^7]

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**
    - Contributes to EU Green Deal, Horizon Europe missions, Digital Europe Programme, and national smart city strategies.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**
    - If consumers are provided with intuitive digital tools, real-time data, and market access, then they will engage in flexible energy management, support renewable integration, and realize both economic and environmental benefits[^1][^2][^3][^4].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**
    - Residential consumers (including vulnerable groups), local communities, utilities, and energy market actors.

- **Innovation Elements:**
    - AI-powered digital twins, blockchain-based energy markets, NLP interfaces, cross-country living labs for real-world validation[^1][^2][^4][^7].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** EU decarbonization and digitalization mandates.
- **Economic:** Unlocking new market models, reducing energy costs, creating green tech jobs.
- **Social:** Responding to consumer demand for transparency, fairness, and participation; addressing energy poverty.
- **Environmental:** Achieving CO₂ reduction, improving energy efficiency, supporting climate resilience.
- **Technological:** Maturation of IoT, AI/ML, and digital twin technologies; smart grid readiness[^8][^9][^10].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Holistic, consumer-centered design combining technical, market, and social innovation; inspired by Living Lab methodology for co-creation and iterative testing[^1][^2][^3][^4].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** Integration of distributed PV, wind, batteries, fuel cells, EV chargers, and smart appliances in residential settings. Digital overlays via IoT and digital twins[^5].
- **Scope and Phasing:** Multi-year, staged rollout: technology development → pilot deployment in living labs → refinement → cross-site learning → dissemination.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
    - IoT sensors for real-time monitoring and control.
    - AI-based digital twins for predictive energy optimization.
    - Blockchain-enabled local energy marketplaces for peer-to-peer trading.
    - FIWARE platform for interoperability and data management.
    - NLP interfaces for accessible user engagement[^1][^5][^2][^4][^7].

- **Innovation Type:**
    - Technological (AI, blockchain, digital twins).
    - Social (participatory design, consumer empowerment).
    - Financial (new market and revenue models).
    - Governance (regulatory sandboxes, cross-country harmonization).

- **Integration Approach:** Technologies layered onto existing infrastructure, interoperable via open standards (FIWARE), with APIs for utility and third-party integration[^5][^2][^4].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Mix of competitive tenders and direct partnerships among Horizon Europe consortia members[^1][^3].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Community workshops, focus groups, co-design sessions, iterative feedback loops in Living Labs[^1][^2][^4].
- **Participatory Elements:** Residents involved in design, testing, and evaluation; specific focus on vulnerable and digitally excluded groups.
- **Project Management:** Coordinated by University of Porto, with work packages led by partners specializing in market design (VITO), technology (Aalborg University), and dissemination (EPRI Europe)[^1][^2][^3][^4].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Total budget not fully disclosed, typical Horizon Europe projects of this scale range €5-10 million.
- **Funding Mix:** ~80% EU grant, 20% co-financing from partners and national sources[^1][^3].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Piloted local energy markets and flexibility services for potential revenue; long-term models under evaluation.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Ongoing business model validation in Living Labs; plans for commercialization and scale-up post-project.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Ensuring interoperability across diverse national grids; real-world reliability of AI and blockchain platforms; cybersecurity and data privacy[^5][^2][^8][^10].
- **Financial:** Upfront technology investment; uncertain near-term returns in emerging markets.
- **Social and Political:** Recruiting and retaining consumer participants; building trust in digital tools; aligning incentives among stakeholders.
- **Administrative:** Navigating regulatory differences across countries; coordination among 17 partners.
- **Environmental:** Managing variable renewable generation and ensuring resilience during grid events.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Regular cross-lab learning sessions; technical troubleshooting teams; engagement with regulators for sandboxes and pre-approvals.
- **Design Modifications:** Iterative UI/UX redesign based on user feedback; modular technology stack for easier adaptation to local needs.
- **Lessons Learned:** Early and ongoing involvement of end-users is key; regulatory alignment is as important as technical innovation[^1][^2][^4].

---

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** Deployment of rooftop PV, wind turbines, batteries, fuel cells, and smart appliances in hundreds of residential units across six labs[^1][^5].
- **Service Delivery:** Real-time digital energy management, peer-to-peer trading, AI-driven optimization, and consumer dashboards.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Up to 40% reduction in CO₂ emissions; significant increases in energy self-sufficiency and operational cost savings (site-dependent)[^1][^4][^7].

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Greater energy literacy, active engagement in energy decisions, improved comfort and quality of life.
- **Equity Outcomes:** Targeted outreach to vulnerable groups; user-friendly tools lower barriers for digitally excluded populations.
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** No evidence of residential displacement; focus on inclusion.
- **Social Capital:** Strengthened local networks through collaborative energy initiatives.
- **Cultural Impacts:** Fosters a new culture of energy citizenship and sustainability.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** New roles in energy management, technology integration, and community facilitation.
- **Economic Activity:** Stimulates local clean tech markets; potential for new business models in energy services.
- **Property Values:** Likely positive, though not systematically measured.
- **Cost Savings:** Lower energy bills and operational costs for participating residents; avoided infrastructure upgrades for utilities.
- **Economic Inclusion:** Models for revenue sharing and market participation broaden access to energy transition benefits.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Demonstrated up to 40% CO₂ reduction at lab level.
- **Resource Management:** Improved efficiency in electricity, heat, and storage utilization.
- **Environmental Quality:** Indirect improvements in air quality and noise through electrification and load shifting.
- **Waste and Pollution:** No significant increase; digitalization reduces paper and manual reporting.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Labs designed to meet high energy performance standards; aiming for TRL 6-7 technologies[^1][^3][^4][^7].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Builds expertise among municipal staff and utilities for digital energy governance.
- **Policy Influence:** Early results informing EU digital energy and AI regulation discussions[^2][^8][^10].
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Blockchain and open data approaches increase trust and system transparency.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** High—validated technical, social, and economic benefits; strong user engagement; regulatory learning.
- **Unintended Consequences:** Ongoing concern about digital divide; need for further work on privacy and ethical AI.
- **Long-term Viability:** Positive outlook if regulatory and market conditions evolve to support continued innovation and scale.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:** Success depends on advanced digital infrastructure, supportive regulatory environment, and engaged communities.
- **Critical Success Factors:** Strong cross-sectoral partnerships, regulatory flexibility, iterative user engagement, modular technology design.
- **Scalability:** High potential, especially in other urbanized European contexts with similar grid and policy structures.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:** Analogous to pilot projects in Germany (E.ON), Italy (Enel), and the UK (National Grid ESO) using AI for smart grid management[^8].
- **Best Practices:** Emphasis on Living Lab methodology, user-centered design, and open standards.
- **Cautionary Lessons:** Beware of overestimating consumer digital literacy; ensure regulatory adaptability and robust cybersecurity.

- **Contextual Similarities:** Urban neighborhoods, mixed building stock, need for renewable integration and consumer engagement.
- **Methodological Parallels:** Digital twins, AI-driven energy management, participatory living labs.
- **Technological Overlaps:** IoT, AI, blockchain, open data platforms.
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Multi-actor governance; public-private-academic collaboration.
- **Transferable Strategies:** Modular tech stack, regulatory sandboxing, continuous user feedback loops.
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Adjust for local regulatory context, digital literacy, climate, and market structure.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Holistic Approach:** Integrates technology, market design, and social innovation.
- **Demonstrated Impact:** Quantifiable environmental and economic benefits.
- **User-Centered:** Prioritizes accessibility and engagement, especially for vulnerable groups.
- **Scalability:** Designed for replication and adaptation across Europe.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Digital Divide:** Risk that less digitally literate or lower-income groups may be left behind without targeted support.
- **Complexity:** High technological and regulatory complexity can slow deployment and scaling.
- **Market Uncertainty:** Business models for local energy markets and flexibility services still emerging.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Positive Steps:** Explicit focus on inclusion, outreach, and co-design with marginalized groups.
- **Ongoing Risks:** Must guard against new forms of exclusion as digitalization advances.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Long-term Trajectory:** Likely to inform EU and national policy, shape future urban energy communities, and accelerate market adoption of AI-powered energy tools.
- **Ongoing Challenges:** Regulatory harmonization, market incentives, and consumer trust.
- **Next Phases:** Scaling to additional cities, refining business models, deepening integration with broader smart city and climate strategies[^1][^2][^3][^4][^7].
