# Executive summary## Sharing Cities – Smart Urban Energy Platform

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Sharing Cities – Smart Urban Energy Platform
- **Alternative Names:** Sharing Cities H2020 Project, Sharing Cities Smart City Demonstrators
- **Location:** Milan (Porta Romana/Vettabbia), Italy; London (Royal Borough of Greenwich), UK; Lisbon (Downtown), Portugal
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Milan (45.4642° N, 9.1900° E), London (51.4826° N, 0.0077° E), Lisbon (38.7223° N, 9.1393° W)
- **Timeline:** 2016–2021 (official completion December 2021)[^1]; key milestones include project launch (2016), implementation of pilots (2017–2020), dissemination and scale-up activities (2020–2021)
- **Project Scale:** €24 million seed funding from Horizon 2020, €265 million further investment leveraged, impacting demonstration districts and over 15,000 residents directly[^1][^2][^3]
- **Leading Organization(s):** City municipalities of Milan, London, Lisbon; technical partners including Fondazione Politecnico di Milano, Greater London Authority, Lisboa E-Nova; academic and private sector partners[^1][^4][^5][^6]
- **Funding Sources:** European Commission Horizon 2020 grant, local public funds, private investment, collaborative partnerships[^1][^2]

#### **Executive Summary**
The Sharing Cities – Smart Urban Energy Platform is a landmark European initiative aimed at transforming urban energy systems and mobility through integrated digital platforms, building retrofits, and community engagement. Operating in Milan, London, and Lisbon, the project implemented “smart” solutions including urban digital twins, real-time IoT integration, and citizen co-design tools. By focusing on demonstration districts, it piloted deep energy retrofits, smart grids, e-mobility services, and digital platforms for collaborative urban management. The project not only achieved notable reductions in energy use and CO₂ emissions but also fostered new business models, jobs, and knowledge sharing. Stakeholder engagement was central, with participatory mechanisms and open data platforms empowering residents and local organizations. Sharing Cities serves as a replicable blueprint for European cities, highlighting the critical role of digital infrastructure, data-driven decision-making, and inclusive governance in accelerating the transition to sustainable, resilient urban neighborhoods[^1][^2][^3][^7].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  - **Milan:** Population ~1.4 million; economic base in industry, fashion, financial services; dense metropolitan area with a legacy of industrial and residential zones[^2].  
  - **London (Greenwich):** One of London’s historic boroughs, diverse population, mixed-use urban fabric, significant regeneration activity.  
  - **Lisbon:** Capital city, ~500,000 residents; rapid urban change, vibrant cultural scene, focus on innovation and sustainability[^1][^6].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - **Milan (Porta Romana/Vettabbia):** Mix of historic and modern buildings, social housing, commercial activity; transitioned from industrial to mixed-use, with varying income levels and infrastructure quality[^2][^5].  
  - **Greenwich:** Urban regeneration zone, blend of public housing, retail, and transport corridors.  
  - **Lisbon (Downtown):** Dense, central, historic district, facing pressure from tourism, aging infrastructure, and air quality challenges[^6].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**
- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High energy consumption and CO₂ emissions
  - Aging building stock with poor energy efficiency
  - Fragmented data and lack of coordinated urban management
  - Air pollution, traffic congestion, and social disparities
  - Limited citizen involvement in sustainability planning[^1][^2][^3][^6]

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - EU-wide climate targets, Horizon 2020 funding opportunities, city-level commitments to decarbonization, and local advocacy for improved urban quality[^1][^2].

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Municipal governments, energy utilities, technology providers, academic institutions, local communities, NGOs, and citizen groups[^1][^4][^5].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**
- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Multi-level governance involving city councils, national governments, and EU bodies[^1].
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - EU Horizon 2020 Smart Cities Mission, local climate action plans, building retrofit incentives, mobility transition policies[^1][^2][^3].
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - GDPR-compliant data management, public procurement regulations, urban planning codes, energy market liberalization, institutional collaboration agreements[^1][^4].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**
- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Demonstrate and replicate digital platforms for urban energy management, mobility, and citizen engagement
  - Achieve measurable reductions in energy use, emissions, and resource consumption
  - Build scalable business and governance models for green neighborhoods[^1][^2][^3]

- **Vision Statement:**  
  - “Build smart cities together using common solutions for shared urban challenges, leveraging digital innovation and collaborative models to create sustainable, inclusive neighborhoods.”[^1]

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Integrated with EU climate and digital agendas, national sustainability strategies, and citywide decarbonization plans[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**
- **Theory of Change:**  
  - Deploy integrated digital platforms, retrofit buildings, and incentivize sustainable behaviors. Use data-driven approaches to optimize investments and interventions, resulting in energy savings, emissions reductions, and improved urban quality[^1][^2][^3].
- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Residents of demonstration districts (including social housing), small businesses, local governments, and wider urban communities[^2][^3].
- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Urban digital twins, open data portals, participatory co-design, integrated energy management systems, smart mobility services[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**
- **Political Drivers:**  
  - Commitments to EU climate targets, mayoral leadership, cross-city collaboration[^1].
- **Economic Drivers:**  
  - Opportunities for green investment, job creation, reduction of energy costs[^2].
- **Social Drivers:**  
  - Community advocacy for better living conditions, equity in energy access[^2][^3].
- **Environmental Drivers:**  
  - Climate adaptation, air quality improvement, resource conservation[^1][^2].
- **Technological Drivers:**  
  - Advancements in IoT, digital twins, smart grid technologies, open data infrastructure[^1][^2][^3].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**
- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Holistic smart city paradigm—integrating technology, people, and place through participatory and data-driven planning[^1].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Building retrofits (insulation, digital controls), installation of smart lamp posts, e-vehicle charging points, urban sharing platforms, and mobility hubs[^1][^2][^3].
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial pilots in three lighthouse cities, followed by replication in fellow cities. Phased implementation: planning, demonstration, evaluation, dissemination[^1][^4][^3].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**
- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Urban digital twins, IoT sensors, integrated energy management systems, community dashboards, building management systems, smart lighting, e-mobility platforms[^1][^2][^3].
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (digital platforms, sensors), social (co-design, behavioral incentives), financial (new business models), governance (cross-city collaboration)[^1][^2][^3].
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Platforms interface with existing city data stores, grid infrastructure, and mobility networks; interoperability plans and open APIs foster integration[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Implementation Methodology**
- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Competitive tenders for technology providers, collaborative partnerships across sectors[^1][^2].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Co-design workshops with residents, businesses, and government agencies; regular participatory events; digital social markets[^2][^3].
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Citizen co-design platforms, public dashboards, behavioral incentive schemes[^1][^2][^3].
- **Project Management:**  
  - Multi-city program coordination, local project managers, advisory committees, technical working groups[^1][^4][^7][^5].

#### **Financing Model**
- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - €24 million initial Horizon 2020 grant, €265 million further investment; ~$300 million in Milan alone from 2015–2021[^1][^2].
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - EU grant (~8%), local public funds (~12%), private/PPP investment (~80%)[^1][^2].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Business models for energy services, mobility platform fees, potential land value capture[^1][^2].
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Focus on scalable solutions, business case development, and replication to attract ongoing investment[^1][^2][^3].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**
- **Technical Challenges:**  
  - Harmonizing digital infrastructure across varied building types and legacy systems; integration of IoT with old infrastructure; ensuring data interoperability[^1][^2][^3].
- **Financial Challenges:**  
  - Managing large-scale investment, aligning funding streams, ensuring cost-effectiveness[^1][^2].
- **Social and Political Challenges:**  
  - Stakeholder buy-in, overcoming “smart city fatigue,” addressing privacy concerns, equity issues[^1][^2].
- **Administrative Challenges:**  
  - Coordination across cities, bureaucratic procedures, procurement delays[^1].
- **Environmental Challenges:**  
  - Unforeseen impacts (e.g., construction waste), adaptation to climate variabilities[^2].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**
- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Iterative co-design, pilot testing, feedback loops, regular stakeholder consultations[^2][^3].
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Adjusted digital platforms for local needs, enhanced privacy controls, expanded citizen engagement mechanisms[^1][^2].
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
  - Importance of open standards, flexible design, cross-sector partnerships, and robust data governance[^1][^2][^3].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**
- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - Deep retrofits of ~20 buildings in Milan; smart lamp posts and EV chargers in all cities; integrated energy management systems[^2][^3].
- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Improved energy services, shared mobility (e-bikes, e-cars), citizen dashboards, open data access[^1][^2][^3].
- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - 6.1 GWh/year decrease in building energy needs; 4,500 tons CO₂ saved via mobility measures; 1,600 e-vehicles deployed; 15,000+ people affected; 100+ jobs created in demonstration zones[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Social Impacts**
- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Reduced energy poverty, increased comfort in social housing, improved air quality, participatory urban management[^2][^3].
- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - Benefits targeted to low-income and vulnerable residents, especially through social housing retrofits[^2].
- **Displacement and Gentrification:**  
  - No significant evidence of displacement; efforts made to prioritize inclusivity[^2].
- **Social Capital:**  
  - Strengthened networks via co-design, increased civic engagement through digital platforms[^1][^2].
- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Reinforced local identity via participatory planning, preserved heritage in historic districts[^2].

#### **Economic Impacts**
- **Job Creation:**  
  - 100+ full-time jobs created via building retrofits and smart services; indirect employment via innovation-driven business growth[^2][^3].
- **Economic Activity:**  
  - €265 million additional investment; new business models and market opportunities for SMEs[^1][^2].
- **Property Values:**  
  - Likely uplift in values due to improved energy performance and urban quality; precise data limited[^2].
- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Lowered energy bills for residents; operational savings for municipalities[^2][^3].
- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - Focus on social housing and affordable mobility fosters inclusion[^2][^3].

#### **Environmental Impacts**
- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - Significant CO₂ reductions (Milan: 500 tons/year, overall: 4,500 tons from mobility); progress toward net-zero targets[^2].
- **Resource Management:**  
  - Optimized energy use via demand response and integrated management; increased share of renewables[^2][^3].
- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - Improved air quality, reduced noise (via EVs), expanded green infrastructure[^1][^2][^3].
- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - Improved waste management through digitization; pollution reduction from mobility shift[^1][^2].
- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - Achieved 50% energy savings in retrofitted buildings; performance tracked via digital dashboards[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**
- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Enhanced via multi-city collaboration, open data tools, and innovation partnerships[^1][^2].
- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Led to replication in “fellow” cities; influenced city and EU policy on smart cities and climate[^1][^4].
- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - Open data portals, participatory mechanisms, and public reporting improved governance[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Performance Against Objectives**
- **Success Metrics:**  
  - All main objectives met or exceeded; notable scaling and replication achieved[^1][^2].
- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Some technical complexity and stakeholder fatigue; overall, impacts overwhelmingly positive[^1][^2].
- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Business cases and knowledge dissemination support sustained impact and further scale-up[^1][^2][^3].

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- **Integrated Approach:** Combined physical infrastructure, digital platforms, and participatory governance for holistic impact[^1][^2].
- **Scalability:** Demonstrated replication across cities and districts; clear documentation and playbooks support transferability[^1][^3].
- **Measurable Impact:** Significant reductions in energy use and emissions, robust social and economic benefits[^2][^3].
- **Citizen Engagement:** Strong participatory mechanisms and empowerment of local communities[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- **Technical Complexity:** Platform integration and legacy system harmonization proved challenging[^1][^2].
- **Stakeholder Fatigue:** Sustained engagement difficult over multi-year program; some resistance to change[^2].
- **Equity Gaps:** While social inclusion was prioritized, full equity impact assessment requires more longitudinal data[^2].

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- **Positive Outcomes:** Targeted retrofits in social housing, participatory planning, open data tools advanced inclusion[^2][^3].
- **Potential Gaps:** Need for deeper engagement with marginalized groups and continued monitoring of equity impacts[^2].

#### **Future Outlook**
- **Long-Term Trajectory:** Ongoing partnerships and replication in new cities; integration with broader EU smart city and climate initiatives[^1][^4][^3].
- **Ongoing Challenges:** Maintaining momentum post-funding, scaling up without losing focus on inclusion and participation[^1][^2].
- **Evolution/Next Phases:** Expansion to more cities; refinement of digital platforms; continued dissemination of best practices and lessons learned[^1][^2][^3].
