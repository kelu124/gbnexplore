# Executive summary## Tivoli GreenCity – The First Sustainable Neighbourhood in Brussels

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Tivoli GreenCity
- **Alternative Names:** Tivoli eco-neighbourhood, Tivoli sustainable district
- **Location:** Laeken district, Brussels, Belgium
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Positioned between the dense residential area of Laeken and the industrial Port of Brussels zone
- **Timeline:** Conception in early 2010s; inaugurated in two phases in 2016 and 2019; ongoing operations and evaluation (impact study conducted October 2023 – June 2024)
- **Project Scale:** 4.5 hectares; approximately 397–400 housing units; 10,000 m² of public spaces
- **Leading Organization(s):** citydev.brussels (public economic and urban development operator); construction and development partnerships including BESIX; Sciences Po Urban School (Paris) for impact evaluation
- **Funding Sources:** Public investment through citydev.brussels; mixed funding model combining public and private capital

#### **Executive Summary**

Tivoli GreenCity represents a pioneering sustainable urban development initiative in Brussels, Belgium, designed to demonstrate how environmental performance, social inclusion, and economic viability can be integrated within a dense urban context.[^1][^2] Inaugurated in two phases between 2016 and 2019, the 4.5-hectare neighbourhood comprises approximately 397–400 passive and zero-energy housing units, with 271 subsidised (open-access) homes and 126 social housing units, complemented by retail spaces, two eco-crèches, public green spaces, and an incubator for sustainable enterprises (Greenbizz).[^1][^2] The initiative strategically bridges the existing dense residential district of Laeken with the industrial Port of Brussels, transforming a transitional urban zone into a mixed-use, multifunctional neighbourhood.

The project has received recognition as an exemplary sustainable development operation, earning 11 prizes and certifications both within Belgium and internationally.[^1] Its design incorporates advanced environmental technologies including passive house construction, zero-energy building techniques (35% of housing units), green roofs, rainwater harvesting systems, rooftop vegetable gardens, and collective infrastructure for resource efficiency.[^1][^3] Beyond technological innovation, Tivoli GreenCity emphasizes functional mixing—integrating residential, commercial, and productive activities—and participatory governance involving residents in decision-making processes.

Significantly, the initiative has evolved from a design-phase laboratory for sustainable urbanism into a lived neighbourhood, prompting critical evaluation of how theoretical sustainability objectives translate into residents' daily practices. An impact study commissioned by citydev.brussels and conducted by Sciences Po Urban School (2023–2024) provides valuable insights into this transition, revealing both successful performance in energy efficiency and important gaps between design intentions and actual use patterns in shared spaces.[^1][^2] This case study demonstrates both the potential and the complexities of implementing comprehensive sustainable neighbourhood development in European metropolitan contexts.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:** Brussels, the capital of Belgium and the de facto capital of the European Union, is a major metropolitan centre with a population exceeding 1.2 million in the city proper and over 2 million in the metropolitan region. The city functions as a complex hub of political, administrative, economic, and cultural activity, characterized by significant income inequality, diverse immigrant populations, and a mix of historic urban fabric with modern development. Brussels faces typical metropolitan challenges including housing shortages, traffic congestion, environmental degradation in industrial zones, and uneven distribution of green space and services across neighbourhoods.

**Neighbourhood/District Characteristics:** The Laeken district, where Tivoli GreenCity is located, represents a transitional urban zone historically shaped by industrial and port activities. Before the initiative, the area exhibited characteristics typical of post-industrial European cities: a dense residential district with mixed-income populations; adjacent industrial and port infrastructure; fragmented land use patterns; and environmental pressures from historical industrial activity. The neighbourhood sits at the intersection of residential Brussels and the functioning Port of Brussels, creating both constraints (noise, pollution, traffic) and opportunities (available land, economic activity). The area's demographic profile includes working-class and lower-middle-income residents, with significant immigrant communities reflecting Brussels' multicultural character. Infrastructure quality was variable, with older residential buildings and aging utilities typical of early-20th-century urban fabric.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:** The initiative addressed multiple interconnected urban challenges:

- **Housing Shortage and Affordability Crisis:** Brussels faced acute shortages of affordable housing, particularly for lower-income households, with market-rate development dominating available sites.
- **Environmental Degradation:** Industrial legacy contamination, air and noise pollution from port and transport activities, and limited green space characterized the area.
- **Social Fragmentation:** The transitional zone between residential and industrial areas lacked cohesive community infrastructure and public spaces that could foster social interaction.
- **Economic Stagnation:** The industrial zone was underutilized relative to its potential, with limited opportunity for integration of sustainable economic activities.
- **Climate and Resource Vulnerability:** Brussels, like other European cities, faced increasing pressure to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and improve resource efficiency in response to climate change imperatives.
- **Urban Disconnection:** The physical and functional separation between residential Laeken and the Port of Brussels created barriers to integrated urban development.

**Triggering Events:** The initiative emerged within a broader context of European sustainability commitments and urban policy evolution in the early 2010s. Key catalysts included: the growing recognition of climate change imperatives and EU environmental regulations; Brussels' ambitions to position itself as a sustainable metropolitan centre; citydev.brussels' strategic mandate to develop mixed-use neighbourhoods addressing housing and economic development simultaneously; and emerging international discourse on sustainable urbanism and eco-neighbourhood design.

**Stakeholder Landscape:** Pre-initiative stakeholders included: citydev.brussels (the public development agency); Brussels municipal and regional government; existing residents of Laeken; port and industrial operators; environmental organizations; housing advocates; and private developers and construction firms. Interests varied considerably: residents were concerned about gentrification, displacement, and environmental quality; port operators sought to maintain industrial functionality; environmental advocates pressed for sustainability measures; developers sought profitable projects; and government agencies balanced multiple policy objectives.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:** Tivoli GreenCity operated within Belgium's complex federal structure, involving multiple governance levels: the Brussels-Capital Region (regional government); the City of Brussels (municipal government); and citydev.brussels, a public economic interest grouping created specifically to implement urban development projects. This multi-level governance structure required coordination across different administrative authorities and policy domains.

**Relevant Policies:** The initiative was shaped by several policy frameworks:[^1][^2]

- **Brussels Urban Development Strategy:** Citydev.brussels' mandate to create mixed-use neighbourhoods combining housing, business space, and community facilities
- **European Sustainability Standards:** Alignment with EU environmental regulations and climate commitments
- **Belgian Housing Policy:** Requirements for integrating affordable and social housing within development projects
- **Brussels Green Infrastructure Strategy:** Policies promoting green roofs, urban gardens, and ecological corridors
- **Circular Economy Initiatives:** Support for sustainable businesses and green enterprise development, reflected in the Greenbizz incubator

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** Enabling factors included citydev.brussels' institutional capacity to coordinate complex projects; supportive regional policy frameworks for sustainable development; and availability of public land for development. Barriers included: the complexity of coordinating multiple governance levels; tensions between sustainability objectives and market-rate development pressures; regulatory requirements for social housing integration that increased project complexity; and the challenge of securing adequate funding for environmental infrastructure while maintaining affordability targets.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:** Tivoli GreenCity was explicitly designed to achieve multiple, interconnected objectives:[^1][^2]

- Create a model sustainable neighbourhood demonstrating that environmental performance and social inclusion could be achieved simultaneously within dense urban contexts
- Develop approximately 400 housing units with a significant proportion (271 of 397 units) as subsidised or social housing, addressing Brussels' affordable housing crisis
- Integrate sustainable economic activities through the Greenbizz incubator, fostering green jobs and circular economy enterprises
- Establish innovative environmental performance standards, including passive house construction and zero-energy buildings for a substantial portion of units
- Create multifunctional public spaces and shared infrastructure fostering social cohesion and community interaction
- Demonstrate effective land use integration between residential, commercial, productive, and recreational functions
- Serve as a "laboratory for innovation" in sustainable urbanism, generating transferable knowledge for future projects

**Vision Statement:** Tivoli GreenCity aimed to create a harmonious connection between the existing dense residential area of Laeken and the industrial zone of the Port of Brussels,[^4] transforming a fragmented urban zone into an integrated, livable neighbourhood that balanced environmental sustainability, social equity, and economic vitality. The vision positioned the neighbourhood as a demonstration of how Brussels could evolve toward a more sustainable metropolitan future while maintaining affordability and social inclusion.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:** The initiative aligned with multiple strategic frameworks: citydev.brussels' institutional mission to combine housing, businesses, and community facilities; Brussels' regional sustainability ambitions and climate commitments; European Union environmental and urban development policies; and broader international discourse on sustainable cities and eco-neighbourhoods. The project positioned itself within the emerging "eco-neighbourhood" movement in European cities, contributing to global experimentation with sustainable urban development models.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:** The initiative operated on a causal logic connecting design interventions to sustainability outcomes: advanced building technologies (passive house, zero-energy construction) would reduce operational energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions; green infrastructure (rooftop gardens, green roofs, rainwater harvesting) would improve environmental quality and climate resilience; mixed-use design combining housing, retail, and productive activities would reduce transportation needs and foster economic vitality; integrated public spaces and shared facilities would generate social cohesion and community engagement; and the combination of these elements would create a replicable model demonstrating sustainable urban development feasibility.

**Target Beneficiaries:** Primary beneficiaries were intended to include: residents of the new neighbourhood (particularly lower-income households accessing subsidised and social housing); workers in sustainable enterprises housed in Greenbizz; surrounding Laeken residents benefiting from improved infrastructure and public space; Brussels residents and the broader metropolitan region benefiting from reduced emissions and a demonstration project; and future urban developers and policymakers learning from the initiative's successes and challenges.

**Innovation Elements:** Tivoli GreenCity was distinctive in several respects: the comprehensive integration of passive house and zero-energy construction at neighbourhood scale (35% zero-energy units); the combination of environmental performance with significant affordable housing provision (68% of units as subsidised or social); the inclusion of a dedicated green business incubator (Greenbizz) within residential development; the explicit incorporation of participatory governance in the planning phase; and the commitment to systematic impact evaluation through the Sciences Po Urban School study, creating an open-data evaluation matrix (71 indicators across 36 criteria) designed for replication and adaptive learning.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers:** Brussels' ambitions to position itself as a sustainable European capital; regional government commitment to innovative urban development; and citydev.brussels' institutional mandate to demonstrate integrated development models combining housing, economic activity, and sustainability.

**Economic Drivers:** Recognition that sustainable development could attract investment and create economic opportunity; the potential for green business development through Greenbizz; and the possibility of demonstrating financially viable models for integrating affordable housing with market-rate development.

**Social Drivers:** Brussels' acute affordable housing shortage; advocacy for inclusive urban development; and recognition that sustainable neighbourhoods required strong community engagement and social cohesion to function effectively.

**Environmental Drivers:** EU climate commitments and greenhouse gas reduction targets; Brussels' vulnerability to climate impacts (flooding, heat stress); recognition of the built environment's significant role in emissions; and broader European momentum toward sustainable urbanism.

**Technological Drivers:** Maturation and cost-reduction of passive house and renewable energy technologies; advances in green infrastructure design; and emerging digital tools for energy monitoring and smart building management.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:** Tivoli GreenCity drew on several urban design and planning philosophies: the "eco-neighbourhood" model emphasizing integrated environmental, social, and economic sustainability; new urbanism principles promoting mixed-use development and pedestrian-oriented design; the "circular economy" approach minimizing waste and maximizing resource efficiency; and participatory planning methodologies involving residents in design decisions. The project positioned itself as a "laboratory for innovation," intentionally testing approaches to sustainable urbanism that could inform future development.

**Physical/Spatial Design:** The neighbourhood comprised several integrated components:[^1][^2][^3][^5]

- **Residential Development:** Approximately 397–400 housing units designed to passive house standards, with 35% achieving zero-energy performance; 271 subsidised (open-access) units and 126 social housing units ensuring economic diversity
- **Public Spaces:** 10,000 m² of public space including the Jardin des Justes (central square), green corridors, and landscaped areas designed to encourage social interaction and ecological function
- **Shared Infrastructure:** Rooftop vegetable gardens, collective laundry facilities, shared greenhouses, and communal spaces designed to foster resource efficiency and community engagement
- **Commercial and Productive Activities:** 7 retail outlets supporting residential functions; the Greenbizz incubator housing young companies in environmental, social, and circular economy sectors; production workshops and a FabLab for maker activities
- **Community Facilities:** 2 eco-crèches (childcare facilities) designed to high sustainability standards
- **Green Infrastructure:** Extensive green roofs, rainwater harvesting and management systems, ecological corridors, and biodiversity enhancement measures
- **Transportation:** Design prioritizing pedestrian and cycling access; integration with Brussels' public transport network; reduced parking provision relative to conventional developments

**Scope and Phasing:** The project was implemented in two phases: Phase 1 (inaugurated 2016) and Phase 2 (inaugurated 2019), allowing for adaptive learning and refinement between phases. This phased approach permitted evaluation of early outcomes and adjustment of design or management strategies for subsequent phases.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:** Tivoli GreenCity incorporated multiple technological systems:[^1][^3][^6]

- **Building Envelope Technologies:** Advanced insulation, high-performance windows, airtight construction achieving passive house standards
- **Renewable Energy Systems:** Solar photovoltaic installations; district heating/cooling systems; heat recovery ventilation
- **Water Management:** Rainwater harvesting and storage systems; greywater recycling; permeable paving; bioswales for stormwater management
- **Smart Energy Management:** Building automation systems; energy monitoring and management platforms; smart metering for consumption tracking
- **Green Infrastructure:** Extensive green roofs and living walls; rooftop gardens and vegetable production areas; ecological corridor design
- **Waste Management:** Integrated waste sorting and composting systems; shared facilities for resource efficiency
- **Digital Platforms:** Monitoring systems for energy consumption and environmental performance; community engagement platforms

**Innovation Type:** The innovations encompassed multiple categories: technological (advanced building systems, renewable energy, smart monitoring); social (participatory design processes, shared facilities for community building, integration of diverse income groups); financial (mixed funding combining public subsidy with market-rate units); governance-related (multi-level coordination, participatory planning); and process-oriented (phased implementation with adaptive learning, systematic impact evaluation).

**Integration Approach:** Technologies were integrated into a comprehensive systems approach rather than isolated interventions. Building performance systems connected to smart energy management; green infrastructure integrated with stormwater and biodiversity objectives; shared facilities designed to support both resource efficiency and social cohesion; and monitoring systems connected to adaptive management processes.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:** The project involved competitive procurement of construction firms and service providers. BESIX, a major European construction company, was among the key implementing partners, coordinating complex construction involving multiple building types and sustainability requirements. Procurement emphasized not only cost but also demonstrated capacity for passive house and sustainable construction standards.

**Stakeholder Engagement:** Participatory processes were integrated throughout planning and implementation:[^1][^2]

- **Participatory Design Phase:** Community consultation during the planning phase to incorporate resident input into neighbourhood design, shared space configuration, and facility programming
- **Resident Selection and Integration:** Processes for selecting residents (particularly for subsidised and social housing) that emphasized community-building and commitment to sustainable practices
- **Ongoing Community Engagement:** Post-occupancy engagement processes, though the impact study revealed limitations in sustaining participation beyond the initial planning phase
- **Business Integration:** Engagement with sustainable enterprises selected for Greenbizz to ensure alignment with neighbourhood values and community benefit

**Participatory Elements:** The initiative incorporated participatory mechanisms including resident consultations during design; community input on shared space programming; participatory governance structures for neighbourhood management; and resident involvement in sustainability practices (e.g., vegetable garden management, waste sorting). However, the impact study revealed that participatory commitment, while strong during planning, required sustained support and engagement structures to maintain resident involvement in ongoing neighbourhood management.

**Project Management:** Implementation involved coordination across multiple scales and organizations: citydev.brussels provided overall project coordination and governance; construction firms managed building delivery; municipal and regional authorities coordinated permitting and infrastructure integration; and community organizations facilitated resident engagement. A scientific committee oversaw the impact evaluation study, ensuring methodological rigor and integration with international best practices.

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:** Specific total project costs are not detailed in available sources, though the scale (4.5 hectares, ~400 units, extensive infrastructure) suggests substantial public and private investment. The project combined public funding (citydev.brussels capital), private development investment, and potentially European sustainability funding.

**Funding Mix:** The financing model combined:

- **Public Investment:** Citydev.brussels capital funding for land acquisition, infrastructure, and subsidised housing development
- **Private Capital:** Investment from construction and development partners (e.g., BESIX) for market-rate housing and commercial space
- **Subsidised Housing Financing:** Specific funding mechanisms for the 271 subsidised and 126 social housing units
- **Potential European Funding:** Possible support from EU sustainability or urban development programs, though not explicitly detailed

**Revenue Mechanisms:** The project employed mixed revenue approaches: market-rate housing sales and rental income; subsidised housing rents at affordable levels; commercial rental income from retail and Greenbizz tenants; and potentially value capture mechanisms where public land contributions generated returns supporting ongoing operations.

**Financial Sustainability:** The mixed-income model (combining market-rate, subsidised, and social housing) was designed to cross-subsidize affordability while maintaining financial viability. Ongoing operational costs for shared facilities and common areas required sustainable management structures, with resident contributions and municipal support. The Greenbizz incubator required ongoing public support as an economic development initiative, recognizing that early-stage sustainable enterprises might not generate sufficient rental income independently.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges:** Achieving passive house standards at neighbourhood scale while maintaining cost-effectiveness required significant technical coordination and quality assurance. Integration of multiple sustainability systems (renewable energy, water management, green infrastructure) across diverse building types and shared facilities presented engineering complexity. Adapting passive house design to diverse housing types (apartments, family homes, social housing) while maintaining performance standards required careful specification and construction oversight.

**Financial Challenges:** Integrating substantial proportions of subsidised (68%) and social housing within a development project increased costs and complexity relative to market-rate development. Securing adequate funding for environmental infrastructure (green roofs, rainwater systems, shared facilities) while maintaining affordability targets required careful financial structuring. The Greenbizz incubator required ongoing public subsidy as an economic development initiative, as early-stage sustainable enterprises typically could not pay market-rate commercial rents.

**Social and Political Challenges:** The participatory planning process, while generating valuable community input, created high expectations and potential for disappointment if all community preferences could not be accommodated. Integrating economically diverse residents (market-rate, subsidised, and social housing) required intentional community-building to prevent social stratification. Concerns about gentrification and displacement of surrounding residents required careful management of neighbourhood change. Political cycles and changing municipal priorities could affect ongoing support and funding for the initiative.

**Administrative Challenges:** Coordinating across multiple governance levels (regional, municipal, citydev.brussels) required sustained institutional coordination. Permitting and regulatory compliance for innovative building systems and mixed-use development involved navigating complex administrative procedures. Integration of the neighbourhood with existing city infrastructure (utilities, transportation, services) required coordination with multiple municipal departments.

**Social Adoption Challenges:** The impact study revealed a critical challenge: design features intended to promote social interaction and resource efficiency were not being adopted consistently by residents.[^1] Specifically:

- **Shared Spaces Underutilization:** Rooftop greenhouses, collective vegetable gardens, and communal laundry rooms designed to encourage social interaction and resource sharing were being used inconsistently
- **Access Barriers:** Some shared facilities were accessed through private residential areas, creating friction and limiting use
- **Misaligned Expectations:** Initial misunderstandings about investments residents would need to make (time, effort, financial contributions) for shared facilities reduced participation
- **Participatory Sustainability Limits:** The participatory process during planning, while valuable, did not automatically translate into sustained resident commitment to ongoing community management and sustainability practices

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Design Modifications:** In response to challenges with shared space adoption, subsequent design considerations included: ensuring direct public access to shared facilities without routing through private areas; clearer initial communication about investment requirements and benefits; and more realistic design of shared spaces reflecting likely usage patterns rather than idealized community interaction.

**Management and Engagement Strategies:** The impact study highlighted the need for long-term support and engagement structures after residents moved in:[^1] ongoing community programming and facilitation; clear communication about shared facility benefits and usage; professional management of common areas; and adaptive adjustment of shared space programming based on resident feedback and actual usage patterns.

**Monitoring and Adaptive Management:** The development of the 71-indicator evaluation matrix represented a significant adaptive response, creating a systematic tool for monitoring neighbourhood performance and identifying areas requiring adjustment. This framework enables ongoing assessment of energy performance, user satisfaction, shared space adaptation, and resilience to climate variations, supporting evidence-based adaptive management.

**Lessons for Future Phases:** The phased implementation approach allowed for learning between phases. Insights from Phase 1 (2016) could inform Phase 2 (2019) design and management strategies, including adjustments to shared space configuration, resident engagement structures, and community programming.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies:** Several elements of Tivoli GreenCity's success were contextually dependent:

- **Institutional Capacity:** The project required a capable public development institution (citydev.brussels) with mandate, resources, and political support for integrated sustainable development. Cities lacking equivalent institutional capacity would need to develop such capacity or work through alternative governance models.
- **Land Availability:** The project benefited from available public or publicly controlled land in a transitional urban zone. Cities with fragmented private land ownership or constrained land availability would face different development challenges.
- **Policy Support:** The initiative operated within supportive regional policy frameworks for sustainability, affordable housing, and mixed-use development. Cities with regulatory barriers to density, mixed-income housing, or sustainable building standards would need policy reform.
- **Financial Resources:** The project required substantial public investment in land, infrastructure, and affordable housing subsidy. Resource-constrained cities would need to identify alternative financing mechanisms.
- **Technical Expertise:** Implementation required specialized expertise in passive house design, sustainable systems integration, and community engagement. Capacity development would be necessary in contexts lacking this expertise.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Comprehensive Integration:** Tivoli GreenCity's most significant strength is the coherent integration of environmental performance, social equity, and economic viability within a single neighbourhood design. Rather than treating sustainability as an isolated add-on or environmental performance as divorced from social objectives, the initiative demonstrated how these dimensions could be mutually reinforcing. The integration of 68% affordable/social housing within a high-performance sustainable neighbourhood directly challenged the false dichotomy between sustainability and affordability.

**Institutional Innovation:** The project demonstrated innovative institutional capacity through citydev.brussels' ability to coordinate across multiple governance levels, manage complex procurement and construction, and maintain commitment to ambitious sustainability and equity objectives over extended implementation timelines. This institutional innovation may prove as significant as the physical neighbourhood itself.

**Evidence-Based Learning:** The commitment to systematic impact evaluation through the Sciences Po Urban School study, combined with the development of an open-data evaluation matrix, represents best practice in urban development evaluation. Rather than treating the neighbourhood as a finished product, the initiative recognized that evaluation of lived experience was essential to understanding actual performance and informing future projects. The decision to make the evaluation matrix publicly available in open-data format, with intention for iterative improvement through shared learning, represents exemplary transparency and commitment to knowledge sharing.

**Replicable Methodology:** The 71-indicator evaluation matrix, explicitly designed for replication and adaptation, provides other cities and projects with a proven methodology for assessing sustainable neighbourhood performance. This methodological contribution may have broader impact than the physical neighbourhood itself.

**Environmental Performance:** The achievement of passive house standards and zero-energy construction for 35% of units, combined with extensive green infrastructure and resource management systems, demonstrates that substantial environmental performance improvement is technically feasible at neighbourhood scale. The confirmation through the impact study that energy efficiency performed as planned provides evidence supporting broader adoption of these technologies.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Shared Space Underutilization:** The impact study's finding that shared facilities designed to encourage social interaction and resource efficiency were underutilized represents a significant gap between design intentions and lived reality. This suggests that physical design, while important, is insufficient for achieving social objectives without accompanying social programming, community facilitation, and management structures. The rooftop greenhouses, collective vegetable gardens, and communal laundry rooms—conceptually appealing as expressions of sustainable community living—failed to achieve their intended functions for substantial portions of residents.

**Participatory Engagement Limitations:** While the participatory planning process was valuable, the impact study revealed that this initial engagement did not automatically translate into sustained resident commitment to community management and sustainability practices post-occupancy. The intensity of participatory involvement during planning created expectations for ongoing engagement that the project's management structures did not maintain. This suggests that participatory planning, while important, requires complementary post-occupancy engagement structures to sustain community commitment.

**Limited Social Cohesion Evidence:** While the initiative was designed to foster social cohesion through mixed-income housing, shared facilities, and community programming, the impact study did not provide detailed evidence regarding whether these objectives were achieved. The underutilization of shared spaces suggests that the neighbourhood may not have achieved its social cohesion objectives to the extent intended, despite strong design intentions.

**Gentrification Risks:** While the integration of 68% affordable/social housing mitigates direct gentrification within the neighbourhood, the initiative's success and amenities may have contributed to gentrification pressures in surrounding Laeken. The impact study does not provide detailed analysis of neighbourhood change in surrounding areas or displacement effects in the broader context. This represents a potential unintended consequence that requires ongoing monitoring.

**Economic Viability of Shared Facilities:** The long-term financial sustainability of shared facilities and common areas requires ongoing subsidy and professional management. The reliance on resident contributions and municipal support raises questions about long-term financial viability, particularly if resident engagement and usage decline over time.

**Limited Diversity in Sustainability Approaches:** While Tivoli GreenCity demonstrates one model of sustainable neighbourhood development, it represents a particular approach emphasizing high-performance building standards, green infrastructure, and mixed-income housing. Other sustainable neighbourhood models emphasizing different priorities (e.g., radical resource reduction, hyper-local food production, alternative governance structures) are not represented. The initiative's success should not be interpreted as indicating that this is the only or necessarily the optimal approach to sustainable urbanism.

**Scalability Questions:** While the evaluation matrix was designed for replication, the question remains whether Tivoli GreenCity's model is scalable to the level required to address metropolitan housing shortages and sustainability challenges. The neighbourhood, while significant, represents approximately 400 housing units—a meaningful but modest contribution to Brussels' housing needs. Scaling this model to hundreds or thousands of neighbourhoods would require institutional capacity, resources, and political commitment at levels not yet demonstrated.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

**Achievements in Economic Inclusion:** Tivoli GreenCity made substantial progress toward economic inclusion through the integration of 68% affordable/social housing within a high-quality, sustainable neighbourhood. This directly challenged market-driven development patterns that typically segregate affordable housing and demonstrated that economic diversity could be achieved within premium sustainable developments. The inclusion of childcare facilities (2 eco-crèches) supported access for lower-income families who otherwise might struggle with childcare costs.

**Remaining Equity Questions:** However, several equity questions remain:

- **Distributional Justice:** While the neighbourhood itself achieved economic diversity, the broader question of whether this represents meaningful progress toward metropolitan equity requires assessment of: whether the 271 subsidised units represent a meaningful proportion of Brussels' affordable housing needs; whether residents displaced from surrounding areas had access to this affordable housing; and whether development contributed to gentrification pressures elsewhere
- **Procedural Justice:** The participatory planning process engaged residents in design decisions, but the question remains whether all affected stakeholders (particularly surrounding residents and displaced populations) had meaningful voice in decision-making
- **Recognition Justice:** The initiative's emphasis on sustainability and innovation, while important, should not overshadow questions regarding whether it meaningfully advanced environmental justice for lower-income residents or whether environmental benefits were equitably distributed

**Environmental Justice Dimensions:** The neighbourhood's environmental improvements (green space, reduced emissions, improved air quality) benefited residents directly. However, the question remains whether these environmental improvements extended to surrounding areas or whether the neighbourhood represented an environmental enclave within a less-improved urban context.

#### **Future Outlook**

**Long-term Trajectory:** Tivoli GreenCity appears positioned for continued successful operation as a functioning neighbourhood. Residential occupancy, retail activity, and institutional operations (Greenbizz, eco-crèches) appear stable. However, several trajectories merit monitoring:

**Energy Performance Sustainability:** The neighbourhood's energy performance achievements should be sustained through continued maintenance of building systems and resident engagement with energy efficiency practices. Deterioration of building envelope integrity or resident abandonment of energy-conscious practices could erode performance over time.

**Social Cohesion Evolution:** The trajectory of social cohesion and community engagement remains uncertain. If participatory engagement structures and community programming are sustained and strengthened, social cohesion could improve over time. Conversely, if engagement structures are allowed to atrophy, the neighbourhood could evolve toward conventional social fragmentation despite its intentional design.

**Economic Sustainability of Shared Facilities:** The viability of shared facilities and common areas depends on sustained management investment and resident engagement. If either declines, these facilities could deteriorate or become underutilized, reducing the neighbourhood's social and resource efficiency benefits.

**Gentrification and Demographic Change:** The neighbourhood's trajectory regarding economic diversity and demographic composition requires ongoing monitoring. Market pressures could eventually erode affordability protections, or the neighbourhood's success could attract higher-income residents, potentially shifting the income composition over decades.

**Ongoing Challenges and Opportunities:**

- **Adaptive Management:** The commitment to systematic evaluation and adaptive management positions the neighbourhood to respond to emerging challenges and optimize performance over time
- **Scaling and Replication:** The opportunity exists to apply lessons from Tivoli GreenCity to other neighbourhoods in Brussels and other European cities, potentially multiplying impact
- **Climate Adaptation:** As climate impacts intensify, the neighbourhood's resilience measures (green infrastructure, water management, thermal performance) will be tested; adaptive refinement may be necessary
- **Economic Evolution:** The Greenbizz incubator and green economy focus position the neighbourhood to evolve with emerging sustainable economy opportunities, though long-term viability depends on sustained support and market development

**Evolution and Next Phases:** Potential future developments include:

- **Expansion of Evaluation Framework:** Continued application and refinement of the 71-indicator evaluation matrix, potentially expanding to other neighbourhoods and contexts
- **Scaling of Replication:** Application of Tivoli GreenCity's model and lessons to additional neighbourhoods in Brussels and other European cities
- **Deepening of Social Engagement:** Intensified investment in community programming and engagement structures to strengthen social cohesion and shared facility utilization
- **Climate Adaptation:** Proactive adaptation of neighbourhood design and management to emerging climate impacts (intensified heat, flooding, etc.)
- **Economic Development:** Evolution of Greenbizz and green economy activities as sustainable economy sectors mature and market opportunities develop

### **Conclusion**

Tivoli GreenCity represents a significant achievement in sustainable urban development, demonstrating that environmental performance, social equity, and economic viability can be integrated within a coherent neighbourhood design at meaningful scale. The integration of 68% affordable/social housing within a high-performance sustainable neighbourhood directly challenges conventional assumptions about the incompatibility of sustainability and affordability.

The initiative's most important contributions extend beyond the physical neighbourhood itself. The systematic impact evaluation methodology and open-data evaluation matrix represent exemplary practice in evidence-based urban development, with potential for broader application. The commitment to transparency, learning, and adaptive management positions Tivoli GreenCity as a laboratory not only for sustainable urbanism but also for urban development evaluation and knowledge-sharing practices.

However, the initiative's experience also reveals important complexities and limitations. The underutilization of shared spaces designed to promote social interaction and resource efficiency demonstrates that physical design, while important, is insufficient for achieving social objectives without accompanying social programming, community facilitation, and sustained engagement structures. The challenge of sustaining participatory commitment post-occupancy suggests that participatory planning, while valuable, requires complementary institutional structures to maintain community engagement over time.

The neighbourhood's long-term significance will depend not only on its continued physical and operational success but on its contribution to broader metropolitan sustainability, equity, and urban development practice. The replication of its model, refinement of its evaluation methodology, and integration of its lessons into urban development practice more broadly will determine whether Tivoli GreenCity represents an isolated exemplary project or a catalyst for systemic transformation in European urban development toward greater sustainability and equity.
