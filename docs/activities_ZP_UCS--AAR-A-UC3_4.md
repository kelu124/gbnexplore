# Executive summary## Madrid Nuevo Norte Green District (PROBONO Project Living Lab)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** Madrid Nuevo Norte Green District (as part of the PROBONO Project)
- **Alternative Names:** Madrid Nuevo Norte (MNN); Distrito Castellana Norte; MNNP; Madrid Living Lab (PROBONO context)
- **Location:** Northern Madrid, Community of Madrid, Spain (mainly the area adjacent to Chamartín train station)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 40.4820° N, 3.6865° W (central point of redevelopment)
- **Timeline:** 
  - **Planning:** 1990s–2010s (multiple iterations)
  - **Formal Approval:** 2020
  - **PROBONO Project Commencement:** 2021
  - **Current Status:** Under phased implementation (2025: pilot geothermal network, ongoing construction)
- **Project Scale:** 
  - **Area:** ~2.4 million m² (240 hectares)
  - **Population Impact:** 15,000 new homes, 350,000 m² of green space, >250,000 projected users (residents, workers, visitors)
  - **Investment:** €6 billion+ (approximate total)
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - Crea Madrid Nuevo Norte (formerly Distrito Castellana Norte, DCN)
  - Madrid City Council
  - Regional Government of Madrid
  - ACCIONA
  - IDOM (engineering and design)
  - PROBONO Project Consortium (47 partners across 13 countries, H2020 EU program)
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - Mixed: Private developers, public sector (city, regional, and national government), EU Horizon 2020 funding (PROBONO), potential PPPs

#### **Executive Summary**

Madrid Nuevo Norte (MNN) is among the most ambitious urban regeneration initiatives in Europe, aiming to transform a large, underutilized corridor in north Madrid into a **net-zero, positive energy neighborhood** that sets a new benchmark for sustainable urban development. As the Spanish Living Lab within the EU-funded PROBONO project, the initiative integrates **low-temperature geothermal district heating**, **building-integrated renewables**, and advanced digital planning tools to create a scalable, resilient, and citizen-centric urban environment[^1][^2].

The project is designed to address Madrid’s pressing urban challenges—such as housing demand, pollution, social fragmentation, and lack of green space—while positioning the city as a European leader in sustainable development. Through participatory co-design, robust stakeholder engagement, and the deployment of cutting-edge energy systems, MNN aims to deliver a district with **no visual or acoustic impact from its heating infrastructure**, significant carbon emissions reductions, and improved quality of life for residents. Key implementation challenges include integrating new systems with existing infrastructure, navigating complex regulatory processes, and ensuring social equity amid rapid urban transformation[^2].

With its pilot geothermal network now underway, MNN aspires not only to transform Madrid’s urban landscape but also to serve as an international reference for green district development, contributing directly to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the European Green Deal[^3]. Its experience offers valuable insights for other cities pursuing large-scale, technology-enabled, and socially inclusive regeneration.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Madrid is Spain’s capital and largest city, with a population exceeding 3.2 million (urban area >6 million), a dynamic service-oriented economy, and a prominent role in national and European governance. The city faces challenges related to housing affordability, air quality, and climate adaptation, while serving as a hub for innovation and international business.
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The initiative targets a corridor previously dominated by obsolete rail infrastructure and industrial land adjacent to Chamartín station in northern Madrid.
  - **Historical Patterns:** Historically isolated and underdeveloped due to railway land use, with fragmented connectivity and limited residential or public amenities.
  - **Current Land Use:** Mix of derelict industrial plots, transport corridors, and sparse urban fabric.
  - **Demographic Profile:** Surrounding areas are socioeconomically diverse, with both high-income neighborhoods and vulnerable populations. The regeneration area itself had low residential density pre-project.
  - **Infrastructure Quality:** Outdated infrastructure, limited green space, and poor accessibility—particularly for non-motorized modes.
  - **Environmental Conditions:** Urban heat island effect, air and noise pollution from transport, fragmented ecological corridors.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - Physical and social fragmentation of the northern urban fabric.
  - Underutilization of valuable land.
  - Lack of affordable, sustainable housing.
  - Insufficient green and public spaces.
  - High carbon emissions and energy inefficiency.
  - Poor connectivity for public and active transport.
- **Triggering Events:**
  - Strategic planning for Madrid’s growth.
  - Infrastructure obsolescence (rail yard downsizing).
  - Policy shifts toward climate action and sustainable urbanism.
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - Key actors included city and regional governments, private developers (DCN), local communities, environmental NGOs, and business interests, each with differing priorities (e.g., economic growth, social equity, environmental sustainability).

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** Multi-level governance involving city, regional, and national authorities, with significant private sector participation.
- **Relevant Policies:** Madrid’s urban plan, Spanish climate and energy strategies, EU Green Deal, Horizon 2020 program requirements.
- **Legal/Institutional Enablers or Barriers:**
  - Complex land ownership and regulatory approvals required prolonged negotiations.
  - European funding and policy frameworks provided incentives and technical standards.
  - Politicization and local opposition challenged certain aspects of the project[^2].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Create a **net-zero, positive energy district**.
  - Regenerate and reconnect a neglected urban corridor.
  - Deliver high-quality, affordable housing and public space.
  - Demonstrate scalable, replicable green infrastructure models.
- **Vision Statement:** To set a European and global benchmark for sustainable, inclusive, and technologically advanced urban regeneration.
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** Directly supports Madrid’s urban strategy, Spanish climate goals, the European Green Deal, and the UN SDGs[^3].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:** By integrating advanced energy systems, participatory design, and cutting-edge urban planning, the initiative aims to transform a derelict area into a thriving, sustainable, and resilient district, setting a replicable precedent for other cities[^1][^2].
- **Target Beneficiaries:** New and existing residents, local businesses, the city at large, and urban professionals seeking models of green urbanism.
- **Innovation Elements:**
  - First large-scale deployment of a **low-temperature geothermal district network** in Madrid.
  - Advanced digital tools for design, operational planning, and community feedback.
  - Integration of renewable energy at district and building scales.
  - Deep citizen co-design and participatory governance.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Urban competitiveness, climate leadership, and political mandates for sustainable growth.
- **Economic:** Unlocking land value, attracting investment, creating jobs, and supporting Madrid’s global image.
- **Social:** Enhancing livability, equity, and public health; addressing housing shortages.
- **Environmental:** Climate mitigation (GHG reduction), adaptation (urban cooling, resilience), and biodiversity.
- **Technological:** Leveraging emerging technologies for energy, data, and citizen engagement.

---

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Guided by principles of **sustainable urbanism**, positive energy districts, and the “Living Lab” model for innovation and co-creation.
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** 
  - New mixed-use neighborhoods with high-density, energy-efficient buildings.
  - Extensive green corridors, public parks, and pedestrian-prioritized streets.
  - Infrastructure for multimodal mobility (public transit, cycling, walking).
- **Scope and Phasing:** 
  - Multi-phase, starting with pilot energy infrastructure and flagship public spaces.
  - Full build-out planned over a decade or more.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Low-temperature geothermal district heating/cooling** network[^1].
  - Building-integrated photovoltaic (solar) systems.
  - Digital strategic planning and feedback platforms.
  - Smart meters and advanced building management systems.
- **Innovation Type:** 
  - Technological (energy, data systems).
  - Social (co-design, participatory governance).
  - Financial (mixed funding, innovative procurement).
  - Governance (Living Lab, multi-stakeholder partnership).
- **Integration Approach:** Technologies designed to minimize visual/acoustic impact, ensure interoperability, and integrate with existing infrastructure where possible[^1].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Major public tenders for infrastructure, with strong private sector participation; competitive selection for innovation partners.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** 
  - Extensive consultation with local communities and interest groups.
  - Digital tools for soliciting feedback and scenario modeling.
- **Participatory Elements:** 
  - Citizen co-design workshops.
  - Ongoing evaluation and adaptive planning.
- **Project Management:** Large-scale, multi-disciplinary teams; steering committees representing public, private, and civic stakeholders.

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Over €6 billion for the full regeneration; significant portion dedicated to sustainable infrastructure and public space.
- **Funding Mix:** Public (city, region, state), private developers (land value capture), EU grants (H2020), possible PPPs.
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Sale/lease of developed land, future tax revenue, utility cost savings, potential user fees for services.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Long-term viability rests on energy cost savings, increased land values, and diversified funding streams.

---

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Integrating geothermal and renewable systems with Madrid’s legacy infrastructure; deploying unproven technologies at scale[^1].
- **Financial:** Managing cost overruns, securing sufficient upfront capital, and balancing public/private interests.
- **Social/Political:** Risk of gentrification, local resistance to change, politicization among municipal and regional parties[^2].
- **Administrative:** Complex coordination across multiple agencies and partners; lengthy regulatory approval processes.
- **Environmental:** Ensuring that construction does not negatively impact local ecosystems or increase short-term pollution.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** 
  - Pilot projects to test technical solutions before full rollout.
  - Iterative planning with real-time data and stakeholder feedback.
- **Design Modifications:** Adjustments to energy system design and phasing based on technical and community input.
- **Lessons Learned:** Need for context-specific solutions, strong governance, and sustained community engagement to manage risks and maximize benefits[^2].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** 
  - Construction of geothermal district network (pilot phase complete).
  - New mixed-use developments, parks, and green corridors (ongoing).
- **Service Delivery:** 
  - Low-carbon heating and cooling for buildings.
  - Enhanced public transport and active mobility options.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** 
  - 2.4 million m² redeveloped.
  - 15,000 homes, 350,000 m² green space projected.

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Improved quality of life (cleaner air, quieter neighborhoods), access to green spaces, participatory planning opportunities[^2].
- **Equity Outcomes:** Commitment to affordable housing and inclusive design, though risk of rising property values and displacement exists[^2].
- **Displacement/Gentrification:** Potential risk acknowledged; mitigation measures under discussion.
- **Social Capital:** Increased civic participation and stakeholder engagement.
- **Cultural Impacts:** New public spaces designed to foster cultural exchange and community identity.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Thousands of construction and permanent jobs projected.
- **Economic Activity:** Significant private and public investment; increased attractiveness for business and innovation.
- **Property Values:** Anticipated increase, with careful management required to avoid exclusionary effects.
- **Cost Savings:** Lower energy costs for residents and businesses from efficient systems.
- **Economic Inclusion:** Dedicated affordable housing and SME support planned.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Projected reduction in GHG emissions through renewable energy and efficient design[^1].
- **Resource Management:** Efficient use of water, energy, and materials emphasized.
- **Environmental Quality:** Improved air quality, reduced noise, expanded biodiversity.
- **Waste/Pollution:** Modern waste management and construction standards to minimize impact.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Alignment with SDGs, European Green Deal, and local sustainability targets[^3].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Enhanced by international partnerships and innovative governance models.
- **Policy Influence:** Serving as a reference for new urban and energy policies locally and beyond.
- **Transparency/Accountability:** Digital tools and stakeholder forums promote open decision-making.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** Early indicators show progress toward stated sustainability, energy, and inclusion goals.
- **Unintended Consequences:** Risks of gentrification and community resistance noted; ongoing monitoring in place.
- **Long-term Viability:** Dependent on adaptive management and financial sustainability.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:** Large brownfield sites, strong political will, and access to advanced technology are prerequisites; funding and governance models may need adaptation elsewhere.
- **Critical Success Factors:** 
  - Strong public-private partnerships.
  - Community engagement.
  - Integrated planning and robust regulatory frameworks.
- **Scalability:** Approach is designed for replication in other European and global cities, with Living Labs as a model for innovation transfer.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:** Parallels to projects like HafenCity (Hamburg), King’s Cross (London), and Zuidas (Amsterdam), though MNN is notable for its scale and energy innovation.
- **Best Practices:** 
  - Living Lab approach for stakeholder co-creation.
  - Integration of energy, mobility, and digital systems.
  - Alignment with global sustainability standards.
- **Cautionary Lessons:** 
  - Need for careful management of social equity.
  - Risk of “one-size-fits-all” solutions; local adaptation is essential.

- **Contextual Similarities:** Addressing brownfield regeneration, climate action, and urban inclusion.
- **Methodological Parallels:** Use of digital feedback tools, participatory design, and advanced modeling.
- **Technological Overlaps:** District-scale renewables, smart energy management, digital engagement.
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Complex multi-stakeholder governance and public-private partnerships.
- **Transferable Strategies:** Living Lab methodology, citizen co-design, phased rollouts of new technology.
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Local regulatory, financial, and social contexts must inform tailoring.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Ambitious Integration:** Combines energy, land use, mobility, and social goals at unprecedented scale.
- **Technological Leadership:** First-of-its-kind geothermal district network for Madrid.
- **Participatory Governance:** Robust citizen and stakeholder co-design.
- **Global Benchmarking:** Alignment with SDGs and European Green Deal; international recognition[^3].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Complexity and Delay:** Protracted planning and regulatory processes; risk of cost/time overruns.
- **Social Equity Risks:** Potential for gentrification and exclusion if affordability not maintained[^2].
- **Context-Specific Solutions:** Some technical approaches may not transfer easily to other geographies.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Advances:** Explicit commitment to inclusion, affordable housing, and participatory planning.
- **Risks:** Rising land values may challenge social equity goals; ongoing need for monitoring and mitigation.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Trajectory:** Phased development continues through the 2020s; anticipated expansion of geothermal and smart city systems.
- **Ongoing Challenges:** Maintaining affordability, managing stakeholder expectations, adapting to evolving technologies and climate pressures.
- **Next Phases:** Scaling up Living Lab innovations; influencing broader policy and practice in Spain and the EU.
