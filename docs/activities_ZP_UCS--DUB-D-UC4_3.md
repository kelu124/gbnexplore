# Executive summary## EnergyVille Smart Building Living Lab

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** EnergyVille Smart Building Living Lab (as part of the Open Thor Living Lab)
- **Alternative Names:** Open Thor Living Lab, oPEN Lab (as the main residential demonstration), EnergyVille Living Lab
- **Location:** Genk, Thor Park, Limburg, Belgium
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approximately 50.9806° N, 5.4958° E (Thor Park, Genk)
- **Timeline:**  
  - Concept inception: ~2015  
  - Construction of EnergyVille buildings: 2016 (EnergyVille 1), 2018 (EnergyVille 2)[^1][^2][^3]  
  - Launch of Open Thor Living Lab: 2020–ongoing[^4][^5][^6]  
  - oPEN Lab project: 2021–2026[^7]
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Thor Park: ~93 hectares (entire redevelopment area)  
  - Living Lab: 6+ buildings, residential districts, business park, football stadium, 55+ EV charging points[^8]  
  - Residential demonstration: 30+ homes (initial phase)[^7]
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - EnergyVille (collaboration of KU Leuven, VITO, imec, UHasselt)[^2][^3]  
  - City of Genk  
  - Thor Park NV  
  - Partners: local housing company “Wonen in Limburg”, KRC Genk football club, SOLTECH, others[^5][^7]
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - European Union (ERDF, Horizon 2020, oPEN Lab project)[^1][^7][^2]  
  - Flemish government  
  - Province of Limburg  
  - City of Genk  
  - Private sector (industry partners, e.g., SOLTECH)[^5][^2]

#### **Executive Summary**
The EnergyVille Smart Building Living Lab, situated within the innovative Open Thor Living Lab at Thor Park, Genk, is a flagship European project demonstrating the integration of advanced energy technologies in real urban settings. The initiative leverages digital twins, AI-driven analytics, smart meters, and cloud-based management systems to optimize energy flows across mixed-use zones—residential, business, and public infrastructure. By facilitating real-time and predictive control of buildings in response to external grid signals, the Living Lab actively supports grid flexibility, peak load reduction, and increased renewable energy uptake.

This ecosystem, co-created by EnergyVille and its partners, blends research, demonstration, and commercialization, enabling companies, researchers, and residents to test and validate new solutions in situ. Key features include a comprehensive data backbone (SmarThor platform), regulatory sandboxes allowing innovative regulatory approaches, and the direct involvement of end-users and multiple stakeholders. The initiative is driven by the urgent need for climate-neutral urban transformation, with a focus on retrofitting existing building stock, optimizing energy use, and supporting the transition to Positive Energy Neighborhoods (PENs)—areas that generate more energy than they consume annually.

The Living Lab’s significance lies in its demonstration of how digital and physical innovations, combined with participatory approaches, can accelerate the energy transition in cities. It acts as a template for similar initiatives across Europe, offering tested strategies for integrating renewables, digital control, and user engagement into urban energy systems.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Genk (population ~66,000) is a medium-sized city in the Limburg province, Flanders, Belgium. Historically a coal mining center, Genk has undergone significant economic transitions since the closure of its mines in the late 20th century. Today, it positions itself as a regional hub for technology, innovation, and sustainability, leveraging its industrial heritage and strategic location near the Dutch and German borders[^1][^5].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  The Living Lab is located in Thor Park, a 93-hectare site formerly home to the Waterschei coal mine. The area is being redeveloped as a science and business campus, with adjacent social housing (Nieuw Texas), traditional residential neighborhoods (Waterschei garden district), and the KRC Genk football stadium. The district is characterized by:
  - Mixed land uses: research facilities, housing, business incubators, sports infrastructure
  - Demographic diversity: includes social housing residents (lower-income), knowledge workers, and students
  - Infrastructure: Modernized via new roads, public transport access, and state-of-the-art utility networks (heating, cooling, electricity)
  - Environmental context: Brownfield redevelopment with a focus on ecological restoration and green space[^5][^8]

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Legacy of industrial decline—economic stagnation, social challenges in former mining communities
  - Outdated housing and municipal infrastructure with high energy demand and poor efficiency
  - Need for large-scale decarbonization to meet EU and Flemish climate targets
  - Lack of mechanisms for integrating renewables and flexibility into local grids

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Closure of coal mines and need for economic regeneration
  - EU and regional climate policy (e.g., Green Deal, 2050 neutrality target)
  - Opportunity for ERDF/Horizon funding and policy innovation via regulatory sandboxes

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - City of Genk: interested in regeneration and climate leadership
  - Research institutions (KU Leuven, VITO, imec, UHasselt): seeking real-world testbeds
  - Residents: beneficiaries of improved housing and lower bills
  - Local businesses, SMEs, utility companies: innovation and market opportunities
  - Regional/national/federal governments: policy and funding support[^1][^5][^7][^2]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Multi-level: city, province, Flemish region, EU
  - Partnership model: public-private-academic collaboration

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - EU Green Deal, “Fit for 55,” Horizon 2020/Europe
  - Flemish climate and energy plans (e.g., 2030 targets)
  - Local masterplans for Thor Park and Genk’s neighborhood renewal

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Regulatory sandboxes: allow for temporary relaxation of rules to enable innovation in energy markets and smart grids[^8]
  - Complexities in integrating new business models (e.g., energy communities, peer-to-peer trading) into existing law

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Demonstrate integrated smart building and neighborhood energy management
  - Support the transition to Positive Energy Neighborhoods (PENs)
  - Accelerate the market uptake of advanced energy solutions (digital twins, AI, smart grids)
  - Reduce greenhouse gas emissions and peak energy demand

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To create a living, working, and innovation ecosystem that leads Europe in the transition to climate-neutral, energy-positive urban neighborhoods, leveraging digital and social innovation for inclusive, resilient, and sustainable cities.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Integral to Genk’s post-industrial regeneration strategy
  - Supports EU and Flemish energy and climate policy
  - Acts as a flagship for the European Positive Energy Neighborhoods movement[^5][^7]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  If advanced digital technologies and participatory renovation are deployed in a real urban environment, then energy consumption and emissions can be optimized, grid flexibility increased, and renewable integration maximized, leading to climate-neutral and economically resilient neighborhoods.

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Residents (lower energy bills, improved comfort)
  - Local businesses (new markets, innovation capacity)
  - Utilities (grid management, new services)
  - Policy-makers (tested models for replication)

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Digital twins at building and neighborhood scale
  - AI-based prediction and real-time analytics
  - Regulatory sandbox for energy innovation
  - Integration of various energy vectors (electricity, heat, mobility, storage)[^4][^5][^9][^7]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Desire for leadership in climate and innovation; leveraging EU and Flemish policy frameworks
- **Economic:** Revitalization of post-coal economy; attraction of businesses and investment[^1][^5]
- **Social:** Community uplift, improved living conditions, participatory governance
- **Environmental:** Decarbonization, resource efficiency, ecological restoration
- **Technological:** Demonstration of smart grids, IoT, digital twins, and modular renovation techniques

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Integration of living lab principles—testing, co-creation, iteration—in an urban redevelopment context. Focus on system-level energy optimization and stakeholder collaboration[^4][^5][^7].

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Repurposing of mining infrastructure for research, business, and residential use
  - Upgrading and construction of NZEBs (Nearly Zero-Energy Buildings)
  - Deployment of district heating/cooling, EV charging, green corridors

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial research facilities (2016–2018)
  - Addition of residential and mixed-use demonstration zones (2020–2026)
  - Ongoing expansion as new partners and technologies are integrated[^5][^7][^8]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Digital twin models for buildings/neighborhoods
  - AI-based energy consumption prediction and optimization
  - Smart meters and sensors (energy, indoor climate, occupancy)
  - Cloud-based management (SmarThor platform)[^10]
  - 5th-generation district heating networks
  - DC-grid integration
  - Renewable generation: PV (including BIPV), solar thermal, batteries, EV charging[^4][^5][^8]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (digital platforms, AI, hardware)
  - Social (participatory design, living lab co-creation)
  - Governance (regulatory sandbox, new business models)
  - Financial (pooling of funds, new revenue mechanisms)

- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Interoperability between legacy and new systems (focus on open protocols, modularity)
  - Cross-sectoral optimization (energy, mobility, built environment)

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Competitive selection for research and demonstration projects
  - Industry partnerships via open calls and R&D collaboration

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Community workshops, participatory design sessions
  - Inclusion of residents in demonstration projects and monitoring

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Co-design of retrofit packages and user interfaces
  - Feedback loops via monitoring and dashboards

- **Project Management:**  
  - Coordinated by EnergyVille, with task forces for research, demonstration, and community engagement
  - Iterative, agile methodology with regular evaluation points[^7]

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Multi-million euro investment (exact breakdown unavailable; oPEN Lab budget: €20+ million across three sites[^7])
  - Major investments in infrastructure, digital platforms, and research capacity

- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Substantial EU grants (ERDF, Horizon)
  - Regional (Flemish) and local government co-financing
  - Private sector (industry R&D, demonstration funding)[^1][^5][^2]

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Service contracts with industry partners
  - Potential user fees for new services (e.g., energy management, EV charging)
  - Intellectual property (IP) licensing and spin-offs

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Long-term vision includes commercialization of technologies and business models validated in the lab

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Achieving interoperability between diverse legacy and new building systems[^4]
  - Ensuring cybersecurity for IoT and cloud-based systems[^9]
  - Managing complexity of data integration and analytics

- **Financial:**  
  - High upfront costs for infrastructure and retrofits
  - Need for sustained funding for long-term demonstration

- **Social and Political:**  
  - Gaining acceptance from residents for new technologies and behavior change
  - Aligning diverse stakeholder interests

- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination across multiple organizations and regulatory bodies
  - Navigating regulatory uncertainty (addressed via sandbox)

- **Environmental:**  
  - Managing construction impacts on brownfield site
  - Ensuring that new systems deliver actual resource and emissions savings

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Use of regulatory sandbox to circumvent outdated regulations and enable innovation
  - Iterative refinement of digital and physical systems based on real-world feedback

- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Modular, phased implementation to allow learning and adaptation
  - Enhanced focus on cybersecurity and data governance[^9]

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Need for deep engagement with end-users to ensure adoption and optimize performance
  - Importance of transparent data sharing and open innovation

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Deep integration of digital and physical infrastructure
- Robust partnership spanning research, government, business, and community
- Demonstrates replicable models for energy-positive neighborhoods
- Strong emphasis on monitoring, evaluation, and learning
- Alignment with EU and regional climate policy and funding priorities

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- High complexity and coordination challenges
- Upfront investment requirements may be prohibitive in less resourced contexts
- Risk of technology lock-in and dependence on proprietary systems
- Ongoing challenge to ensure true inclusivity and accessibility for marginalized groups

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Focus on social housing and inclusive participation is a strength
- Ongoing need to monitor for unintended exclusion or gentrification effects
- Potential to advance environmental justice by addressing legacy pollution and energy poverty

#### **Future Outlook**

- The Living Lab is positioned to expand as new technologies and partners join
- Continuous evolution toward city- and region-wide replication
- Ongoing challenges include ensuring financial sustainability, deepening community engagement, and staying ahead of technological/cybersecurity risks
- Planned next phases: scaling up heat networks, expanding Positive Energy Neighborhoods, enhancing digital twin capabilities, and exporting best practices across Europe[^5][^7][^8]
