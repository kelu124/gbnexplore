# Executive summary## Sharing Cities – Smart District Demonstration (Milan)

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Sharing Cities – Smart District Demonstration
- **Alternative Names:** Sharing Cities Milan, H2020 Sharing Cities Lighthouse Project
- **Location:** Porta Romana/Vettabbia, Milan, Lombardy, Italy
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 45.4476° N, 9.2160° E (Porta Romana/Vettabbia)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Launch: 2016 (as part of the EU Horizon 2020 “Sharing Cities” program)
  - Key Milestones: Retrofit of buildings (2017–2019), deployment of IoT and digital twin (2018–2020), operational demonstration (2020–2022)
  - Status: Ongoing, with continued platform expansion and upscaling planned
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Area: ~14 km² demonstration district
  - Target: Over 24,000 m² of private and 5,000 m² of public buildings retrofitted; impacts on >5,000 residents; investment exceeding €20 million
- **Leading Organization(s):** City of Milan (Municipality), with support from local utilities, tech partners (e.g., Siemens, Fastweb), EU project consortium
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - EU Horizon 2020 (primary)
  - Municipality of Milan
  - Private sector co-financing (PPP model)

#### **Executive Summary**
**Sharing Cities** is an EU Horizon 2020 “lighthouse” project, demonstrating how digital platforms and integrated energy, mobility, and data infrastructures can drive the transformation of urban districts toward sustainability, resilience, and equity. In Milan, the focus is on the **Porta Romana/Vettabbia** district—a historically fragmented area characterized by brownfields, inefficient buildings, and social disparities. Here, the initiative deployed a **digital twin platform** that integrates real-time data from energy production (solar PV), building consumption, EV charging, and urban infrastructure. IoT sensors and management systems support scenario-based analytics for material selection, retrofit options, and operational optimization, while accessible visualization and reporting tools foster user engagement and transparency.

The project’s ambitions extend beyond technical upgrades: it engages citizens in the co-design of services, encourages behavioral change through digital incentive platforms, and aims for deep reductions in energy use and carbon emissions. By achieving up to 30% energy savings, improving air quality, and piloting circular economy strategies, Sharing Cities Milan serves as a replicable model for smart, inclusive, and low-carbon neighborhoods across Europe. Its challenges—especially regarding data privacy, interoperability, and cross-sectoral governance—offer lessons for future urban innovation[^1][^2][^3][^4].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Milan is Italy’s second-largest city (population ~1.4 million, metro area >3 million), an economic powerhouse with strong finance, design, and innovation sectors. It occupies a key position in both national and European urban hierarchies, serving as a logistics and cultural hub for the Po Valley region. Its rapid postwar growth left a legacy of industrial zones and energy-inefficient housing[^1][^5].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  *Porta Romana/Vettabbia* lies southeast of Milan’s historic center, spanning 14 km². Historically a mix of railway yards, brownfields, and postwar social housing, it is marked by:
  - 70% of buildings classified as G or F energy class (the lowest efficiency)
  - Abandoned industrial spaces and fragmented land use
  - Socio-economic divides, with pockets of low-income and aging populations
  - Poor mobility linkages, limited green space, and environmental degradation[^2][^6][^3]
  - Ongoing redevelopment aims to “stitch together” previously separated areas, connecting the city center to its agricultural periphery via new parks, mixed housing, and multimodal transport nodes[^1].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High building energy consumption and poor thermal comfort
  - Air pollution and urban heat island effects
  - Mobility inefficiencies and disjointed public transport
  - Social fragmentation and limited civic engagement[^2][^6]
  - Underused or derelict land limiting economic and social vitality

- **Triggering Events:**  
  The confluence of Milan’s post-Expo 2015 urban agenda, EU climate commitments, and the opportunity for Horizon 2020 funding provided the impetus for large-scale smart district action.

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Municipality (urban planning, mobility, energy departments)
  - Social housing managers and private landlords
  - Residents’ associations and “Social Street” groups
  - Technology and utility partners (Siemens, Fastweb, etc.)
  - NGOs (e.g., Legambiente), local businesses, academic partners[^7]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  Smart City Unit within the Municipality coordinates cross-departmental action, with oversight from the city council and alignment with regional and national urban innovation strategies[^2][^8].

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Milan’s Sustainable Urban Mobility Plan (PUMS)
  - Local climate and energy action plans (aligned with Covenant of Mayors)
  - National energy efficiency and circular economy directives

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - GDPR compliance for data sharing
  - Procurement rules for EU-funded projects
  - Institutional inertia and fragmented asset ownership as occasional barriers[^8]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Achieve deep retrofits and energy savings in existing buildings
  - Enable holistic, real-time management of energy and mobility flows
  - Foster citizen engagement and co-design of solutions
  - Demonstrate scalable, replicable smart city models[^1][^3][^4]

- **Vision Statement:**  
  “A nearly-zero-emission, citizen-centered district—connecting people, data, and infrastructure to create a healthier, more inclusive, and sustainable urban future”[^2][^3].

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Consistent with Milan’s citywide smart city agenda, regional decarbonization targets, and EU-wide goals for climate neutrality and digital transformation.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  Deploying integrated digital, energy, and mobility solutions—combined with participatory planning—will reduce emissions, improve efficiency, enhance quality of life, and empower communities[^4].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Residents (improved comfort, lower bills)
  - Local businesses (new opportunities)
  - Vulnerable groups (better access to services)
  - City as a whole (reduced pollution, improved public space)

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Digital twin for scenario-based planning and management
  - IoT-enabled retrofits and real-time environmental monitoring
  - Data-driven incentive platforms for positive behaviors[^1][^2][^4]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  Mayoral leadership, EU funding opportunities

- **Economic:**  
  Attracting investment, supporting green jobs, leveraging brownfield redevelopment

- **Social:**  
  Bridging divides, fostering local pride and participation

- **Environmental:**  
  Meeting climate targets, improving urban resilience

- **Technological:**  
  Harnessing advances in IoT, digital twins, and data analytics

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Principles of *citizen-centric smart city development*, emphasizing co-design, open data, and integration of physical and digital systems[^1][^8][^3].

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Retrofit of ~29,000 m² of residential buildings (insulation, efficient systems)
  - Creation of new green spaces and parkland (187,226 m²)
  - Installation of EV charging, smart lamp posts, and parking sensors
  - Improved multimodal transport around a new mobility hub[^1][^2][^3]

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial focus on building retrofits and data infrastructure (2016–2019)
  - Subsequent deployment of e-mobility, smart parking, and digital engagement tools (2018–2021)
  - Ongoing scaling and integration (2021–present)

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Digital twin platform:** For real-time energy mapping, scenario analysis, and visualization
  - **IoT sensors:** Energy, environmental (air quality, noise), mobility (parking, EV usage)
  - **Building Management Systems (BMS):** Integration with retrofitted buildings
  - **Sustainable Energy Management Systems (SEMS):** Monitoring and control of energy consumption[^4]
  - **EV charging and e-mobility sharing platforms**
  - **Citizen engagement apps:** e.g., greenApes for incentivizing positive behaviors[^1][^2][^4]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  Primarily technological (digital twin, IoT integration), with significant social (co-design), governance (multi-actor coordination), and process (open data, interoperability) innovations.

- **Integration Approach:**  
  Urban Sharing Platform (USP) provides open APIs and interoperability for layering multiple smart city solutions, ensuring legacy systems and new deployments are connected[^1][^8].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  Competitive EU tenders for technology and retrofit providers, with strong criteria for interoperability and innovation.

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Structured co-design workshops with residents
  - Partnerships with local businesses, NGOs, and “social streets”
  - Feedback loops via digital engagement platforms[^1][^2][^7]

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  Citizen input on service design, building retrofits, and digital tools.

- **Project Management:**  
  Led by the Smart City Unit, with cross-departmental coordination and EU project governance structures[^2][^8].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  Total investment >€20 million for Milan site; ~€30 million across three cities[^1][^3]

- **Funding Mix:**  
  65% EU Horizon 2020, 25% municipal, 10% private sector

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  EV charging fees, service contracts, energy savings

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  Focus on long-term cost savings from efficiency, with business models for data and mobility services under development[^8]

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Achieving interoperability among legacy and new systems
  - Ensuring data quality and integration from diverse sensors
  - Retrofit complexity for old, heterogeneous buildings[^8][^4]

- **Financial:**  
  - Initial capital intensity of retrofits and sensor deployment
  - Need for sustainable financing models post-EU funding

- **Social and Political:**  
  - Building trust in data sharing and digital tools
  - Avoiding social exclusion or “digital divides”[^8]

- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination across city departments and partners
  - Compliance with GDPR and privacy regulations

- **Environmental:**  
  - Managing construction impacts during retrofits
  - Ensuring green space expansion aligns with biodiversity and climate goals

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Open API platform to facilitate interoperability
  - Ongoing citizen engagement to build digital literacy and trust
  - Iterative deployment with feedback-driven adjustments[^1][^8][^4]

- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Gradual scaling of technologies to address unforeseen integration issues
  - Additional investment in community outreach

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Importance of governance and cross-sectoral leadership
  - Data governance (quality, security, privacy) must be prioritized early[^8][^9]

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**
  - Retrofitted 29,000 m² of residential buildings and 187,226 m² of new parkland[^2]
  - 60+ EV charging points, 62 e-cars, 150 e-bikes, 175 smart parking sensors
  - 300+ smart lamp posts with environmental monitoring

- **Service Delivery:**
  - Real-time urban data dashboards for stakeholders
  - New shared mobility and car sharing options
  - Digital apps for citizen engagement and reporting[^1][^2][^4]

- **Quantitative Metrics:**
  - Up to 30% reduction in energy consumption in retrofitted buildings
  - 70% reduction in energy needs for upgraded social housing[^1][^2]
  - Improved air quality indicators post-implementation

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Enhanced comfort and thermal quality in dwellings
  - New public spaces and improved mobility options
  - Strengthened civic engagement and sense of ownership

- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - Targeted retrofits in social housing
  - Initiatives to ensure digital inclusion (training, accessible platforms)

- **Displacement and Gentrification:**  
  - No major reports of displacement, but ongoing monitoring is needed

- **Social Capital:**  
  - Increased collaboration between residents, businesses, and city[^1][^7]

- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Revitalization of underused areas, support for local cultural activities

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  - Construction and retrofit jobs
  - Green economy employment in mobility and tech sectors

- **Economic Activity:**  
  - New business opportunities in e-mobility and data services

- **Property Values:**  
  - Early signs of appreciation in retrofitted areas

- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Lower utility bills for residents
  - Efficiency gains for municipal services

- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - Focus on affordable social housing and shared mobility access

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - Up to 30% energy savings; reduced carbon footprint[^1][^2][^4]
  - Contributions to Milan’s climate neutrality goals

- **Resource Management:**  
  - Improved efficiency in energy, water, and material use
  - Circular economy pilots for materials selection

- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - Improved air quality and noise reduction via green infrastructure and smart mobility

- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - Enhanced waste management; reduction in emissions from transport

- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - Performance monitored against EU and local standards; ongoing certification efforts

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Strengthened Smart City Unit, new digital governance protocols

- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Milan’s approach cited as a model in EU smart city policy

- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - Open data platforms and stakeholder dashboards improve accountability[^8][^9]

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  - Most energy, retrofit, and digital engagement targets met or exceeded

- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Complexity of data integration required more time and resources than anticipated

- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Ongoing efforts to sustain and scale platform beyond EU project funding

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:**  
  - Success rooted in Milan’s governance and strong technical partnerships; retrofit and data approaches transferable with adaptation to local contexts[^1][^4]

- **Critical Success Factors:**  
  - Strong municipal leadership
  - Cross-sector partnerships
  - Open data and interoperability standards

- **Scalability:**  
  - Platform designed to be replicable; components (e.g., digital twin, SEMS, citizen engagement) already adopted in other EU cities (London, Lisbon)[^4]

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:**  
  - Other Sharing Cities sites (London, Lisbon)
  - Rotterdam’s climate resilience digital twin
  - Helsinki and Rennes’ digital twin data platforms[^10][^8]

- **Best Practices:**  
  - Co-design with citizens
  - Integration of energy, mobility, and data systems
  - Focus on open, interoperable platforms

- **Cautionary Lessons:**  
  - Need for early investment in data governance
  - Ensure digital and social equity in access and benefits

#### **Parallels to Specific Use Cases**
- **Contextual Similarities:**  
  - Urban districts with fragmented infrastructure, social divides, and sustainability challenges

- **Methodological Parallels:**  
  - Digital twin deployment, scenario analytics, participatory planning

- **Technological Overlaps:**  
  - IoT, BMS, SEMS, open data platforms

- **Stakeholder Dynamics:**  
  - Multi-actor partnerships, resident engagement

- **Transferable Strategies:**  
  - Co-design, digital incentives, open APIs

- **Adaptation Considerations:**  
  - Tailoring to local regulations, data privacy laws, and community needs

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- **Holistic integration** of digital, energy, and mobility systems
- **Citizen-centric approach** with real co-design and engagement
- **Ambitious energy and climate impacts**
- **Open, replicable platform** supporting broader smart city transition[^1][^4]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- **Data privacy and interoperability** remain ongoing technical and governance challenges[^8]
- Initial focus on technical solutions required later rebalancing toward social aspects
- Long-term financial sustainability beyond project funding must be strengthened

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- Proactive efforts to reach vulnerable populations (social housing, training)
- Ongoing need to monitor digital divides and ensure inclusive benefits

#### **Future Outlook**
- **Scaling up:** Integration across Milan and other cities
- **Ecosystem development:** Toward a citywide digital twin and data ecosystem
- **Sustainability:** Focus on business models and enduring governance
- **Continuous improvement:** Iterative learning, open data, and civic participation to address emerging urban challenges[^8][^9][^4]
