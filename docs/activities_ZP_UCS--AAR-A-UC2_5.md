# Executive summary

## CO-SHAPE: Co-Shaping Areas in Peri-Urban Environments

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** CO-SHAPE – Co-Shaping Areas in Peri-Urban Environments
- **Alternative Names:** CO-SHAPE Pilot City Activity; Aarhus Energy Park Spørring Project
- **Location:** Spørring, Aarhus, Denmark (peri-urban area on the northern edge of Aarhus's rural hinterland)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approximately 56.1°N, 10.2°E (Aarhus region)
- **Timeline:** April 2024 – March 2027 (postdoc research project); pilot activities ongoing; two-year demonstration phase within broader timeline
- **Project Scale:** 1,000 hectares of peri-urban land; population affected estimated at 80% of Aarhus (349,873 inhabitants); municipality-wide institutional change
- **Leading Organization(s):** Aarhus Municipality (City of Aarhus/AAK), Aarhus School of Architecture, NetZeroCities Pilot Cities Programme (Horizon Europe), private developers, energy operators, civic associations
- **Funding Sources:** Horizon Europe funding (NetZeroCities); municipal budgets; private sector investment in renewable energy infrastructure; research institution support

#### **Executive Summary**

CO-SHAPE represents a paradigmatic shift in how European cities approach climate neutrality through integrated peri-urban development. Situated in Spørring, a village on the northern periphery of Aarhus, Denmark's second-largest city, the initiative transforms a 1,000-hectare area into a biogenic energy park while simultaneously pioneering innovative governance and participatory planning models.[^1][^2] Rather than treating renewable energy infrastructure as a purely technical challenge imposed from above, CO-SHAPE centers on co-creation—a deliberate process of bringing citizens, landowners, farmers, energy developers, and municipal departments together to collectively shape the future of the area they inhabit.

The project integrates multiple renewable energy and circular economy technologies: biogas production from agricultural waste, grass protein production, biochar facilities for soil carbon sequestration, solar power, energy storage systems, and Power-to-X technologies that combine captured CO₂ with green hydrogen to produce sustainable aviation and shipping fuels.[^2] Collectively, these installations are projected to reduce annual CO₂ emissions by over 100,000 tonnes—approximately 10% of Aarhus's total emissions.[^2] Yet the initiative's most significant innovation lies not in its technological components but in its governance architecture. By establishing "solution rooms" where diverse stakeholders negotiate trade-offs from project inception rather than through traditional post-hoc public consultation, CO-SHAPE demonstrates how participatory processes can overcome the "Not in My Backyard" (NIMBY) sentiments that typically plague infrastructure development. The project directly supports Aarhus's commitment to achieve climate neutrality by 2030 and serves as a replicable model for similar peri-urban typologies across Europe.[^1] Early outcomes include an 85% satisfaction rate among participating stakeholders, a 40% increase in public trust toward municipal decision-making, and a 50% reduction in bureaucratic delays.[^1]

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile**

Aarhus, Denmark's second-largest city with approximately 349,873 inhabitants, occupies a distinctive position within Northern Europe's urban hierarchy.[^1] The city benefits from a vibrant knowledge economy anchored by a major university and student population, combined with a rich cultural heritage featuring ancient architecture interwoven with forests, agricultural land, and water systems.[^2] Aarhus's geographic integration with natural surroundings—the city's proximity to beaches, forests, and rural hinterlands—contributes to an exceptionally high quality of life and is central to residents' identity and well-being.[^2] The city has positioned itself as a climate leader, embedding sustainability into municipal governance and strategic planning. Economically, Aarhus combines traditional sectors (agriculture in surrounding areas) with knowledge-intensive industries, creative enterprises, and emerging green technology sectors.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics**

Spørring represents a classic peri-urban typology: a village positioned at the transitional zone between dense urban areas and agricultural hinterlands.[^2] Historically, such areas have served as buffer zones, combining dispersed residential settlement patterns with active farming, natural ecosystems, and increasingly, infrastructure facilities. The landscape is characterized by agricultural land, forest patches, and scattered residential properties. Demographically, peri-urban residents often include both long-established farming families and newer residents attracted by proximity to urban amenities combined with rural character. Land use is traditionally mixed, with agricultural production as the dominant economic activity, though pressure for alternative uses—residential development, industrial facilities, renewable energy infrastructure—has intensified.

Environmentally, peri-urban areas occupy critical positions in regional water cycles, biodiversity corridors, and carbon sequestration. However, these areas often experience competing pressures: agricultural productivity requirements, conservation concerns, residential amenity protection, and infrastructure development needs. Spørring specifically faced the challenge of accommodating renewable energy development while maintaining community acceptance and environmental quality.

**Pre-Initiative Conditions**

Aarhus Municipality faced several interconnected challenges that prompted the CO-SHAPE initiative:

- **Insufficient governance tools for comprehensive planning:** Traditional municipal structures organized energy, planning, agriculture, and environmental management into separate departments with limited cross-sectional coordination, making holistic peri-urban development difficult.[^1]
- **Community resistance to energy infrastructure:** Residents' reluctance to live near or accept energy production facilities—the NIMBY phenomenon—represented a critical barrier to necessary renewable energy expansion.[^1]
- **Lack of cross-departmental competencies:** Municipal staff lacked experience and institutional frameworks for collaborative planning across traditional silos, limiting capacity for integrated land-use solutions.[^1]
- **Competing land-use pressures:** The peri-urban area faced tension between agricultural preservation, residential development, biodiversity conservation, and infrastructure needs.[^2]
- **Governance legitimacy gaps:** Traditional top-down planning processes generated public skepticism about decision-making transparency and stakeholder inclusion.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework**

Aarhus Municipality operates within Denmark's decentralized governance system, where municipalities hold significant autonomy in urban planning, environmental management, and climate action. The city council and municipal administration provide the formal governance structure, while elected officials and appointed administrators jointly shape policy direction.

**Relevant Policies**

Several policy frameworks shaped CO-SHAPE's development:

- **Climate Action Plan 2021-2024 (CAP21-24):** Established Aarhus's commitment to climate neutrality by 2030, providing the overarching strategic context for renewable energy and land-use initiatives.[^1]
- **Strategic Shift (2023):** In response to climate urgency, Aarhus shifted toward "more radical interventions" for achieving 2030 targets, creating political space for experimental governance approaches like CO-SHAPE.[^1]
- **"A Greener Aarhus" Policy:** Addresses biodiversity, climate adaptation, and green infrastructure integration, emphasizing co-creation with citizens and multiple stakeholders.[^3] This policy framework reflects broader commitment to inclusive, multifunctional land use.
- **Municipal Master Plan:** Provides spatial planning framework; CO-SHAPE principles are being translated into concrete guidelines through theme plans including "A Greener Aarhus with More Blue" and connected natural habitat strategies.[^3]
- **Renewable Energy Regulations:** Danish and municipal frameworks governing renewable energy development, grid integration, and environmental assessment.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers**

Enablers include Denmark's progressive climate legislation, municipal autonomy in planning, and established legal frameworks for environmental assessment and public participation. The Horizon Europe NetZeroCities program provided funding and institutional legitimacy for experimental governance approaches. However, barriers included traditional planning law emphasizing sequential rather than participatory processes, property rights complexities involving multiple landowners, grid connection requirements, and agricultural regulations protecting farmland.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals**

CO-SHAPE pursued multiple interconnected objectives:

- **Establish integrated renewable energy infrastructure:** Transform Spørring into a functional biogenic energy park combining biogas, biochar, solar, energy storage, and Power-to-X technologies operating as an interdependent circular system.[^1][^2]
- **Pioneer comprehensive energy planning as governance innovation:** Develop planning methodologies that integrate energy production with landscape architecture, agriculture, recreation, and environmental conservation through holistic spatial planning.[^1]
- **Overcome NIMBY through participatory governance:** Demonstrate that co-creation processes involving citizens, developers, and authorities from project inception could generate genuine buy-in and reduce opposition to infrastructure development.[^2]
- **Break down municipal silos:** Establish cross-departmental collaboration mechanisms enabling coordinated action on complex, multifaceted urban challenges.[^1]
- **Achieve quantified emissions reductions:** Reduce CO₂ emissions by over 100,000 tonnes annually (approximately 10% of Aarhus's total), directly supporting the 2030 climate neutrality target.[^2]

**Vision Statement**

The initiative envisions peri-urban areas transformed from zones of competing interests into spaces of integrated sustainability where renewable energy production, agricultural innovation, biodiversity conservation, and community well-being advance simultaneously through deliberate co-creation.[^2] Rather than infrastructure "happening to people," the vision positions residents and stakeholders as active shapers of their environment's transformation.[^2]

**Alignment with Broader Plans**

CO-SHAPE directly supports Aarhus's climate neutrality commitment and has been embedded within the municipality's updated climate action plan.[^2] The principles of co-creation have been made mandatory requirements for new renewable energy projects, institutionalizing pilot learnings into standard municipal practice.[^2] The initiative aligns with European climate objectives under the Horizon Europe framework and contributes to EU climate-neutral and smart cities mission targets.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change**

The initiative operates on a sophisticated theory of change recognizing that technical solutions alone cannot overcome systemic barriers to climate transition:

1. **Problem Recognition:** Climate neutrality requires massive renewable energy expansion, but traditional top-down infrastructure development generates community opposition, bureaucratic delays, and suboptimal spatial integration.

2. **Governance Innovation:** By creating structured spaces (solution rooms) where diverse stakeholders with competing interests negotiate from project inception, trust emerges, shared understanding develops, and solutions reflect collective rather than imposed preferences.

3. **Institutional Change:** Successful co-creation in Spørring generates institutional learning that reshapes municipal culture, embedding participatory approaches into standard practice beyond the pilot area.

4. **Systemic Transformation:** As participatory governance becomes institutionalized, the municipality's capacity for complex, integrated development increases, enabling broader climate transition and quality-of-life improvements.

5. **Replication:** Documented methodologies and demonstrated success create models transferable to similar peri-urban contexts across Europe, accelerating climate transition at scale.

**Target Beneficiaries**

Primary beneficiaries include residents of Spørring and surrounding areas (improved environmental quality, economic opportunities, genuine voice in decisions affecting their lives), Aarhus Municipality (achievement of climate targets, enhanced governance capacity, replicable models), energy operators (market opportunities, social license to operate), farmers (new income streams from biogas feedstock and grass protein production), and broader European cities (transferable governance innovations and planning methodologies).

**Innovation Elements**

CO-SHAPE's distinctiveness lies in its integration of governance innovation with technical innovation. Rather than simply deploying renewable technologies, the project systematically experiments with participatory planning processes, cross-departmental coordination mechanisms, and stakeholder engagement methodologies designed to overcome known barriers to sustainable infrastructure development. The "solution room" concept—informal spaces for negotiating complex trade-offs—represents genuine innovation in municipal governance practice.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers**

Denmark's strong climate commitments, Aarhus's mayoral leadership prioritizing sustainability, and the 2023 municipal strategic shift toward "radical interventions" for 2030 targets created political momentum. EU Horizon Europe funding and NetZeroCities program participation provided external validation and resources for experimental approaches. Local political support for co-creation has been formalized through requirements that new renewable energy projects incorporate participatory processes.[^2]

**Economic Drivers**

The project addresses market opportunities in renewable energy, circular economy technologies, and emerging green industries. Biogas production from agricultural waste creates new revenue streams for farmers; biochar and grass protein production represent novel economic activities; Power-to-X technologies position Aarhus as a green technology innovator. Long-term energy cost savings through efficiency and local production motivate municipal investment. The circular economy model reduces resource costs while creating employment in technology installation, operation, and maintenance.

**Social Drivers**

Community desire for genuine participation in decisions affecting their environment, concerns about quality of life near industrial facilities, and social cohesion benefits from inclusive decision-making processes motivated the co-creation approach. The initiative responds to documented public skepticism about traditional planning processes and addresses equity concerns about burden-sharing in climate transition.

**Environmental Drivers**

Climate urgency—the need to reduce emissions by 10% through this single initiative—provides overriding environmental motivation. Additionally, the project addresses soil health through biochar application, supports biodiversity through integrated land management, and improves water cycle management through multifunctional land use. Carbon sequestration through biochar production and reduced emissions from waste-to-energy conversion represent direct environmental benefits.

**Technological Drivers**

Maturation of biogas technology, advances in Power-to-X systems, declining solar costs, and proven energy storage solutions made integrated renewable energy parks technically and economically feasible. Digital platforms for stakeholder engagement and data management enabled participatory governance at scale previously impractical.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework**

CO-SHAPE draws on multiple planning philosophies:

- **Differentiated Urban Typology Approach:** Recognizing that peri-urban areas require distinct strategies from dense urban centers or rural zones, the project tailors solutions to specific geographic and social characteristics.[^1]
- **Circular Economy Principles:** The biogenic energy park exemplifies circular systems where outputs from one process become inputs for another—agricultural waste becomes biogas feedstock; biogas byproducts become biochar; captured CO₂ becomes fuel precursor.
- **Landscape Architectural Integration:** Rather than treating energy infrastructure as isolated technical facilities, the design integrates landscape architecture, recreation opportunities, and aesthetic considerations into comprehensive spatial planning.[^1]
- **Participatory Governance:** Building on co-creation and deliberative democracy traditions, the approach positions stakeholders as co-designers rather than passive recipients of decisions.
- **Reflexive Governance:** The initiative incorporates mechanisms for continuous learning and adaptation, treating the pilot as an experiment generating insights for broader institutional evolution.[^1]

**Physical/Spatial Design**

The Energy Park Spørring integrates multiple components across the 1,000-hectare area:

- **Biogas Facility:** Converts agricultural waste (manure, crop residues) into biogas for energy production and digestate for soil amendment
- **Biochar Production:** Pyrolysis plant converts organic residues into biochar, which improves soil carbon storage and agricultural productivity
- **Grass Protein Production:** Novel agricultural activity producing animal feed and potentially human food from grass, diversifying land use
- **Solar Installation:** Photovoltaic arrays distributed across suitable areas
- **Energy Storage Systems:** Battery or thermal storage enabling grid balancing and demand management
- **Power-to-X Infrastructure:** Systems combining captured CO₂ from biogas with green hydrogen to produce sustainable e-fuels for aviation and shipping
- **Recreational and Green Space:** Integrated throughout to support community well-being and biodiversity

The design avoids concentrating facilities in ways that would maximize NIMBY opposition; instead, distributed infrastructure combined with landscape integration aims to minimize visual and environmental impacts while creating multifunctional spaces serving energy production, agriculture, recreation, and conservation simultaneously.

**Scope and Phasing**

The project operates on multiple timescales:

- **Research Foundation (2024-2027):** Postdoc research at Aarhus School of Architecture examining sustainability transitions, providing theoretical grounding and continuous learning
- **Pilot Demonstration (two-year focus within broader timeline):** Intensive co-creation and governance innovation testing
- **Institutional Integration:** Embedding learnings into municipal practice and policy
- **Replication and Scaling:** Transferring methodologies to other municipalities and European contexts

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed**

The initiative combines established and emerging technologies:

- **Biogas Technology:** Proven anaerobic digestion converting organic waste to methane; mature technology with established supply chains
- **Pyrolysis/Biochar:** Thermal conversion of biomass into stable carbon; increasingly commercialized but still relatively novel at scale
- **Grass Protein Extraction:** Emerging agricultural technology requiring development and optimization
- **Solar Photovoltaics:** Mature, cost-effective renewable technology
- **Battery Energy Storage:** Established technology for grid services and demand management
- **Power-to-X (PtX):** Advanced technology combining CO₂ capture with hydrogen electrolysis to produce synthetic fuels; cutting-edge but increasingly demonstrated at pilot scale
- **Digital Engagement Platforms:** Technologies enabling remote participation, real-time information sharing, and collaborative decision-making
- **GIS and Spatial Modeling:** Tools for landscape scenario-building and comprehensive planning visualization

**Innovation Type**

While individual technologies are not uniformly novel, their integration represents technological innovation—creating interdependent systems where circular principles optimize overall performance. More significantly, the initiative emphasizes governance and process innovation: the "solution room" methodology, cross-departmental coordination mechanisms, and participatory scenario-building represent social and institutional innovations potentially more transformative than any single technology.

**Integration Approach**

Technologies are integrated as an interdependent system rather than independent installations. Biogas production generates feedstock for biochar production; biochar improves soils for grass protein production; agricultural residues feed back into biogas; captured CO₂ from biogas feeds Power-to-X systems; excess heat from processes is recovered; renewable electricity from solar and biogas powers electrolysis for hydrogen production. This systems integration maximizes resource efficiency and circular economy benefits. Digital platforms integrate stakeholder engagement with technical planning, ensuring social and technical dimensions advance together.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting**

The project involves multiple procurement pathways:

- **Municipal Procurement:** City contracts for planning services, co-creation facilitation, and technical studies
- **Private Sector Engagement:** Energy operators, technology providers, and developers selected through competitive processes or negotiated partnerships
- **Research Institution Partnerships:** Aarhus School of Architecture engaged through research contracts providing intellectual foundation and continuous learning capacity
- **Community Engagement:** Citizens and stakeholders participate through voluntary involvement in solution rooms and co-creation workshops rather than traditional contractual relationships

**Stakeholder Engagement**

CO-SHAPE's most distinctive feature is systematic, early-stage stakeholder engagement:

- **Solution Rooms:** Structured spaces bringing together city staff, citizens, landowners, and developers to collectively work through project complexities, negotiate trade-offs, and develop shared solutions.[^2] These represent departure from traditional sequential planning where developers propose, cities review, and public consultation occurs as afterthought.
- **Internal Municipal Solution Rooms:** Cross-departmental forums enabling energy, planning, agriculture, and environmental staff to collaborate on typically siloed issues.[^2]
- **Co-creation Workshops:** Intensive sessions engaging diverse stakeholders in landscape architectural scenario-building, energy planning, and comprehensive spatial design.[^1]
- **Continuous Dialogue:** Ongoing communication maintaining stakeholder engagement beyond initial planning phases

**Participatory Elements**

The initiative emphasizes genuine co-creation rather than tokenistic consultation:

- **Early Involvement:** Stakeholders participate from project conception, not after decisions are made
- **Meaningful Power:** Participants genuinely shape outcomes through negotiation and collective decision-making rather than providing advisory input
- **Transparency:** Open sharing of information, constraints, and trade-offs enables informed participation
- **Iterative Process:** Multiple engagement cycles allow refinement based on feedback
- **Accessibility:** Engagement approaches accommodate diverse participation styles and capacities

**Project Management**

Leadership from Martin Kaae Riis (CO-SHAPE project lead at Aarhus Municipality) emphasizes collaborative governance and adaptive management. Project coordination integrates technical planning with participatory processes, ensuring neither dominates. Regular reflection and learning loops enable course correction. The project operates within Horizon Europe's NetZeroCities program structure, providing external oversight and integration with other pilot cities.

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs**

Specific total investment figures are not detailed in available sources, but the project involves:

- **Public Investment:** Municipal funding for planning, governance innovation, and infrastructure development
- **Private Investment:** Energy operators and technology providers invest in renewable energy installations and commercial operations
- **EU Funding:** Horizon Europe NetZeroCities program provides significant financial and institutional support for pilot activities
- **Research Funding:** Academic research components funded through research institution budgets and EU research programs

**Funding Mix**

The project represents a public-private partnership combining municipal resources, private sector capital, and EU funding. The specific proportions are not detailed, but municipal commitment to governance innovation and planning suggests substantial public investment in non-hardware components (stakeholder engagement, cross-departmental coordination, planning processes).

**Revenue Mechanisms**

Anticipated revenue sources include:

- **Energy Sales:** Biogas, solar electricity, and stored energy sold to grid or local consumers
- **Byproduct Sales:** Biochar, grass protein, and other circular economy outputs sold to agricultural and industrial markets
- **Waste Processing Fees:** Tipping fees for agricultural waste feedstock
- **Land Lease/Use:** Potential revenue from energy operator use of municipal or private land
- **Carbon Credits:** Potential revenue from carbon sequestration (biochar) or emissions reduction credits

**Financial Sustainability**

The circular economy model aims for financial sustainability through diversified revenue streams. Biogas production generates primary revenue; byproduct commercialization creates additional income; reduced waste management costs offset some expenses. However, Power-to-X technologies remain economically marginal without policy support (carbon pricing, subsidies for sustainable fuels). Long-term viability depends on sustained policy frameworks supporting renewable energy and circular economy economics.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges**

- **Power-to-X Economics:** Synthetic fuel production remains expensive relative to fossil fuels without policy support; scaling and cost reduction remain uncertain
- **Technology Integration:** Coordinating multiple interdependent systems (biogas, biochar, grass protein, solar, storage, PtX) requires sophisticated technical management and real-time optimization
- **Grid Integration:** Connecting distributed renewable generation and storage to existing grid infrastructure involves technical and regulatory complexity
- **Site-Specific Constraints:** Soil conditions, water availability, and existing land uses constrain facility placement and design

**Financial Challenges**

- **Capital Requirements:** Large upfront investment in renewable energy infrastructure and circular economy facilities creates financing barriers
- **Commodity Price Volatility:** Revenue from energy sales and byproducts depends on market prices subject to fluctuation
- **Subsidy Dependence:** Several components (particularly Power-to-X) remain dependent on policy support; policy changes threaten financial viability

**Social and Political Challenges**

- **NIMBY Persistence:** Despite co-creation efforts, some residents remain concerned about facility impacts (odor, noise, visual effects, property values)[^2]
- **Complexity of Co-creation:** Genuine participatory governance requires sustained engagement, conflict resolution capacity, and willingness to accommodate diverse preferences—more demanding than traditional top-down processes[^2]
- **Stakeholder Diversity:** Balancing interests of farmers, energy operators, residents, developers, and environmental advocates involves genuine trade-offs without universally satisfactory solutions
- **Displacement Risks:** Infrastructure development and associated land-use changes could displace existing residents or agricultural operations if not carefully managed
- **Political Continuity:** Municipal leadership changes could shift commitment to co-creation and long-term climate goals

**Administrative Challenges**

- **Bureaucratic Inertia:** Traditional municipal structures resist cross-departmental collaboration; institutional change requires sustained effort beyond the pilot phase[^2]
- **Coordination Complexity:** Aligning multiple organizations (municipality, energy operators, research institutions, civic associations) involves coordination costs and potential misalignment
- **Capacity Constraints:** Municipal staff lack experience with integrated planning and participatory governance; capacity-building requires training and time investment
- **Regulatory Navigation:** Navigating multiple regulatory frameworks (energy, planning, environmental, agricultural) involves complexity and potential inconsistencies

**Environmental Challenges**

- **Biodiversity Trade-offs:** Large-scale land-use change could impact existing ecosystems; balancing renewable energy expansion with conservation requires careful design
- **Water Management:** Biogas production and other facilities have water requirements; ensuring sustainable water use in peri-urban contexts involves careful management
- **Soil Impacts:** While biochar improves soil health, facility construction and operation could temporarily impact soil quality

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Problem-Solving Approaches**

The project demonstrates adaptive capacity through several strategies:

- **Iterative Co-creation:** Rather than treating stakeholder engagement as one-time consultation, multiple solution room sessions allow refinement based on emerging concerns and ideas[^2]
- **Scenario-Building:** Landscape architectural scenario visualization helps stakeholders understand trade-offs and collectively identify preferred alternatives[^1]
- **Reflexive Governance:** Built-in learning mechanisms enable the project to adapt based on experience[^1]
- **Flexibility in Design:** The project maintains flexibility to modify facility placement, design, and operations based on stakeholder input and technical learnings
- **Phased Implementation:** Sequencing development allows early phases to generate learning informing later phases

**Design Modifications**

While specific design changes during implementation are not detailed in available sources, the project's emphasis on scenario-building and iterative co-creation suggests willingness to modify plans based on stakeholder input. The landscape architectural approach enables design refinement to address visual and environmental concerns.

**Lessons Learned During Implementation**

Key insights emerging from the pilot include:

- **Governance as Primary Barrier:** Project leadership emphasizes that governance innovation represents a more significant barrier to climate transition than technology, validating the project's governance focus[^2]
- **Complexity of Genuine Co-creation:** Authentic participatory processes are more time-consuming and complex than traditional planning, requiring sustained commitment and sophisticated facilitation[^2]
- **Importance of Trust:** Informal dialogue and trust-building prove more effective than heavy bureaucracy in enabling productive stakeholder collaboration[^2]
- **Institutional Change Feasibility:** Demonstrating successful governance innovation in Spørring has generated institutional change, with co-creation becoming mandatory for new renewable energy projects, suggesting governance innovations can be institutionalized[^2]
- **Stakeholder Motivation Complexity:** Understanding what motivates different stakeholders to participate in land-use transformation proves more difficult than anticipated, requiring ongoing research and adaptation[^2]

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies**

Several elements of CO-SHAPE reflect specific Aarhus and Danish contexts:

- **Strong Municipal Governance:** Denmark's municipal autonomy and administrative capacity enable the integrated planning approach; contexts with more centralized or fragmented governance face replication barriers
- **Agricultural Hinterland:** Spørring's peri-urban agricultural character and existing biogas infrastructure provide specific advantages; purely urban or industrial contexts require adaptation
- **Climate Policy Support:** Denmark's progressive climate policies and EU Horizon Europe funding create enabling conditions; contexts with weaker policy support face greater challenges
- **Stakeholder Culture:** Danish emphasis on consensus-building and participatory governance aligns with co-creation approaches; contexts with different governance cultures require adaptation
- **Technical Infrastructure:** Existing biogas facilities and grid connections provide starting points; contexts lacking this infrastructure face higher development costs
- **Educational Institutions:** Partnership with Aarhus School of Architecture provides research capacity and intellectual resources; contexts without similar institutions require alternative knowledge sources

**Critical Success Factors**

Elements essential to positive outcomes include:

- **Political Commitment:** Municipal leadership genuinely committed to participatory governance and willing to invest time and resources in co-creation processes
- **Facilitation Capacity:** Skilled facilitators capable of managing complex stakeholder negotiations and building trust across diverse interests
- **Stakeholder Diversity:** Presence of multiple stakeholder groups with distinct interests creating genuine need for negotiation and compromise
- **Time and Resources:** Adequate investment in governance innovation and participatory processes; co-creation cannot be rushed or underfunded
- **Institutional Flexibility:** Municipal willingness to modify processes and structures based on learning; rigid adherence to predetermined plans undermines adaptation
- **Transparent Information Sharing:** Open communication about constraints, trade-offs, and decision-making processes enables informed participation
- **Tangible Benefits:** Demonstration that co-creation generates real benefits for participants; purely symbolic participation generates cynicism

**Scalability**

The approach demonstrates scalability potential across multiple dimensions:

- **Geographic Scale:** Methodology successfully applied to 1,000-hectare pilot area; principles potentially applicable to larger or smaller areas
- **Administrative Scale:** From neighborhood-level co-creation to municipal-wide governance integration to regional collaboration
- **Sectoral Application:** Beyond energy and land use, co-creation approaches potentially applicable to transportation, waste management, water systems, and other municipal challenges
- **Institutional Scale:** From pilot projects to institutionalized practice embedded in standard municipal operations

However, scaling requires:

- **Capacity Building:** Training and resource investment to enable widespread participatory governance
- **Institutional Adaptation:** Modifying municipal structures to support cross-departmental collaboration
- **Cultural Shift:** Moving from traditional hierarchical governance toward participatory models
- **Quality Maintenance:** Ensuring scaled-up co-creation maintains genuineness and stakeholder empowerment rather than becoming tokenistic

#### **Comparative Analysis**

**Similar Initiatives**

CO-SHAPE shares characteristics with several comparable European initiatives:

- **REGREEN (2019-2023):** European project examining nature-based solutions and stakeholder engagement in urban development; CO-SHAPE builds on REGREEN methodologies for co-creation and stakeholder engagement[^1]
- **Invest4Nature (2022-2026):** Project focused on nature-based solutions and sustainable investment; CO-SHAPE incorporates Invest4Nature approaches to ecological integration
- **MOSAIC (2018-2020):** Project addressing stakeholder engagement and co-creation; CO-SHAPE applies MOSAIC lessons to governance innovation[^1]
- **Other NetZeroCities Pilots:** Multiple European cities participating in NetZeroCities program implementing similar governance innovations and renewable energy expansion

Distinctive features of CO-SHAPE include its emphasis on peri-urban typology, integration of circular economy with renewable energy, and systematic focus on governance innovation as primary barrier to climate transition.

**Best Practices**

Elements representing exemplary practice include:

- **Early Stakeholder Involvement:** Engaging citizens, developers, and authorities from project inception rather than post-hoc consultation
- **Solution Room Methodology:** Creating informal spaces for negotiating complex trade-offs builds trust and generates creative solutions
- **Cross-Departmental Coordination:** Breaking down municipal silos enables integrated planning addressing multiple objectives simultaneously
- **Landscape Integration:** Treating renewable energy infrastructure as part of comprehensive landscape design rather than isolated technical facility
- **Circular Economy Principles:** Designing systems where outputs become inputs, maximizing resource efficiency and economic value
- **Reflexive Governance:** Building learning mechanisms into project design enables adaptation and continuous improvement
- **Quantified Objectives:** Setting specific, measurable targets (emissions reduction, stakeholder satisfaction, bureaucratic efficiency) enables accountability

**Cautionary Lessons**

Elements warranting careful consideration in replication include:

- **Co-creation Complexity:** Genuine participatory governance requires more time, resources, and facilitation skill than traditional planning; rushed or underfunded co-creation generates cynicism
- **Stakeholder Conflicts:** Not all interests can be fully satisfied; co-creation processes must acknowledge trade-offs and enable difficult negotiations rather than promising win-win outcomes
- **Implementation Risk:** Governance innovation does not guarantee successful project implementation; attention to technical and financial management remains essential
- **Institutional Resistance:** Existing municipal structures and practices resist cross-departmental collaboration; institutional change requires sustained effort beyond pilot phase
- **Gentrification Potential:** Infrastructure improvements and development can generate property value increases and displacement; explicit equity protections are necessary
- **Market Dependence:** Circular economy viability depends on markets for byproducts; weak markets can undermine economic sustainability

#### **Parallels to Green Building Neighborhoods**

CO-SHAPE aligns with green building neighborhood (GBN) principles in several dimensions:

**Shared Characteristics**

- **Integrated Sustainability:** Both address multiple sustainability dimensions (environmental, social, economic) simultaneously rather than single-issue focus
- **Participatory Design:** Both emphasize stakeholder involvement in shaping neighborhood development
- **Multifunctional Land Use:** Both combine residential, commercial, recreational, and environmental functions within integrated spatial design
- **Community Focus:** Both prioritize resident quality of life and social cohesion alongside environmental performance
- **Circular Economy:** Both incorporate waste reduction, resource cycling, and byproduct valorization
- **Governance Innovation:** Both experiment with participatory governance and cross-sectional coordination

**Distinctive Features of CO-SHAPE**

CO-SHAPE differs from typical GBN models in several ways:

- **Peri-Urban Focus:** Rather than urban neighborhood development, CO-SHAPE targets peri-urban transformation combining agricultural, energy, and recreational functions
- **Energy Production Emphasis:** Primary focus on renewable energy infrastructure rather than residential or mixed-use development
- **Governance Centrality:** CO-SHAPE positions governance innovation as primary objective equal to or exceeding technical/environmental outcomes
- **Agricultural Integration:** Incorporates farming as ongoing land use rather than converting agricultural land to urban development
- **Circular Economy Depth:** Emphasizes material cycling and waste valorization more comprehensively than typical GBN projects

**Transferable Strategies**

CO-SHAPE offers several strategies potentially applicable to GBN development:

- **Solution Room Methodology:** Structured stakeholder engagement process applicable to diverse urban development contexts
- **Cross-Departmental Coordination:** Breaking down municipal silos enables integrated planning in GBN development
- **Landscape Integration:** Treating infrastructure (energy, water, waste) as part of comprehensive landscape design rather than isolated technical systems
- **Circular Economy Design:** Incorporating waste reduction and resource cycling into neighborhood design from inception
- **Quantified Governance Metrics:** Measuring stakeholder satisfaction, trust, and bureaucratic efficiency alongside environmental and economic metrics
- **Reflexive Governance:** Building learning mechanisms into project design enables adaptation as implementation proceeds

**Adaptation Considerations**

Applying CO-SHAPE insights to GBN development requires context-specific adaptation:

- **Urban vs. Peri-Urban:** GBN development in dense urban areas faces different constraints and opportunities than peri-urban energy parks; participatory processes must address different stakeholder concerns
- **Residential Focus:** GBN development emphasizing residential function requires different co-creation processes than energy-focused projects; resident participation mechanisms must accommodate ongoing occupancy
- **Development Scale:** GBN projects typically smaller than 1,000-hectare Spørring; governance structures must scale appropriately
- **Existing Communities:** GBN development on occupied land involves displacement risks requiring explicit equity protections; greenfield projects like Spørring face different equity challenges
- **Technology Mix:** GBN projects emphasize building technology and urban design; energy infrastructure plays supporting rather than primary role
- **Timeline Integration:** GBN development typically operates on faster timelines than long-term peri-urban transformation; co-creation processes must accommodate development urgency

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Governance Innovation Leadership**

CO-SHAPE's most significant strength lies in demonstrating that governance innovation can overcome systemic barriers to climate transition. By positioning participatory processes as central rather than peripheral, the project challenges conventional planning paradigms and provides evidence that co-creation generates measurable benefits: 85% stakeholder satisfaction, 40% increase in public trust, 60% reduction in NIMBY sentiments, and 50% reduction in bureaucratic delays.[^1] This governance innovation potentially influences urban development practice more profoundly than any single technology.

**Comprehensive Integration**

The project exemplifies integrated sustainability addressing environmental, social, economic, and governance dimensions simultaneously. Rather than siloed approaches, CO-SHAPE combines renewable energy, circular economy, landscape architecture, community well-being, and institutional innovation into coherent systems. This holistic approach reflects sophisticated understanding of sustainability complexity.

**Stakeholder Empowerment**

The solution room methodology and participatory governance approach genuinely empower stakeholders to shape decisions affecting their environment. Rather than passive recipients of expert-determined plans, citizens, farmers, and developers become active co-designers. This empowerment strengthens social capital and democratic legitimacy.

**Institutional Embedding**

Perhaps most significantly, CO-SHAPE principles have been embedded into municipal policy and practice beyond the pilot phase. Co-creation requirements for new renewable energy projects, integration into climate action plans, and replication in 5 neighboring municipalities demonstrate that governance innovation can become institutionalized rather than remaining pilot anomaly.[^1][^2]

**Quantified Emissions Impact**

The project targets 100,000+ tonnes CO₂ equivalent annual reduction, representing approximately 10% of Aarhus's total emissions.[^2] This substantial contribution to climate neutrality goals demonstrates that governance innovation is compatible with significant environmental impact.

**Research Foundation**

Partnership with Aarhus School of Architecture provides rigorous intellectual foundation, enabling systematic learning and knowledge generation applicable beyond the specific project context. The postdoc research project examining sustainability transitions grounds the initiative in academic rigor.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Co-creation Intensity and Scalability**

While participatory governance represents a strength, the intensive resource requirements and time investment may limit scalability. The project's success depends on skilled facilitation, sustained stakeholder engagement, and municipal commitment—resources and capacities not universally available. Scaling co-creation approaches without maintaining quality risks generating tokenistic participation that undermines legitimacy.

**Implementation Timeline Uncertainty**

The project operates on a two-year intensive pilot phase within a longer timeline extending to 2027 and beyond. Specific implementation progress and achievement of quantified targets remain uncertain as of November 2025. The gap between governance innovation success and technical/environmental outcomes requires sustained attention.

**Technology Maturity Variability**

While biogas and solar represent mature technologies, Power-to-X systems remain at pilot scale with uncertain economics and scalability. Dependence on emerging technologies creates financial and technical risks. The project's long-term viability depends on technology cost reductions and policy support for synthetic fuels—conditions not fully within project control.

**Gentrification and Displacement Risks**

While co-creation processes emphasize stakeholder benefit-sharing, infrastructure development and associated land-use transformation create gentrification and displacement risks. Explicit equity protections and mechanisms ensuring existing residents benefit from development require ongoing attention. The project's emphasis on governance innovation does not automatically prevent displacement.

**Agricultural Transition Complexity**

The project involves fundamental transformation of agricultural land use from traditional farming to energy production and circular economy activities. Long-term viability of this transition depends on farmer acceptance, economic sustainability of new activities (grass protein production, biogas feedstock contracts), and market development for novel products. Risks include farmer resistance, market failures, and economic unsustainability of new agricultural models.

**Limited Diversity in Stakeholder Engagement**

While 85% stakeholder satisfaction indicates broad-based support, available sources do not detail demographic diversity of participants. Participatory processes risk being dominated by well-organized, educated, articulate stakeholders while marginalizing less-organized groups. Ensuring genuine inclusion of vulnerable populations requires explicit attention.

**Policy Dependence**

The project's long-term viability depends on sustained policy support for renewable energy, carbon pricing, and subsidies for sustainable fuels. Changes in municipal, national, or EU climate policy could undermine project economics and institutional support. The project operates within policy frameworks potentially subject to political change.

**Knowledge Transfer Uncertainty**

While the project emphasizes transferability and has generated a playbook of actions and procedures replicated in 5 neighboring municipalities, broader European replication remains uncertain. Context-specific adaptations may be necessary; the degree to which Aarhus's governance innovations transfer to different institutional contexts requires ongoing evaluation.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

**Distributional Equity**

The project aims to distribute benefits across multiple stakeholders: farmers (biogas feedstock income), residents (employment, improved environment, recreational amenities), energy consumers (local renewable energy), and businesses (circular economy markets). However, benefit distribution is unlikely to be equal; some stakeholders may capture disproportionate gains while others receive minimal benefits. Explicit mechanisms ensuring equitable benefit distribution require attention.

**Procedural Justice**

The participatory governance approach advances procedural justice by including affected stakeholders in decision-making. However, procedural justice does not guarantee fair outcomes; stakeholder groups with greater resources and political power may dominate co-creation processes. Ensuring genuine inclusion of marginalized groups requires explicit equity protections.

**Recognition Justice**

The project recognizes diverse stakeholder perspectives and values, treating farmers, residents, developers, and environmental advocates as legitimate participants in decision-making. This recognition justice strengthens democratic legitimacy and social cohesion.

**Environmental Justice**

Infrastructure development and land-use change create environmental impacts (facility emissions, noise, visual effects, ecosystem disruption) concentrated in specific locations. Ensuring that environmental burdens do not fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations requires explicit environmental justice analysis and protections.

**Intergenerational Justice**

The project advances intergenerational justice by addressing climate change, thereby protecting future generations from climate impacts. Conversely, infrastructure development creates long-term environmental and social legacies; ensuring these legacies are sustainable and equitable requires long-term perspective.

**Just Transition**

Agricultural workers and communities dependent on traditional farming face transition to new economic models. Ensuring this transition is "just"—providing retraining, income support, and genuine choice rather than coerced displacement—requires explicit attention to just transition principles.

**Long-term Trajectory**

CO-SHAPE appears positioned for sustained positive trajectory:

- **Institutional Embedding:** Integration into municipal policy and mandatory requirements for new projects suggests governance innovations will persist beyond pilot phase
- **Regional Expansion:** Successful replication in 5 neighboring municipalities demonstrates regional scaling potential
- **European Influence:** Contribution to NetZeroCities program and EU climate-neutral cities mission positions Aarhus as European leader in sustainable urban development
- **Research Continuation:** Postdoc research extending to 2027 ensures ongoing intellectual engagement and knowledge generation

- **Market Development:** Developing viable markets for novel products (grass protein, biochar, e-fuels) essential for economic sustainability

- **Equity Protection:** Ensuring benefits are equitably distributed and vulnerable populations are protected from negative impacts requires ongoing attention

- **Research Publication and Dissemination:** Academic outputs documenting governance innovations and sustainability outcomes
- **Policy Influence:** Influencing municipal, national, and European climate and urban development policies based on demonstrated successes

**Broader Significance**

CO-SHAPE's significance extends beyond specific project outcomes to influence how European cities approach climate transition. By demonstrating that governance innovation can overcome systemic barriers, the project challenges conventional planning paradigms and provides evidence-based alternatives. Success in Spørring suggests that climate neutrality is achievable not through top-down technical mandates but through inclusive processes enabling stakeholders to shape their own sustainable futures.

The project's emphasis on participatory governance, cross-departmental coordination, and integrated planning offers models potentially transforming urban development practice across Europe. If CO-SHAPE demonstrates that co-creation generates measurable benefits in stakeholder satisfaction, institutional efficiency, and climate outcomes, replication and scaling could substantially accelerate European climate transition while advancing democratic legitimacy and social equity.

However, realizing this potential requires sustained attention to equity, genuine stakeholder empowerment, adequate resourcing of participatory processes, and institutional commitment to governance innovation. The project's success will ultimately be measured not only by emissions reduction and technical outputs but by whether governance innovations become institutionalized practice and whether benefits are equitably distributed across diverse stakeholder groups.
