# Executive summary## Geothermal District Heating Network in Paris-Saclay

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Geothermal District Heating Network in Paris-Saclay
- **Alternative Names:** Paris-Saclay 5th Generation District Heating and Cooling Grid (5GDHC)
- **Location:** Paris-Saclay, Urban Campus, Orsay, Île-de-France, France
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 48.713° N, 2.168° E (Plateau de Saclay, 20 km southwest of Paris)
- **Timeline:**
  - Project planning and CREM contract: 2015
  - Drilling of geothermal wells: 2017-2018
  - Network inauguration: June 2019
  - Initial operation: 2019-2022 (Idex)
  - Veolia takeover: January 2023[^1]
  - Ongoing extension and integration with new buildings (target 2024+)[^2][^3]
- **Project Scale:**
  - 12 km network (target: 25 km)[^3]
  - 650,000 m² connected (target: 2,150,000 m²)[^2][^3]
  - Over 10,000 households equivalent[^2]
  - 50+ MW combined heating/cooling capacity[^3]
  - €56 million initial investment[^2]
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - EPA Paris-Saclay (public planning authority)
  - Idex Group (initial contractor/operator)
  - Veolia (current operator)[^1]
  - Consortium partners: Egis, Artelia (design/supervision)
- **Funding Sources:** Primarily public funding (EPA Paris-Saclay), European Interreg NWE D2Grids program, potential PPP elements[^3][^4]

#### **Executive Summary**
The Paris-Saclay Geothermal District Heating Network is a pioneering energy infrastructure serving one of Europe’s foremost innovation clusters. Conceived to deliver **low-carbon heating and cooling** to a rapidly expanding mix of **residential, academic, and commercial buildings**, the initiative harnesses deep geothermal resources from the Albian aquifer, coupled with advanced heat pumps and a 5th generation, low-temperature, closed-loop grid. 

Since its inauguration in 2019, the network has supplied over **60% of heating demand** through renewable and recovered energy, notably reducing local greenhouse gas emissions (4x less CO₂ than conventional systems)[^2]. The system is **automatically controlled** 24/7, balancing thermal needs across diverse users and integrating heat recovery from industrial and scientific facilities (e.g., CNRS supercomputer, Synchrotron Soleil)[^1]. 

A flagship European demonstrator under the D2Grids program, Paris-Saclay’s grid exemplifies **demand-driven, decentralized urban energy**, with robust stakeholder collaboration and ongoing network expansion. It stands as a model for sustainable urban development, offering **stable long-term energy costs**, improved air quality, and social benefits for the local community, while providing transferable lessons for cities seeking resilient, climate-aligned energy solutions[^3][^4].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Paris-Saclay is an urban campus and innovation cluster in the southwest periphery of Paris, Île-de-France. The area is home to leading universities (University Paris-Saclay, Institut Polytechnique de Paris), research centers, and tech companies, forming a strategic node in France’s knowledge economy[^5]. The population is highly educated, internationally diverse, and the economic base is research, education, and high-tech industry.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
- **Historical patterns:** Transition from agricultural plateau to high-tech and academic campus since early 2000s.
- **Land use:** Mix of student residences, laboratories, office buildings, commercial spaces, and green corridors[^3].
- **Demographics:** Predominantly students, researchers, and professionals; multicultural, high income and education levels.
- **Infrastructure:** Modern, with new buildings designed for energy efficiency; legacy infrastructure being renovated.
- **Environmental conditions:** Plateau topography, proximity to major aquifers (Albian Sands), moderate climate, air quality concerns linked to regional traffic, but strong environmental policy framework.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**
- High carbon footprint from conventional heating (gas, oil)[^2][^3]
- Need to support rapid urban growth sustainably
- Inadequate energy resilience and rising energy costs
- Lack of integration between new and existing energy systems

**Triggering Events:**
- National and EU climate targets (COP21, Energy Transition for Green Growth Act)[^3]
- Regional push for innovation-led growth
- EPA Paris-Saclay’s strategic planning for campus expansion

**Stakeholder Landscape:**
- Public authorities (EPA Paris-Saclay, local municipalities)
- Academic and research institutions
- Private energy companies (Idex, Veolia)
- Developers, residents, and business tenants

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**  
EPA Paris-Saclay holds mandate for local land use and infrastructure planning[^3]. Strong alignment with French national energy policy and EU directives (Renewable Energy Directive, Energy Efficiency Directive).

**Relevant Policies:**  
- Energy Transition for Green Growth Act (France)
- Paris Region’s climate and energy strategy
- Zoning for district energy systems
- Participation in European D2Grids program

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
- Legal authority for network development vested in EPA
- CREM contracts facilitate integrated design-build-operate-maintain cycle[^3][^1]
- Complexity in coordinating multiple actors and negotiating connection tariffs

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**  
Guided by principles of energy sobriety, circular economy, and collaborative governance. Emphasizes local renewable resources, decentralized management, and demand-driven operation[^3].

**Physical/Spatial Design:**  
- Deep geothermal wells (Albian aquifer, 700+ meters)[^5]
- Two heatplants (Moulon, Ecole Polytechnique)[^5]
- 12 km underground grid, expanding to 25 km[^3]
- Decentralized substations, tempered loop, advanced metering

**Scope and Phasing:**  
- Phase 1: Initial grid construction, well drilling (2015-2018)
- Phase 2: Campus connections, commissioning (2019)
- Phase 3: Further neighborhood expansion (2020+)
- Ongoing: Integration with new buildings, heat recovery projects[^2][^3]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**
- Deep geothermal doublets (production/injection wells)[^5]
- Heat pumps (distributed, building-level)
- Low-temperature closed-loop grid (tempered water, ~20°C)[^3]
- Automated control (24/7 monitoring, remote diagnostics)
- Photovoltaic integration for self-consumption and grid support[^3]

**Innovation Type:**  
Technological (5GDHC, geothermal, smart grids), financial (CREM contract), governance (EPA-led), process (scientific co-design).

**Integration Approach:**  
Connection to both new and renovated buildings; interoperability with campus electrical grid; future biomass boiler under study[^2].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:**
- CREM contract with Idex Group (design, build, operate, maintain, 2015-2022)[^1]
- Competitive tendering for network extension
- Veolia selected as operator (2023+)[^1]

**Stakeholder Engagement:**  
Consultations with building owners, research institutions, residents. Scientific advisory input for ground temperature and system optimization[^3].

**Participatory Elements:**  
Opportunities for feedback via campus associations, user panels, and scientific partnerships.

**Project Management:**  
EPA Paris-Saclay oversees coordination; Artelia and Egis for technical design/supervision[^2][^3].

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:**  
€56 million initial investment[^2]; ongoing costs for grid extension.

**Funding Mix:**  
Primarily public funds (EPA), European D2Grids support, potential PPP for future phases[^2][^3][^4].

**Revenue Mechanisms:**  
User energy fees (regulated tariffs), shared savings from renewable integration, sale of excess photovoltaic energy[^3].

**Financial Sustainability:**  
Long-term contracts, stable energy pricing, reinvestment of savings into network upgrades.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Geological risk (reservoir heterogeneity, thermal breakthrough), well drilling complexity, thermal balance management[^5]
- **Financial:** High upfront costs, need to secure long-term funding
- **Social/Political:** Coordination between diverse stakeholders, managing expectations, tariff negotiations
- **Administrative:** Multi-actor governance, integration with legacy systems, regulatory approvals
- **Environmental:** Risk of aquifer depletion, ground temperature monitoring, ensuring sustainable reinjection[^5]

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
- Advanced 3D reservoir modeling to manage geological risks[^5]
- Real-time ground temperature and flow monitoring
- Adaptive control algorithms for demand-response and peak management
- Phased expansion to control costs and manage complexity

**Design Modifications:**  
- Inclusion of heat recovery from scientific equipment[^3][^1]
- Planned addition of biomass boiler for backup[^2]
- Integration of photovoltaic systems on rooftops for grid support[^3]

**Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
- Importance of stakeholder buy-in and scientific collaboration
- Need for flexible, modular network design
- Continuous performance monitoring enables rapid response to operational issues[^5]

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** 12 km grid, two heatplants, 650,000 m² connected (target 2.15 million m²)[^2][^3][^5]
- **Service Delivery:** 24/7 heating and cooling for campus and residential users, automated demand management[^3]
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Over 60% heating demand met by geothermal energy; 100 GWh/year heat and 20 GWh/year cooling delivered; 10,000+ household equivalents[^2]

- **Community Benefits:** Lower energy bills, improved thermal comfort, enhanced air quality[^2][^3]
- **Equity Outcomes:** Inclusive access for academic and social housing, efforts to minimize energy poverty

- **Property Values:** Likely increase due to improved infrastructure and environmental quality (data not specified)
- **Cost Savings:** Long-term reduction in energy expenses for users, stable tariffs[^2][^3]

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate/Emissions:** Fourfold reduction in CO₂ emissions versus conventional heating[^2]

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**
