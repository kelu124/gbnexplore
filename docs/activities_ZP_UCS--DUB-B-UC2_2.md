# Executive summary## Open Thor Living Lab

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Open Thor Living Lab
- **Alternative Names:** oPEN Thor, Open Thor, Thor Living Lab
- **Location:** Genk, Limburg, Belgium (located on a former coal mining site)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Genk, Belgium (approximately 50.96°N, 5.93°E)
- **Timeline:** Project launch October 19, 2021; ongoing as of November 2025
- **Project Scale:** Encompasses five integrated zones including Thor science and business park, New Texas social housing estate (27-30+ residential units), adjacent Waterschei garden district, KRC Genk buildings and grounds; serves over 400 international researchers
- **Leading Organization(s):** EnergyVille (partnership between Belgian universities and research institutes), NV Thor Park, City of Genk, VITO (Flemish Institute for Technological Research)
- **Funding Sources:** European Union's Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme (Grant agreement No. 101037080); co-funded through various mechanisms including H2020, Interreg, and bilateral/multilateral contract research

#### **Executive Summary**

The Open Thor Living Lab represents a comprehensive European innovation initiative designed to accelerate the energy transition through real-world experimentation and validation in an integrated urban-industrial ecosystem.[^1][^2] Located on a former coal mining site in Genk, Belgium, the initiative transforms a post-industrial landscape into a state-of-the-art testing environment where innovative energy solutions can be developed, tested, and scaled.[^3] The project focuses specifically on energy transition challenges in three interconnected domains: housing, work, and mobility.[^1][^2] 

The initiative operates as Europe's first regulatory sandbox for energy in Flanders, enabling experiments that deviate from current regulations while maintaining real-world applicability.[^3] Through a quadruple helix collaboration model involving research institutions, industry partners, government bodies, and residential communities, the Open Thor Living Lab addresses systemic challenges including energy poverty, building energy efficiency, renewable energy integration, and sustainable mobility.[^1][^4] The project encompasses 33 international partners from 7 countries working toward the development of Positive Energy Neighbourhoods (PENs)—areas that produce more energy than they consume.[^4] By integrating cutting-edge technologies with participatory governance and rigorous scientific validation, the initiative aims to generate scalable solutions applicable to the broader European renovation wave and energy transition agenda.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:** Genk is a municipality located in the Belgian province of Limburg, positioned in the southeastern part of Flanders near the Dutch and German borders. Historically, Genk developed as a coal mining center during the 20th century, with the mining industry serving as the primary economic driver and employer for decades. The city's economy and identity became deeply intertwined with coal extraction, creating both prosperity and vulnerability as global energy markets shifted away from fossil fuels. With Belgium's transition away from coal and the closure of mining operations, Genk faced significant economic restructuring challenges, including employment loss, population decline, and the need to repurpose extensive industrial infrastructure. The city has positioned itself as a pioneer in sustainable development and the circular economy, leveraging its industrial heritage as a foundation for innovation in clean energy and advanced manufacturing.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The Open Thor Living Lab is situated on a former coal mining site, a landscape marked by industrial infrastructure, degraded environments, and underutilized land. The initiative encompasses five distinct but interconnected zones:[^1][^2]

- **Thor Science and Business Park:** A repurposed industrial area now hosting technology companies, research facilities, and innovation-focused enterprises
- **New Texas Social Housing Estate:** A residential area containing 27-30+ social housing units serving lower-income residents, representing vulnerable populations historically affected by energy poverty
- **Waterschei Garden District:** An adjacent suburban residential area with mixed housing types and demographics
- **KRC Genk:** Sports and recreational facilities providing community gathering spaces
- **EnergyVille Research Facilities:** Integrated laboratory infrastructure for energy research and testing

The demographic profile includes a significant proportion of lower-income households, particularly within the social housing component, reflecting broader patterns of residential segregation and economic inequality. The area represents a post-industrial landscape transitioning toward knowledge-based economic activities and sustainable development. Existing infrastructure quality varies, with some buildings representing older construction standards requiring substantial energy efficiency improvements. Environmental conditions have been shaped by historical mining activities, industrial pollution, and land degradation, though remediation efforts have been undertaken as part of site repurposing.

**Pre-Initiative Conditions:** The site exemplified post-industrial decline characteristic of European coal regions. The closure of coal mining operations eliminated primary employment sources, creating economic stagnation and social challenges. The physical landscape bore the marks of extractive industry—contaminated land, abandoned infrastructure, and degraded ecosystems. Existing residential areas, particularly social housing estates, faced energy poverty challenges, with residents struggling to afford adequate heating and cooling due to poor building performance and limited income. The broader region needed economic revitalization and new development pathways to replace lost mining revenues and employment.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:** The Open Thor Living Lab was designed to address multiple interconnected urban and energy challenges:[^4][^2]

- **Energy Poverty:** Vulnerable residents in social housing faced unaffordable energy bills due to inefficient building stock and limited income
- **Building Energy Inefficiency:** Existing residential and commercial buildings, many constructed before modern energy standards, consumed excessive energy for heating, cooling, and electricity
- **Limited Renewable Energy Integration:** Traditional energy systems lacked mechanisms for distributed renewable generation and storage
- **Post-Industrial Economic Decline:** The region required new economic activities and employment opportunities following coal industry closure
- **Lack of Integrated Energy Solutions:** Energy systems operated in silos without coordination between buildings, district infrastructure, and mobility services
- **Insufficient Testing Environments:** The absence of regulatory flexibility prevented real-world validation of innovative energy technologies and business models
- **Limited Knowledge Transfer:** Gap between research innovations and practical market deployment

**Triggering Events:** The initiative emerged from convergent drivers: the European Union's commitment to climate neutrality by 2050 and the recognition that buildings account for over one-third of EU CO2 emissions;[^4] Belgium's energy transition policies and commitment to the Horizon 2020 research framework; the need for economic restructuring in post-coal regions; and growing recognition that most required technologies already existed but lacked practical validation in renovation contexts.[^4]

**Stakeholder Landscape:** Pre-initiative stakeholders included local government authorities concerned with economic development and social welfare; residents of social housing facing energy affordability challenges; property owners and managers; energy companies and utilities with established business models potentially threatened by distributed generation; research institutions seeking real-world testing environments; technology companies interested in market validation; and environmental advocates pushing for sustainability transitions.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:** The initiative operates under a multi-level governance structure involving European Union coordination (through the Horizon 2020 framework and VITO/EnergyVille leadership), Belgian national and regional (Flemish) authorities, local municipal government (City of Genk), and private sector partners (Thor Park NV and participating companies). This creates a complex but potentially robust governance arrangement spanning international, national, regional, and local scales.

**Relevant Policies:** The initiative aligns with several policy frameworks:[^4][^2]

- **European Union's Climate Neutrality Objective (2050):** Commitment to net-zero greenhouse gas emissions
- **European Renovation Wave:** EU strategy to accelerate building renovation rates and improve energy efficiency
- **Horizon 2020 Research and Innovation Programme:** EU framework funding innovation projects addressing societal challenges
- **Positive Energy Neighbourhoods (PEN) Concept:** Emerging policy framework for communities producing net energy surpluses
- **Belgian Energy Transition Policies:** National commitments to renewable energy integration and building efficiency
- **Circular Economy Principles:** Regional emphasis on resource efficiency and waste reduction
- **Regulatory Sandbox Designation:** Flanders' recognition of Open Thor as the first regulatory sandbox for energy, enabling experimental deviation from standard regulations

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** The regulatory sandbox designation represents a critical enabler, allowing the initiative to test technologies and business models that might otherwise violate existing regulations.[^3] This flexibility facilitates experimentation with novel approaches to energy management, district-level systems, and demand response mechanisms. However, the complexity of multi-level governance creates potential coordination challenges and delays. Property rights and tenant protections in social housing may constrain certain implementation approaches. Building codes and safety standards, while necessary, may require modification to accommodate innovative technologies. The need for multiple permits and approvals across jurisdictional levels can slow implementation.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:** The Open Thor Living Lab pursues multiple integrated objectives:[^1][^4][^2]

- **Demonstrate Positive Energy Neighbourhood Viability:** Prove that existing residential areas can be transformed into net energy-producing communities through integrated technologies and management systems
- **Address Energy Poverty:** Enable vulnerable residents to access affordable, sustainable energy through efficiency improvements and distributed renewable generation
- **Accelerate Building Renovation:** Develop and validate practical solutions for retrofitting existing buildings—the vast majority of which will remain standing through 2050
- **Integrate Energy Systems:** Create seamless coordination between building-level, district-level, and mobility-related energy systems
- **Validate Emerging Technologies:** Test heat pumps, battery storage, smart charging, district heating networks, DC grids, and intelligent data platforms in real-world conditions
- **Generate Scalable Solutions:** Produce replicable models and knowledge applicable across Europe's building stock and energy systems

**Vision Statement:** The initiative aspires toward a future where European cities transition to sustainable, energy-positive neighborhoods through integrated technological innovation, participatory governance, and equitable access to clean energy—transforming post-industrial landscapes into models of circular economy and climate resilience.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:** The Open Thor Living Lab directly supports the European Union's 2050 climate neutrality objective and the European Renovation Wave strategy. It contributes to Belgium's energy transition commitments and Flanders' circular economy initiatives. Locally, it aligns with Genk's economic diversification strategy, transforming the city from a coal-dependent economy toward a knowledge-based, sustainable development hub.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:** The initiative operates on a causal logic: if innovative energy technologies (renewable generation, storage, heat pumps, smart systems) are integrated at building and district scales; and if real residents and businesses participate in co-creating solutions; and if researchers and industry partners collaborate in a regulatory sandbox enabling experimentation; then effective, scalable solutions for energy poverty, building efficiency, and renewable integration will be validated and accelerated toward market deployment, ultimately enabling Europe's broader energy transition and climate objectives.

**Target Beneficiaries:** Immediate beneficiaries include residents of social housing units gaining access to efficient, affordable energy systems; businesses and research institutions accessing world-class testing infrastructure; technology companies obtaining real-world validation pathways; and local economies benefiting from innovation-sector employment. Secondary beneficiaries include the broader European building sector accessing validated renovation solutions; utilities and energy companies adapting to distributed generation models; and ultimately, European populations benefiting from climate mitigation and energy security.

**Innovation Elements:** The initiative combines technological, social, and governance innovations:[^1][^4][^2]

- **Technological Integration:** Unprecedented coordination of PV systems, solar thermal collectors, heat pumps, home batteries, EV charging infrastructure, district-level battery storage, 5th-generation district heating networks, DC grids, and intelligent data platforms
- **Regulatory Innovation:** Establishment of Europe's first energy regulatory sandbox enabling controlled experimentation outside standard regulations
- **Governance Innovation:** Quadruple helix collaboration model integrating research, industry, government, and residential community participation
- **Social Innovation:** Participatory co-creation processes centering resident input in technology selection and system design
- **Business Model Innovation:** Exploration of novel financing, ownership, and energy community structures

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:** The Open Thor Living Lab draws from several planning and development philosophies:[^1][^2]

- **Living Lab Methodology:** Real-world experimentation in authentic user environments combining technology, research, and community participation
- **Systems Integration Approach:** Recognition that energy challenges require coordinated solutions across buildings, infrastructure, and user behavior
- **Circular Economy Principles:** Transformation of a post-industrial mining site into a productive, regenerative ecosystem
- **Energy Justice Framework:** Explicit focus on addressing energy poverty and ensuring equitable access to sustainable energy
- **Adaptive Management:** Flexibility to modify approaches based on real-world learning and changing conditions
- **Stakeholder Co-Creation:** Collaborative design processes involving multiple perspectives and knowledge systems

**Physical/Spatial Design:** The initiative involves comprehensive transformation of the former mining site:[^1][^2]

- **Building Retrofits:** Renovation of existing residential and commercial structures to near-zero energy building (NZEB) standards, representing a "unique renovation showcase"
- **Renewable Energy Infrastructure:** Installation of rooftop photovoltaic systems, solar thermal collectors, and concentrated solar thermal energy systems across multiple buildings
- **District-Level Systems:** Development of 5th-generation low-temperature district heating networks, battery storage systems, and energy hubs serving multiple buildings
- **Smart Charging Infrastructure:** 55+ electric vehicle charging points with intelligent demand response capabilities
- **Grid Infrastructure:** Installation of direct current (DC) grids enabling more efficient energy distribution
- **Landscape Restoration:** Remediation and greening of the former mining site, including the Waterschei garden district
- **Research Facilities:** Integration of EnergyVille laboratories for material and system-level testing

**Scope and Phasing:** The project operates across multiple phases and zones simultaneously:[^1][^2] Initial phase focused on establishing research infrastructure and retrofitting pilot residential units. Subsequent phases gradually expanded to additional buildings and systems. The initiative maintains ongoing experimentation with new technologies and business models. Phasing allows for adaptive learning, with each phase informing subsequent implementation approaches.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:** The Open Thor Living Lab integrates an extensive technological ecosystem:[^1][^3]

- **Renewable Energy Generation:** Rooftop photovoltaic (PV) systems, solar thermal collectors, concentrated solar thermal energy systems
- **Thermal Systems:** Heat pumps (individual and communal), district heating/cooling networks (5th generation low-temperature systems)
- **Energy Storage:** Home batteries, district-level battery storage systems, thermal storage in district heating networks
- **Electric Mobility:** Smart EV charging infrastructure (55+ charging points), DC charging systems
- **Grid Infrastructure:** Direct current (DC) grids, AC medium voltage grids, grid emulation systems for testing
- **Digital Systems:** Intelligent data platforms, cloud-based monitoring and optimization (SmarThor cloud platform, Azure cloud integration), building energy management systems, smart metering
- **Integrated Systems:** Energy hubs coordinating multiple technologies, plug-and-play modular boxes for technical installations
- **Laboratory Infrastructure:** Battery testing labs, district heating/cooling models, grid emulation facilities, material testing capabilities

**Innovation Type:** The technological innovations span multiple categories:[^1][^4][^2][^3]

- **Technological Innovation:** Novel integration of renewable generation, storage, and demand response at district scale; development of 5th-generation district heating systems; DC grid implementation
- **Social Innovation:** Participatory design processes; energy community models; resident engagement in system optimization
- **Financial Innovation:** Novel financing mechanisms for building renovation and energy infrastructure in low-income areas
- **Governance Innovation:** Regulatory sandbox enabling experimental deployment; quadruple helix collaboration structures; data-sharing agreements
- **Process Innovation:** Integrated renovation methodologies combining energy efficiency, renewable generation, and behavior change

**Integration Approach:** Technologies are deliberately integrated at multiple levels:[^1][^2][^3]

- **Building Level:** Individual units equipped with PV, heat pumps, batteries, and smart controls
- **District Level:** Interconnected systems enabling load balancing, storage optimization, and coordinated demand response
- **System Level:** Integration with broader electricity, heating, and mobility networks through SmarThor cloud platform and data management systems
- **Behavioral Level:** Digital tools and platforms enabling resident participation in energy optimization decisions

**Procurement and Contracting:** The initiative operates through multiple contracting mechanisms:[^1][^2]

- **Technology Procurement:** Selection and installation of equipment from multiple vendors (including major companies like Daikin contributing heat pump expertise)

- **Community Involvement:** Regular engagement with neighborhood associations and community organizations
- **Business Partnership:** Collaboration with technology companies, energy utilities, and service providers
- **Government Coordination:** Integration of municipal, regional, and national government perspectives
- **Research Collaboration:** Continuous engagement with 400+ international researchers and multiple research institutions

**Participatory Elements:** The quadruple helix model ensures multiple stakeholder voices:[^1][^2][^5]

- **Residents as Co-Creators:** Community members participate in system design, technology selection, and optimization decisions
- **Transparent Communication:** Regular webinars, newsletters, and public reporting on project progress and findings
- **Feedback Mechanisms:** Structured processes for incorporating resident and stakeholder input into project modifications
- **Benefit Sharing:** Explicit focus on ensuring that vulnerable residents benefit from energy efficiency improvements and cost savings

**Project Management:** The initiative operates through coordinated governance structures:[^1][^2]

- **Local Management:** Open Thor management team coordinates activities across the five zones
- **Research Program Management:** Research Program Manager oversees scientific validation and data collection
- **Workstream Organization:** Distinct workstreams addressing technology integration, social engagement, data management, and business model development

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges:** The initiative encountered several engineering and technology-related obstacles:[^4][^2]

- **Building Retrofit Complexity:** Older buildings designed without consideration for modern energy technologies present substantial integration challenges. Poor insulation, inadequate structural capacity for solar installations, and incompatible existing systems require creative problem-solving
- **System Integration:** Coordinating multiple technologies (renewable generation, storage, heating, cooling, mobility) across district scales demands sophisticated control systems and communication protocols
- **Grid Compatibility:** Integration of distributed generation and storage with existing electricity grids requires advanced grid management and potential infrastructure upgrades
- **Data Management:** Handling vast quantities of real-time energy data from multiple sources and systems requires robust cybersecurity, privacy protection, and analytical capabilities
- **Temperature Control:** Achieving thermal comfort in retrofitted buildings while minimizing energy consumption presents ongoing optimization challenges
- **Technology Reliability:** Ensuring consistent performance of emerging technologies in real-world conditions, particularly novel systems like 5th-generation district heating networks

**Financial Challenges:** Economic constraints affected implementation:[^4][^2]

- **Retrofit Costs:** The expense of retrofitting existing buildings to near-zero energy standards significantly exceeds typical renovation budgets, creating affordability challenges particularly for social housing
- **Upfront Investment Requirements:** The capital intensity of renewable generation, storage, and smart infrastructure requires substantial initial investment before operational cost savings materialize
- **Funding Gaps:** While Horizon 2020 support is substantial, covering the full costs of comprehensive district-scale transformation required additional co-funding from regional and private sources
- **Financial Viability Uncertainty:** Emerging business models for energy communities and district-level systems lack established financial track records, creating investment risk

**Social and Political Challenges:** Human and governance dimensions presented obstacles:[^4][^2]

- **Resident Acceptance:** Some residents of social housing expressed concerns about disruption during renovation, unfamiliar technologies, or potential displacement
- **Energy Poverty Persistence:** Ensuring that vulnerable residents actually benefit from efficiency improvements and renewable generation, rather than experiencing displacement or gentrification
- **Behavioral Change:** Encouraging residents to actively participate in energy optimization and adopt new consumption patterns requires sustained engagement and education
- **Political Support:** Maintaining political commitment across electoral cycles and multiple government levels required sustained advocacy
- **Stakeholder Alignment:** Coordinating interests of diverse stakeholders (researchers, companies, residents, utilities, government) with different priorities and incentives

**Administrative Challenges:** Organizational and procedural obstacles emerged:[^1][^2]

- **Multi-Level Governance Complexity:** Coordinating activities across European, national, regional, and local government levels created bureaucratic complexity and potential delays
- **Regulatory Approval Processes:** Obtaining necessary permits and approvals for experimental technologies and operations required navigation of complex regulatory frameworks
- **Data Privacy and Sharing:** Establishing protocols for collecting, sharing, and analyzing residential energy data while protecting privacy and complying with GDPR requirements
- **Institutional Coordination:** Ensuring effective collaboration among 33 international partners with different organizational cultures, priorities, and working methods
- **Capacity Constraints:** Building sufficient technical expertise and management capacity to oversee complex multi-technology systems

**Environmental Challenges:** Site-specific environmental issues required attention:[^2][^3]

- **Legacy Contamination:** The former mining site contained residual contamination from historical industrial activities, requiring remediation before full development
- **Soil Conditions:** Mining history affected soil properties, potentially complicating construction and infrastructure installation
- **Groundwater Considerations:** Former mining activities affected groundwater hydrology, requiring careful management

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:** The initiative employed multiple strategies to address challenges:[^1][^4][^2]

- **Phased Implementation:** Rather than attempting comprehensive transformation immediately, the project proceeded through phases, allowing learning from early experiences to inform subsequent implementation
- **Flexible Technology Selection:** Willingness to modify technology choices based on real-world performance and resident feedback, rather than rigidly adhering to initial plans
- **Enhanced Engagement:** Intensified communication and participatory processes to address resident concerns and build support
- **Regulatory Flexibility:** Leveraging the regulatory sandbox designation to test approaches that might otherwise violate regulations, then advocating for regulatory modifications based on demonstrated success
- **Collaborative Problem-Solving:** Engaging diverse stakeholders in identifying solutions rather than imposing top-down approaches
- **Continuous Monitoring:** Real-time data collection and analysis enabling rapid identification and response to emerging problems
- **Financial Innovation:** Exploring diverse financing mechanisms to address cost barriers, including innovative ownership structures and revenue models

**Design Modifications:** The initiative made substantive changes to original plans based on learning:[^1][^2]

- **Technology Prioritization:** Focusing initial efforts on technologies with demonstrated viability (heat pumps, solar PV) while continuing development of more experimental approaches (5th-generation district heating, DC grids)
- **Scope Adjustment:** Expanding from initial pilot zones to broader district coverage as technical capabilities and social acceptance increased
- **Resident-Centered Design:** Incorporating resident feedback into system design, ensuring technologies aligned with actual user needs and preferences rather than theoretical optimization
- **Incremental Scaling:** Rather than attempting full district transformation immediately, gradually expanding systems as operational experience accumulated
- **Business Model Evolution:** Adjusting energy community and financing structures based on legal constraints and practical experience

**Lessons Learned During Implementation:** Critical insights emerged that shaped project evolution:[^4][^2]

- **Importance of Affordability:** Energy transition solutions must explicitly address affordability for vulnerable populations; efficiency alone is insufficient without attention to energy costs
- **Behavioral Dimensions:** Technology deployment succeeds only when combined with sustained resident engagement and support for behavioral change
- **System Integration Value:** Coordinating multiple technologies across district scales yields greater benefits than isolated building-level improvements
- **Regulatory Barriers:** Existing regulations, while well-intentioned, often impede innovative solutions; regulatory sandboxes provide valuable testing grounds
- **Scalability Requirements:** Solutions must be economically viable at scale; pilot projects that function only with substantial subsidies lack broader applicability
- **Stakeholder Coordination:** Multi-stakeholder initiatives require significant time investment in communication and alignment; shortcuts create downstream problems
- **Data Importance:** Real-time energy data and analytics enable continuous optimization; data systems should be prioritized infrastructure investments

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

The Open Thor Living Lab demonstrates multiple commendable strengths that position it as exemplary practice in urban energy transition:

**Comprehensive Systems Integration:** The initiative's most significant strength lies in its holistic approach integrating renewable generation, energy storage, heating systems, mobility, and smart controls at building and district scales. Rather than addressing energy challenges in isolation, the project recognizes that optimal solutions require coordinated optimization across multiple domains. This systems thinking generates synergies—for example, using EV charging flexibility to balance renewable generation variability, or coordinating heating demand with renewable energy availability—that isolated interventions cannot achieve.

**Explicit Equity Focus:** The initiative's deliberate prioritization of energy poverty reduction and vulnerable populations distinguishes it from many development projects that inadvertently exacerbate inequality. By locating the project in social housing and ensuring that residents benefit from efficiency improvements and renewable generation, the initiative demonstrates commitment to equitable energy transition. The affordability protections and participatory governance structures aim to prevent displacement and ensure that vulnerable populations share in transition benefits.

**Genuine Participatory Governance:** The quadruple helix collaboration model and participatory co-creation processes represent authentic engagement rather than token consultation. Residents participate in technology selection, system design, and optimization decisions. This genuine inclusion not only produces more appropriate solutions but also builds social capital and community agency.

**Regulatory Innovation:** The regulatory sandbox designation enabling controlled experimentation outside standard regulations represents critical innovation. By providing flexibility to test novel approaches while maintaining safety and accountability, the sandbox accelerates learning and enables validation of solutions that rigid regulatory frameworks would prohibit. This model influences policy development across Europe.

**Institutional Permanence:** Integration within EnergyVille's long-term research infrastructure ensures that the living lab persists beyond typical project cycles. This permanence enables sustained learning, adaptation, and continued innovation rather than the common pattern of initiatives ending when initial funding expires.

**International Knowledge Network:** Collaboration with 33 partners from 7 countries and 400+ international researchers creates a robust knowledge network enabling cross-pollination of ideas and learning. The parallel oPEN Lab sites in Spain and Estonia enable comparative analysis and context-specific adaptation.

**Data-Driven Approach:** The sophisticated monitoring, analysis, and optimization systems enable continuous improvement based on real-world performance data. This data-driven methodology generates evidence about what works in real conditions, supporting more effective future interventions.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

Despite significant strengths, the initiative faces several limitations and challenges:

**Scale and Replication Challenges:** While the Open Thor Living Lab demonstrates viability of comprehensive energy transition approaches, scaling to the millions of buildings requiring renovation across Europe presents substantial challenges. The initiative's success depends on factors—EU Horizon 2020 funding, regulatory flexibility, established research institutions, skilled workforce—that may not exist in all contexts. Replication in contexts lacking these enabling conditions may prove significantly more difficult.

**Technology Cost Barriers:** The capital intensity of comprehensive building retrofits and integrated energy systems creates affordability challenges that limit replication, particularly in lower-income regions or countries lacking substantial public investment capacity. While the initiative demonstrates technical viability, it may not demonstrate financial viability at scale without continued subsidies or innovative financing mechanisms.

**Complexity and Expertise Requirements:** The sophistication of integrated multi-technology systems requires substantial technical expertise in design, installation, operation, and maintenance. The initiative's success partly reflects access to world-class research institutions and international expertise. Contexts lacking comparable technical capacity may struggle with system operation and optimization.

**Behavioral Change Limitations:** While the initiative emphasizes resident engagement and data-driven recommendations, the actual extent to which residents modify consumption behavior remains unclear. Technology alone proves insufficient; sustained behavioral change requires ongoing engagement and support that may be difficult to maintain at scale.

**Potential Gentrification Risks:** Despite explicit affordability protections, the transformation of a degraded post-industrial site into an attractive, sustainable neighborhood creates gentrification pressures. While current protections aim to prevent displacement, long-term pressures may eventually undermine affordability. The initiative's success in preventing gentrification over decades remains to be demonstrated.

**Limited Diversity of Housing Models:** While the initiative includes social housing, commercial buildings, and recreational facilities, the housing portfolio remains relatively limited. Broader application requires demonstrating viability across diverse housing types (owner-occupied, private rental, cooperative, etc.) and income levels.

**Data Privacy and Cybersecurity Concerns:** The sophisticated monitoring systems generating detailed energy consumption data raise privacy concerns. While current systems employ privacy protections, the long-term implications of continuous monitoring and data collection warrant ongoing scrutiny.

**Governance Complexity:** The multi-level governance structure (European, national, regional, local) creates coordination complexity. While this structure enables leveraging multiple governance levels' resources and authority, it also creates potential for delays, misalignment, and bureaucratic obstacles.

**Limited Long-Term Performance Data:** While the initiative has operated since October 2021, comprehensive long-term performance data demonstrating sustained benefits over decades remains limited. Questions about long-term technology durability, system reliability, and resident satisfaction over extended periods require continued monitoring.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

The Open Thor Living Lab demonstrates substantial commitment to energy justice, though questions remain about long-term outcomes:

**Positive Equity Dimensions:** The initiative's explicit focus on energy poverty reduction addresses a critical justice concern. Energy poverty—the inability to adequately heat or cool homes or power essential appliances—disproportionately affects vulnerable populations and contributes to health disparities, economic stress, and social exclusion. By reducing energy costs for social housing residents, the initiative directly advances energy justice.

The participatory governance structure and resident co-creation processes represent procedural justice—ensuring that affected populations participate in decisions affecting them rather than having solutions imposed top-down. This approach respects resident agency and incorporates local knowledge and preferences.

The initiative's focus on preventing displacement and maintaining social housing character addresses distributional justice concerns. By ensuring that benefits of neighborhood transformation accrue to existing residents rather than enabling gentrification, the initiative aims to prevent the common pattern where sustainability improvements lead to displacement of vulnerable populations.

**Remaining Justice Concerns:** Despite these positive dimensions, several justice questions warrant ongoing attention:

- **Long-Term Affordability:** Will affordability protections persist over decades as political priorities shift and market pressures intensify?
- **Access to Decision-Making:** While participatory processes exist, do residents possess genuine power to influence major decisions, or does power remain concentrated among researchers, companies, and government?
- **Technology Appropriateness:** Do residents genuinely prefer the technologies implemented, or do they reflect external experts' preferences?
- **Benefit Distribution:** While residents benefit from reduced energy costs, do other stakeholders (companies, researchers, government) capture disproportionate benefits?
- **Broader Population Impacts:** How do the initiative's impacts on property values and neighborhood transformation affect non-resident populations and the broader city?
