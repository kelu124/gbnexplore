# Executive summary## TRÆ – Tall Timber Office Building

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** TRÆ – Tall Timber Office Building
- **Alternative Names:** TRÆ; Denmark’s Tallest Timber Building
- **Location:** South Harbour (Sydhavnen), Aarhus, Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 56.1448° N, 10.2108° E (Kalkværksvej 1, 8000 Aarhus C)[^1]
- **Timeline:**  
  - Conception: 2018  
  - Design/Planning: 2018–2021  
  - Construction: 2022–2024  
  - Completion: 2024[^2][^3][^1]
- **Project Scale:**  
  - 3 buildings: two at 6 stories, one at 20 stories  
  - Total floor area: 14,850 m²[^2][^3][^1]  
  - Height: 78–82 meters (20 stories; tallest timber building in Denmark)[^4][^2][^3]
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - Developer: Kilden & Hindby, PFA Ejendomme  
  - Architect: Lendager  
  - Engineering: Artelia Denmark  
  - Main contractor: Kaj Ove Madsen  
  - Aarhus Municipality (active partner)[^2][^1][^5]
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - Private investment (PFA Ejendomme, Kilden & Hindby)  
  - Public support (Aarhus Municipality as land owner and facilitator)  
  - Grant: Climate Foundation (Klimafonden) – 5 million DKK for research and documentation of carbon impacts[^6][^5]

#### **Executive Summary**

TRÆ is a pioneering office development in Aarhus’s South Harbour district, setting a new benchmark for tall timber construction and circular building practices in Denmark and Europe. Rising 78 meters across 20 stories (with two adjacent 6-story buildings), TRÆ is the country’s tallest timber structure and acts as a “living lab” for sustainable construction. The project’s key innovation is the large-scale integration of mass timber alongside an extensive palette of recycled, upcycled, and bio-based materials. Reused wind turbine blades, recycled glass, and repurposed wood form critical building elements—including façade panels, windows, and interior floors—demonstrating the circular use of materials.

The initiative was made possible through an experimental regulatory approach, close public-private collaboration, and dedicated funding for research and documentation. Three floors are reserved for real-time material experimentation, providing valuable data on lower-carbon construction and informing future regulation. TRÆ’s ambition goes beyond lowering embodied carbon; it seeks to establish a replicable model for market acceptance of sustainable high-rises, foster innovation within the construction sector, and support local supply chains for reused materials.

TRÆ’s development is deeply embedded in Aarhus’s broader urban strategy for the South Harbour, an area being transformed from an industrial waterfront into a vibrant, inclusive neighborhood. Through its bold approach to regulatory flexibility, stakeholder engagement, and design innovation, TRÆ stands as a flagship for climate-friendly urban development and circular economy principles in the built environment[^6][^2][^5].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Aarhus, with a population of approximately 350,000, is Denmark’s second-largest city and a major university, cultural, and economic hub. The city has experienced rapid growth (4,000–5,000 new residents annually), driven by both national and international migration. Aarhus’s economic base is diversified, including education, technology, creative industries, and maritime sectors. The city emphasizes livability, sustainability, and inclusive urban development[^6].

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
The South Harbour (Sydhavnen) is a centrally located former industrial and port district adjacent to the main train station, historic city center, and key cultural institutions (e.g., DOKK1 library, ARoS Art Museum)[^6].  
- **Historical development:** Historically dominated by warehouses, rail infrastructure, and heavy industry, the area is now transitioning to mixed-use urban functions.  
- **Current land use:** An evolving mix of commercial, residential, creative industry, and public spaces.  
- **Demographics:** The district historically included socially vulnerable groups and low-income populations, but is now attracting creative professionals, students, and businesses as regeneration progresses[^6][^2].  
- **Infrastructure:** Excellent transportation links; active waterfront; limited green space; legacy environmental constraints from former uses.  
- **Environmental Conditions:** Proximity to water presents opportunities for blue-green infrastructure but also challenges for flood management and soil remediation.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
- High *embodied carbon* in conventional construction  
- Linear material flows with low reuse or recycling  
- Regulatory barriers to tall timber and experimental materials  
- Social and spatial fragmentation in the district  
- Lack of market precedent for large-scale sustainable office buildings in Denmark[^6][^2][^5]

**Triggering Events:**  
- Political prioritization of climate action and circular economy  
- Policy support for sustainable urban transformation of South Harbour  
- Availability of funding for innovative pilot projects (Klimafonden grant)[^6][^5]

**Stakeholder Landscape:**  
- **Municipality of Aarhus:** Land owner, regulator, and active development partner  
- **Private developers (PFA Ejendomme, Kilden & Hindby):** Investment and risk-taking in innovative construction  
- **Architects/engineers:** Pioneering technical solutions  
- **Tenants:** Major companies (MobilePay, Implement Consulting Group) seeking sustainable office space  
- **Community:** Ongoing engagement with existing residents, creative sector, and vulnerable groups[^6][^2]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**  
- Municipality as both land owner and planning authority, enabling integrated decision-making  
- Strategic urban development plans with explicit sustainability and social objectives[^6]

**Relevant Policies:**  
- City and national climate strategies (targeting carbon neutrality, circularity)  
- Building regulations (BR18), though these lag behind in accommodating upcycled materials[^6]
- District-level plans for South Harbour, emphasizing mixed-use, social inclusion, and environmental quality

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
- Special regulatory flexibility for pilot projects: later-than-usual design/material decisions permitted  
- Barriers: lack of Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) for many reused materials; regulatory uncertainty on fire, structural, and insurance standards for tall timber buildings[^6][^2][^5]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:**  
- Demonstrate feasibility and benefits of tall timber construction in commercial buildings  
- Significantly reduce embodied carbon and promote closed-loop material use  
- Generate open-source data on sustainable construction for market and policy learning  
- Pilot regulatory and procurement innovations for green building[^6][^2][^5]

**Vision Statement:**  
“To gain knowledge and know-how about sustainable solutions... accelerating the transition of climate change mitigation of the building industry in Aarhus.”  
—Bente Lykke Sørensen, City Strategic Chief, Aarhus Municipality[^2]

**Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
- Directly supports Aarhus’s climate neutrality and circular economy goals  
- Integral to South Harbour’s transformation as a model sustainable, inclusive neighborhood  
- Aligned with national ambitions for sustainable construction leadership[^6][^2]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:**  
By constructing a flagship tall timber office using upcycled materials and regulatory innovation, TRÆ will (a) reduce emissions, (b) provide replicable technical and market models, and (c) drive broader adoption of circular building practices[^6][^2][^5].

**Target Beneficiaries:**  
- Immediate: Office tenants, local construction sector, material suppliers  
- Broader: City of Aarhus, Danish building industry, future developments

**Innovation Elements:**  
- Mass timber structural system at unprecedented scale for Denmark  
- Use of upcycled materials (e.g., wind turbine blades, glass, wood) in primary building components  
- Real-time experimentation and feedback loops in design and construction  
- Active involvement of public authority in development process[^6][^2][^1][^5]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** City leadership on climate and sustainability; desire for international visibility[^6]
- **Economic:** Attracting business tenants committed to ESG; supporting new markets for recycled materials
- **Social:** Fostering inclusion, innovation, and a creative urban environment[^6][^2]
- **Environmental:** Reducing carbon emissions and landfill waste; climate adaptation
- **Technological:** Leveraging advanced timber engineering, testing protocols, and digital design tools[^2][^5]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**  
Rooted in *circular economy* and *sustainability-by-design*. Prioritized LCA (Life Cycle Assessment) and minimized embodied carbon from the outset. Emphasis on *urban place-making* and social integration[^6][^2].

**Physical/Spatial Design:**  
- Three buildings: main tower (20 stories), two smaller (6 stories each)  
- Mass timber as primary structural material  
- Integration of recycled/upcycled materials in facades, floors, interior finishes  
- Flexible floorplates, accessible public spaces, and shared amenities[^2][^1]

**Scope and Phasing:**  
- Multi-year design and regulatory process (2018–2021)  
- Construction (2022–2024), with iterative design and ongoing experimentation on three dedicated floors[^6][^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Mass timber construction:** Load-bearing glulam and CLT systems[^4][^2][^5]
- **Upcycled materials:**  
  - Recycled window frames for block flooring (1,500 m²)[^7]  
  - Repurposed wind turbine blades as façade solar screens (27x lower carbon than aluminum alternatives)[^1][^5]  
  - Recycled glass, wood, and bricks throughout the envelope and interiors[^2][^1]
- **Fire safety and structural testing:** Advanced simulation and real-world fire tests to satisfy regulatory requirements[^6][^5]
- **Design innovation:** Early integration of LCA, iterative feedback, mock-ups, robust digital modeling[^6][^2]

**Innovation Type:**  
- Technological (materials, structural systems)  
- Process (participatory design, real-time experimentation)  
- Governance (public-private regulatory partnership)

**Integration Approach:**  
Technologies and materials were tested and approved through close collaboration between design team, engineers, regulatory authorities, and material suppliers. Where possible, local supply chains were mobilized for recycled/upcycled components[^6][^2][^5].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:**  
- Competitive selection of lead architect (Lendager) and main contractor (Kaj Ove Madsen)  
- Involvement of specialized subcontractors for timber engineering and upcycling (e.g., Granslev Tømrer og Gulventreprise)[^1]

**Stakeholder Engagement:**  
- Three years of workshops, public meetings, and advisory boards involving residents, businesses, artists, and vulnerable groups during South Harbour planning[^6]
- Ongoing feedback loops with city architect, engineers, and tenants

**Participatory Elements:**  
- “Living lab” floors allow for user and expert input in material testing  
- Open-source reporting and dissemination of findings

**Project Management:**  
- Integrated project delivery model with city, developer, and design team closely aligned  
- Regular coordination meetings, mock-up reviews, and adaptive decision-making[^6][^2]

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:**  
- Not publicly disclosed in detail. Significant private investment, with public grant (5 million DKK) for research/demonstration[^6][^5]

**Funding Mix:**  
- Predominantly private (PFA Ejendomme, Kilden & Hindby)  
- Strategic public funding and land provision/discount  
- Klimafonden grant for experimental/knowledge-sharing components[^6][^5]

**Revenue Mechanisms:**  
- Lease to anchor tenants (MobilePay, Implement Consulting Group)[^6]  
- Potential for market premium on sustainable office space

**Financial Sustainability:**  
- Supported by strong tenant demand for green buildings  
- Model intended to prove economic viability for future replication[^2]

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Ensuring fire safety and structural integrity in a 20-story timber building (unprecedented scale in Denmark); lack of EPDs and standard LCA methods for upcycled materials; complex engineering of composite elements[^6][^2][^5]
- **Financial:** Higher upfront costs for innovation, testing, and regulatory navigation
- **Social/Political:** Overcoming skepticism from insurers, regulators, and parts of the construction sector; balancing district inclusiveness with large-scale development[^6][^2]
- **Administrative:** Intensive coordination between city departments, design team, and contractors; need for regulatory exceptions and new permitting processes
- **Environmental:** Managing embodied carbon in non-timber components (e.g., glass facades); limited benefits in current LCA frameworks for reused materials due to regulatory lag[^6]

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- Early, ongoing involvement of fire and structural experts; extensive fire and load testing  
- Special regulatory arrangement allowing late-stage material/design changes to enable experimentation[^6][^2]
- Mock-up construction and simulations for real-time learning and risk management  
- Documentation and open-source sharing to accelerate regulatory and market learning  
- Adjustments in design to optimize material use, minimize surface area, and select lowest-carbon suppliers[^6]

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- Construction of Denmark’s tallest timber building (20 stories, 78 meters), plus two 6-story buildings[^4][^2][^3][^1]
- 14,850 m² of high-quality office space[^2][^3][^1]
- Integration of recycled and upcycled materials in primary building components  
- Creation of “living lab” floors for ongoing experimentation and data generation[^6][^2]

#### **Social Impacts**

- Enhanced innovation culture in the local construction sector  
- Raised public and professional awareness of circular building approaches  
- Potential for broader acceptance of sustainable high-rise typologies  
- District-level improvements in inclusiveness and urban vibrancy[^6][^2]

#### **Economic Impacts**

- Job creation in advanced construction, material supply, and design  
- Strengthened local value chains for recycled/upcycled materials  
- Attraction of anchor tenants with ESG commitments (MobilePay, Implement Consulting Group)[^6][^2]
- Demonstrated market viability for sustainable office space

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- Significant reduction in embodied carbon compared to conventional construction (demonstrated through LCA; exact figures to be published)[^6][^2][^5]
- Diversion of waste streams (wind turbine blades, window frames, etc.) from landfill  
- Improved resource efficiency through circular material flows  
- Public documentation of carbon and resource savings to inform future policy[^6][^2][^5]

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- Creation of new models for public-private collaboration in urban development  
- Influence on national and local regulations for circular and timber construction  
- Increased transparency and learning through open-source reporting[^6][^2][^5]

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- Project achieved its primary goals: completed a landmark timber office building with extensive circular material use; generated new data and market precedent; and advanced regulatory innovation[^6][^2][^5]
- Some regulatory and LCA limitations remain for upcycled materials; ongoing advocacy required to update standards  
- Model widely recognized as replicable and scalable, with lessons for broader market and policy adoption[^2][^5]

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Landmark demonstration of tall timber and circular construction at scale  
- Deeply collaborative, with close alignment between public and private actors  
- Rigorous documentation and knowledge-sharing, enabling replication  
- Strong integration with citywide sustainability and inclusion goals[^6][^2][^5]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Continued regulatory lag in recognizing upcycled material benefits in LCA and permitting  
- Higher upfront costs and complexity relative to conventional construction  
- Uncertain market acceptance for some experimental materials over the long term[^6][^2]

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Project embedded in district strategy emphasizing social inclusion  
- Engagement with vulnerable and creative communities in planning process  
- Risk of gentrification exists as district transforms; ongoing monitoring needed[^6]

#### **Future Outlook**

- TRÆ expected to serve as a model for further tall timber and circular construction in Denmark and beyond  
- Open-source reporting will inform policy and market standards  
- Ongoing need to update regulatory frameworks to better support reuse/upcycling  
- Potential for further scaling as market and policy confidence in approach grows[^6][^2][^5]
