# Executive summary## RESPONSE Project – Positive Energy Neighbourhoods

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** RESPONSE Project – Positive Energy Neighbourhoods
- **Alternative Names:** integRatEd Solutions for POsitive eNergy and reSilient CitiEs (RESPONSE), Positive Energy Districts (PEDs) project
- **Location:** Dijon (Fontaine d’Ouche district), France; Turku (Student Village), Finland; with replication in Brussels, Zaragoza, Botosani, Ptolemaida, Gabrovo, Severodonetsk
- **Geographic Coordinates:**  
  - Dijon: 47.3220° N, 5.0415° E  
  - Turku: 60.4518° N, 22.2666° E
- **Timeline:**  
  - Start: October 2020  
  - Duration: 5 years (through 2025)  
  - Key milestones: Pilot phase in Dijon and Turku; Replication in six other European cities; Monitoring and post-project sustainability planning
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Dijon: Fontaine d’Ouche district (social housing, ~8,000 residents)  
  - Turku: Student Village (largest student housing complex in Finland)  
  - Total affected population: Approx. 10,000+ directly, broader impact via replication  
  - Investment value: €23.5 million (Horizon 2020 funding), €7.4 million assigned to Dijon[^1][^2][^3]
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - City of Dijon; Dijon Métropole; Grand Dijon Habitat; Bouygues Construction; City of Turku; Turku Student Village Foundation; Turku Energia; University of Turku; VTT; plus 50+ consortium partners across Europe[^1][^2]
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - European Union Horizon 2020 Smart Cities & Communities Programme  
  - Local government co-financing  
  - Private sector partners (e.g., Bouygues Construction)  
  - NGOs and research institutions[^1][^2][^3]

#### **Executive Summary**
RESPONSE is a flagship EU Horizon 2020 project pioneering Positive Energy Districts (PEDs)—urban neighbourhoods that generate more energy than they consume—through large-scale demonstration in Dijon (France) and Turku (Finland), and replication in six additional European cities. The project integrates advanced renewable energy systems (solar PV, geothermal), energy storage, digital citizen engagement platforms, smart meters, and innovative business models to achieve ambitious targets: 11.2 GWh/year of local renewable energy penetration, 3,090 MWh/year of energy savings, and a reduction of 9,799 tons CO₂eq/year in pilot districts[^4][^5][^1].

Dijon’s Fontaine d’Ouche PED stands out for applying these solutions in a socially deprived area with extensive social housing, while Turku’s Student Village demonstrates energy-positive retrofits in the Nordic context. RESPONSE is unique for its holistic approach—combining technological innovations with participatory governance, scenario-based policy tools, and real-time energy data feedback. The project addresses cross-sectoral coordination, legacy infrastructure integration, and citizen buy-in, with notable engagement activities such as living labs and co-creation workshops[^6][^7][^8].

The initiative’s outcomes include validated scalable PED concepts, replicable business models, policy guidance for sustainable urban transformation, and significant social, economic, and environmental co-benefits: empowering citizens, reducing energy poverty, stimulating local economies, and contributing to climate neutrality by 2050[^4][^9][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
- **Dijon:** Regional capital, population ~155,000; historical city with strong agri-food, logistics, and administrative sectors. Fontaine d’Ouche is a 1960s social housing district, diverse, lower-income, with significant immigrant populations and aging infrastructure[^2].
- **Turku:** Southwest Finland’s largest city, population ~195,000; university town, major port, high-tech and service sectors. Student Village houses ~7,000 students, representing a young, international, and mobile demographic[^1][^7].

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
- **Fontaine d’Ouche (Dijon):**  
  - Developed in the post-war period as a social housing area.  
  - Land use: Predominantly residential, high-density blocks, some commercial/services.  
  - Demographics: Majority low- to moderate-income, ethnically diverse.  
  - Infrastructure: Aging buildings, legacy heating networks, limited green spaces.  
  - Environmental: High energy consumption, urban heat island effects, air quality issues[^2].
- **Student Village (Turku):**  
  - Built from 1969, expanded over decades.  
  - Land use: Residential, student services, some retail.  
  - Demographics: Young adults, mix of Finnish and international students.  
  - Infrastructure: Mixed building ages, prior energy retrofits, district heating.  
  - Environmental: Cold climate, energy-intensive heating, opportunities for renewable integration[^1][^7].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**
- High carbon emissions from energy use.
- Legacy infrastructure incompatible with modern energy systems.
- Energy poverty (especially in Fontaine d’Ouche).
- Lack of citizen engagement in energy transition.
- Fragmented governance across energy, housing, and urban planning[^2][^9][^7].

**Triggering Events:**
- EU’s climate neutrality targets for 2050.
- Horizon 2020 Smart Cities & Communities funding call.
- Local policy shifts toward decarbonization in both cities.

**Stakeholder Landscape:**
- Municipal governments (Dijon Métropole, City of Turku).
- Housing authorities (Grand Dijon Habitat, Turku Student Village Foundation).
- Utility companies (Turku Energia, Groupe Coriance).
- Construction and technology firms (Bouygues, FAFCO, Onyx Solar).
- Research institutions and NGOs.
- Residents (tenants, local community groups)[^1][^2][^10].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**
- Local government leadership, with cross-sector consortia.
- EU-level oversight via Horizon 2020.

**Relevant Policies:**
- EU Green Deal and climate-neutrality roadmap.
- Local climate action plans (Dijon’s 2050 vision, Turku’s climate strategy).
- Urban planning regulations regarding building retrofits and renewable integration.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
- Supportive EU and national frameworks for energy innovation.
- Challenges: Regulatory hurdles for energy sharing, data privacy for digital platforms, legacy building codes.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:**
- Demonstrate and scale Positive Energy District concepts.
- Achieve net positive energy balance and CO₂ neutrality in pilot districts.
- Develop replicable business models and policy guidance for EU cities[^4][^5][^9].

**Vision Statement:**
“Smart Cities Energy Transition: Climate-neutral cities by 2050—resilient, safe, equitable, and sustainable urban neighbourhoods powered by citizens and renewables”[^9][^11].

**Alignment with Broader Plans:**
- Directly supports EU climate and energy targets.
- Integrates with city-level climate action plans and sustainable urban strategies.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:**
- Retrofit and equip buildings/districts with renewables, smart grids, and energy storage → reduce energy demand and emissions → empower citizens through digital tools and participatory governance → scale via business models and replication[^4][^5].

**Target Beneficiaries:**
- Residents (especially vulnerable groups in social housing).
- Municipalities (policy innovation, decarbonization).
- Utility and technology partners (market development).

**Innovation Elements:**
- PEDs in deprived urban districts (not only affluent new builds).
- Integration of real-time energy data, citizen feedback, and scenario-based policy tools.
- Living labs and co-creation for participatory governance[^8][^7].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** EU and local government climate commitments; visibility as “lighthouse” cities.
- **Economic:** Opportunities for local job creation, energy cost savings, and business innovation.
- **Social:** Addressing energy poverty, enhancing citizen agency, building community resilience.
- **Environmental:** Climate adaptation, air quality improvement, resource efficiency.
- **Technological:** Smart meters, energy management platforms, integration of renewables and storage, digital citizen engagement tools[^5][^1].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**
- PED Reference Framework: Annual net zero energy import, net zero CO₂ emissions, surplus renewable energy production.
- Active management of energy flows, integration with mobility, ICT, and urban systems[^12].

**Physical/Spatial Design:**
- Dijon: Deep renovation of social housing, installation of solar PV, upgrades to district heating, creation of energy storage facilities.
- Turku: Retrofitting student housing, deployment of solar PV and geothermal, integration with district heating, smart metering.

**Scope and Phasing:**
- Phase 1: Baseline studies and technical analysis.
- Phase 2: Deployment of integrated solutions in pilot districts.
- Phase 3: Monitoring, citizen engagement, business model development.
- Phase 4: Replication and roadmap dissemination[^6][^5][^1].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**
- Solar photovoltaic panels.
- Geothermal energy systems.
- Battery and thermal energy storage.
- Smart meters and building automation.
- Digital citizen engagement platforms (participatory governance, feedback loops).
- Scenario-based modeling tools for energy policy[^4][^1][^7].

**Innovation Type:**
- Technological (energy systems, automation).
- Social (citizen engagement, living labs).
- Financial (novel business models, cascade funding).
- Governance (integrated multi-level partnerships).

**Integration Approach:**
- Retrofit legacy infrastructure with new systems.
- Cross-sector coordination (energy, housing, ICT).
- Platforms for real-time data sharing and citizen participation[^6][^10].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:**
- Consortium-led competitive procurement.
- Engagement of local contractors and technology providers.

**Stakeholder Engagement:**
- Co-creation workshops, living labs, peer mentor programs.
- Tenant information events and participatory design.

**Participatory Elements:**
- Citizen platforms for feedback, energy data transparency, scenario exploration.

**Project Management:**
- Multi-partner coordination (municipal, private, NGO, academic).
- Monitoring via impact metrics and KPIs[^6][^8].

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:**
- Total investment: €23.5 million (EU Horizon 2020).
- Dijon share: €7.4 million.
- Component breakdown: Building retrofits, energy systems, digital platforms, engagement activities[^1][^2][^3].

**Funding Mix:**
- EU grant (majority), local government co-finance, private sector investment.

**Revenue Mechanisms:**
- Energy savings, local energy sales (self-consumption), potential new revenue streams from non-energy services[^10].

**Financial Sustainability:**
- Business model evaluation tools developed for long-term viability.
- Planning for post-project sustainability and market deployment[^10].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical:**
- Integration with legacy infrastructure (old buildings, district heating).
- Delays in asset deployment, especially in Dijon.
- Interoperability of ICT solutions[^6][^5].

**Financial:**
- Budget overruns and funding gaps.
- Risk of insufficient return on investment in deprived areas.

**Social and Political:**
- Achieving citizen buy-in, especially in vulnerable communities.
- Political coordination across sectors and cities.

**Administrative:**
- Bureaucratic delays in approvals and contracting.
- Coordination among 53 partners from 13 countries[^2].

**Environmental:**
- Unforeseen impacts from building retrofits (waste, construction emissions).
- Climate events (cold winters in Turku impacting system performance).

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:**
- Mitigation plans for delayed assets.
- Technical analysis of barriers to ICT solution replication.
- Enhanced citizen engagement (kids labs, peer mentors, co-creation).

**Design Modifications:**
- Adjusting deployment schedules and integration methods.
- Focused living labs to address community-specific concerns.

**Lessons Learned:**
- Early baseline studies are crucial.
- Engagement must be continuous and multi-channel.
- Flexibility in technical and governance approaches improves resilience[^6][^8].

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Holistic PED model:** Integration of technology, governance, and citizen participation.
- **Focus on Social Equity:** Implementation in deprived districts (Fontaine d’Ouche).
- **Scalability and Replication:** Validated roadmap and business models.
- **Quantifiable Impact:** Rigorous KPIs and monitoring[^4][^5][^1].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Delays and Technical Barriers:** Integration with old infrastructure challenging.
- **Funding Sustainability:** Long-term viability relies on continued investment.
- **Complex Consortium Management:** Coordination across 53 partners presents risks[^6][^2].

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Advances Equity:** Directly targets energy poverty and includes vulnerable groups.
- **Inclusive Development:** Participatory governance and co-creation activities.
- **Potential Gaps:** Need to ensure benefits reach all social groups and avoid unintended exclusion.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Long-term Trajectory:** Expansion to additional EU cities; continuous innovation.
- **Ongoing Challenges:** Sustaining engagement, scaling business models, adapting to evolving climate and technological conditions.
- **Next Phases:** Post-project sustainability planning, integration with broader smart city strategies, ongoing monitoring and improvement[^6][^10].
