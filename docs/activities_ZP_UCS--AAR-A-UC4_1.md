# Executive summary## CO-SHAPE Circular Energy Park & Governance Innovation

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** CO-SHAPE Circular Energy Park & Governance Innovation
- **Alternative Names:** CO-SHAPE, Co-Shaping Areas in Peri-Urban Environments, Energy Park Spørring
- **Location:** Spørring, peri-urban area, Aarhus Municipality, Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 56.301 N, 10.088 E (Spørring, Aarhus)
- **Timeline:** Launched 2023 (pilot phase); ongoing, with key milestones tied to the NetZeroCities Pilot Cities Programme and Aarhus’ 2030 climate neutrality goal[^1][^2]
- **Project Scale:** 1,000 hectares; impacts ~10% of Aarhus’ total emissions (100,000+ tCO₂eq annual reduction); pilot for citywide replication
- **Leading Organization(s):** Aarhus Municipality (lead), Aarhus School of Architecture, partners from NetZeroCities, private developers, landowners, and local citizens[^2][^1][^3]
- **Funding Sources:** Mix of municipal funding, EU-level innovation and climate programme support (NetZeroCities), and private investment[^1][^4]

#### **Executive Summary**
CO-SHAPE is a flagship green transformation initiative in Aarhus, Denmark, focusing on the peri-urban village of Spørring. The project aims to demonstrate how circular energy systems—integrating biogas, biochar, solar, Power-to-X, energy storage, and waste heat recovery—can be deployed at landscape scale to deliver systemic reductions in greenhouse gas emissions and foster resource circularity. What sets CO-SHAPE apart is its *co-creation governance model*: instead of traditional top-down planning, the project is structured around participatory “solution rooms” where municipal staff, citizens, landowners, and energy developers collaborate from the outset to shape infrastructure, land use, and operational models.

The initiative addresses not only technical and environmental challenges but also the “wicked problems” of stakeholder engagement, governance innovation, and social acceptance—barriers that often impede ambitious climate action. CO-SHAPE’s cross-sectoral, cross-departmental framework is embedded in Aarhus’ municipal climate strategy and is designed to be replicable, with a playbook and knowledge transfer mechanisms for other cities. By pioneering new approaches to participatory governance and demonstrating integrated circular energy at scale, CO-SHAPE aspires to accelerate Aarhus’ path to net zero, catalyze institutional change, and serve as a model for peri-urban green transformation across Europe[^2][^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Aarhus is Denmark’s second-largest city, with a population nearing 350,000[^2]. Its economy is anchored in education, research, clean tech, and logistics, with a vibrant university culture. The city is known for its high quality of life, strong climate ambitions (climate neutrality by 2030), and a landscape that blends urban, agricultural, and coastal environments[^1][^5].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** Spørring is a village on the northern peri-urban fringe of Aarhus. Historically rural with agricultural land, it is now at the interface of city expansion and regional land-use pressures. The area is characterized by:
  - **Historical development:** Predominantly agricultural, with incremental urban influence.
  - **Current land use:** Mixed farmland, scattered residential communities, emerging renewable energy installations[^1].
  - **Demographics:** Diverse, with a mix of local landowners, farmers, and new residents; income and age profiles are typical of peri-urban Northern Europe.
  - **Infrastructure:** Basic rural infrastructure, with limited but improving access to public amenities and transport.
  - **Environmental conditions:** Fertile agricultural soils, proximity to water bodies, and existing bioenergy facilities[^1].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - Fragmented land use and inefficient energy systems.
  - Local resistance (NIMBY) to new energy projects.
  - Lack of comprehensive governance tools for integrating energy, agriculture, and land use.
  - Insufficient cross-departmental capabilities within the municipality[^2][^1].
- **Triggering Events:**
  - Aarhus’ commitment to 2030 climate neutrality.
  - Recognition of the need for radical interventions and innovative governance approaches.
  - Previous research and pilot projects highlighting barriers to large-scale renewable energy siting[^2][^6].
- **Stakeholder Landscape:** Key actors included the municipality, local landowners and farmers, private energy developers, residents, and knowledge partners (Aarhus School of Architecture)[^1][^3].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** Led by the municipality, with strong cross-departmental integration and support from national and EU policy frameworks emphasizing climate neutrality and green innovation[^1][^4].
- **Relevant Policies:** Aarhus Climate Action Plan (CAP21-24), Danish national energy transition policies, EU Green Deal, and local zoning and land use regulations.
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** Supportive climate targets and funding, but complex land ownership, sectoral silos, and permitting processes presented barriers[^2].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:** 
  - Achieve large-scale, low-carbon, circular energy production integrated with sustainable land use.
  - Pioneer participatory governance for complex, multi-stakeholder spatial transformation[^2][^1].
- **Vision Statement:** To create a replicable model of circular, citizen-shaped energy landscapes that deliver climate, social, and economic benefits.
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** Directly supports Aarhus’ 2030 net zero target, aligns with Danish and EU sustainability strategies, and complements ongoing research in urban sustainability transitions[^2][^6][^4].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:** By embedding co-creation into planning and implementation, technical and social barriers to green infrastructure can be overcome, leading to more effective, equitable, and accepted climate solutions.
- **Target Beneficiaries:** Residents (quality of life, energy security, participation), local farmers and landowners (new income streams, soil quality), the municipality (climate targets), and broader society (replicable model for climate action).
- **Innovation Elements:** Solution room co-creation model; integrated, circular energy park; sector coupling; and cross-departmental municipal coordination[^1][^2].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Municipal leadership on climate, EU and national policy incentives[^1][^4].
- **Economic:** Attraction of green investment, maximizing land value, and supporting new business models (e.g., green fuels, bioproducts)[^2].
- **Social:** Citizen demand for meaningful involvement, trust building, and addressing NIMBYism.
- **Environmental:** Urgent climate action, improved resource efficiency, and soil carbon sequestration.
- **Technological:** Advances in Power-to-X, bioenergy, waste heat recovery, and energy storage[^1][^2].

---

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Integrated landscape and energy planning grounded in circular economy and participatory governance philosophies. Emphasis on holistic, cross-sectoral systems thinking[^2][^1].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** Development of a biogenic energy park (biogas, biochar, solar, Power-to-X, storage), reconfiguration of agricultural land uses, infrastructure for circular energy/resource flows[^1].
- **Scope and Phasing:** 
  - Initial pilot (1,000 hectares, Spørring)
  - Scenario-building and solution rooms
  - Comprehensive planning and knowledge transfer for scaling[^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - Biogas production (conversion of agricultural residues)
  - Pyrolysis for biochar (carbon sequestration, soil health)
  - Solar photovoltaics
  - Energy storage solutions
  - Power-to-X (using CO₂ and green hydrogen for e-fuels)
  - Waste heat recovery systems[^1]
- **Innovation Type:** Technological (energy systems, sector coupling), Social (co-creation governance), Governance (cross-departmental solution rooms), Process (scenario-based, participatory planning)[^2][^1].
- **Integration Approach:** Technologies are physically and operationally coupled; resource outputs of one process serve as inputs for another (e.g., biogas CO₂ for Power-to-X, biochar for fields)[^1].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Public-private partnerships; competitive and negotiated procurement for tech providers; partnership with research institutions[^2].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Iterative workshops, landscape scenario-building, and participatory solution rooms involving citizens, landowners, and developers from the outset[^2][^1].
- **Participatory Elements:** Co-creation embedded in all phases; direct negotiation and visualization of trade-offs among diverse interests.
- **Project Management:** Coordinated by municipal project team with cross-departmental oversight and external advisory from Aarhus School of Architecture[^2][^3].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Not publicly detailed; significant municipal and EU innovation funding; cost breakdown includes technology investment, land management, and participatory processes[^2].
- **Funding Mix:** Municipal budget, EU NetZeroCities Programme, private sector co-investment[^1][^4].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Anticipated from energy sales (biogas, e-fuels), carbon credits (biochar), and potential land value uplift.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Ongoing revenue from energy and bio-products, cost efficiencies from integrated systems, and long-term municipal-economic benefits[^1].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Complex integration of diverse energy systems; technology-market risk (esp. Power-to-X and e-fuels); infrastructure retrofitting[^1].
- **Financial:** Upfront investment needs; uncertainties in energy markets and carbon credit pricing.
- **Social and Political:** Overcoming NIMBYism; building trust among historically skeptical residents; balancing conflicting land uses[^1].
- **Administrative:** Navigating sectoral silos within municipal government; regulatory complexity.
- **Environmental:** Managing potential local impacts (noise, odour, visual); ensuring holistic environmental assessment.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** “Solution rooms” as safe spaces for multi-stakeholder negotiation; scenario-building to visualize trade-offs; incremental piloting with feedback loops[^1][^2].
- **Design Modifications:** Continuous adaptation of technology mix and siting based on stakeholder input.
- **Lessons Learned:** Co-creation is resource-intensive but essential for social acceptance; cross-departmental working requires cultural change; risk-taking is necessary for true innovation[^1].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** Establishment of a circular energy park (multiple energy installations); new/retrofitted land uses; creation of participatory planning platforms.
- **Service Delivery:** Supply of renewable energy (biogas, electricity, e-fuels); improved soil carbon management; participatory planning services for residents.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** >100,000 tonnes CO₂eq avoided annually (approx. 10% of city emissions); 1,000 hectares transformed[^1].

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Stronger civic engagement; increased public trust in planning; reduced opposition to green infrastructure[^2].
- **Equity Outcomes:** Participatory design ensures broad inclusion, though balancing interests remains challenging; no major displacement reported.
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** Not a major issue due to rural/peri-urban context.
- **Social Capital:** Enhanced networks between municipality, residents, and developers; institutionalization of co-creation[^1].
- **Cultural Impacts:** Building a culture of shared stewardship over landscape and climate assets.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Direct employment in construction, energy operations, and planning; indirect effects in agriculture and maintenance.
- **Economic Activity:** Attraction of green investment; new revenue streams for farmers (biomass, grass protein, biochar); support for local businesses.
- **Property Values:** Potential for uplift due to improved land use and ecosystem services.
- **Cost Savings:** Efficiency gains in energy and resource management.
- **Economic Inclusion:** Focus on ensuring benefits reach both landowners and wider community[^1].

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Large-scale GHG reduction; accelerated progress toward municipal net-zero goals[^1].
- **Resource Management:** Closed-loop flows for energy, carbon, nutrients; improved soil health.
- **Environmental Quality:** Anticipated improvements in biodiversity, air quality, and water management.
- **Waste and Pollution:** Significant reduction in agricultural and organic waste; conversion to valuable products.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Performance measured against city, national, and EU standards; playbook for knowledge transfer[^2][^1].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Strengthening of cross-sectoral municipal teams; development of replicable governance frameworks.
- **Policy Influence:** Embedding co-creation as a requirement for new renewable energy projects; influence on citywide and potentially national policy[^1].
- **Transparency and Accountability:** High levels of process transparency; documented participatory outcomes.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** Early evidence of social acceptance, institutional change, and technical feasibility; quantitative GHG reductions underway[^1].
- **Unintended Consequences:** Some delays and complexity from intensive stakeholder processes; need for ongoing risk and expectation management.
- **Long-term Viability:** Embedded in municipal strategy; revenue and institutional models support persistence post-pilot.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:** Success depends on local capacity for cross-sectoral cooperation, willingness of stakeholders, and supportive policy environment[^2].
- **Critical Success Factors:** Strong political leadership, dedicated resources for engagement, and clear climate targets.
- **Scalability:** Designed as a pilot for citywide and European replication; playbook and scenario-building tools facilitate transfer[^2][^1].

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:** Shares features with other European climate-neutral cities (e.g., Amsterdam, Vienna) but is unique in scale and co-creation depth[^1].
- **Best Practices:** Early, deep stakeholder involvement; integration of technological and governance innovation; cross-sectoral capacity-building.
- **Cautionary Lessons:** Co-creation requires patience, resources, and ongoing adaptation; not all cities may have the same enabling conditions.

#### **Parallels to [Your Specific Use Case]**

- **Contextual Similarities:** Peri-urban transformation, renewable energy integration, complex stakeholder environment.
- **Methodological Parallels:** Participatory planning, scenario-building, and solution rooms.
- **Technological Overlaps:** Circular energy systems, sector coupling, Power-to-X.
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Cross-sectoral governance, citizen and landowner engagement.
- **Transferable Strategies:** Co-creation methodologies, governance innovation, and comprehensive planning playbooks.
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Must tailor engagement and governance to local institutional culture and capacity.

---

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Bold integration of circular energy and land use at landscape scale.
- Pioneering co-creation governance model with demonstrated improvements in public trust and institutional innovation[^1][^2].
- Quantifiable climate impact and clear path to replication.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- High resource and time demands for participatory processes.
- Some risk of stakeholder fatigue or decision-making deadlock.
- Market and technological uncertainties for Power-to-X and other advanced systems.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Strong focus on inclusion and local buy-in; mitigation of NIMBY risks.
- Ongoing need to ensure benefits are equitably distributed, especially as value of land and resources change.

#### **Future Outlook**

- Embedded in city climate strategy, with intention to scale citywide and transfer to other European cities[^1][^2].
- Ongoing adaptation required as technologies, markets, and institutional relationships evolve.
- Next phases likely to focus on knowledge transfer, further technological integration, and refinement of governance models.
