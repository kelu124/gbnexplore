# Executive summary## Spørring Circular Energy Park (CO-SHAPE Project)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Spørring Circular Energy Park (CO-SHAPE Project)
- **Alternative Names:** CO-SHAPE; Energy Park Spørring; Co-Shaping Areas in Peri-Urban Environments
- **Location:** Spørring, Aarhus Municipality, Central Denmark Region, Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 56.3400° N, 10.0667° E (Spørring village)
- **Timeline:**  
  - Initial planning and concept: 2020–2021  
  - Pilot project launch: 2022  
  - Major expansion (biogas plant, solar park): 2023  
  - Ongoing: scheduled to inform Aarhus’s climate neutrality pathway through at least 2030
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Area: ~1,000 hectares peri-urban and rural land  
  - Population directly affected: ~1,500 (Spørring), indirectly: the wider Aarhus municipality (349,000+ residents)
  - Investment: Not fully disclosed; includes major public and private investments in infrastructure and renewable energy
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - Aarhus Municipality (Technical and Environmental Administration)
  - Nature Energy (biogas operator)
  - Aarhus School of Architecture (research/academic partner)
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - Public funds (Aarhus Municipality, Climate Action Plan budgets)
  - Private investment (Nature Energy, Shell plc subsidiary)
  - EU Pilot Cities Programme (NetZeroCities)
  - Potential national climate and innovation funds

#### **Executive Summary**

The Spørring Circular Energy Park, developed under the CO-SHAPE project, is a pioneering peri-urban transformation in Aarhus, Denmark, designed to demonstrate how integrated renewable energy systems and circular resource management can drive both climate action and holistic rural-urban development. Centered on the expansion of the Bånlev Biogas plant, the initiative incorporates a diverse cluster of green technologies, including biogas, solar power, pyrolysis for biochar, energy storage, and Power-to-X—thus enabling the production of green hydrogen and e-fuels. The park is intended to annually reduce over 100,000 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent, a significant contribution toward Aarhus’s ambitious goal of climate neutrality by 2030.

Beyond its technological innovation, Spørring Circular Energy Park is distinguished by its governance model: it is co-created with local citizens, energy developers, and authorities through participatory planning, scenario-building, and solution rooms for collaborative decision-making. This approach aims to overcome historic resistance to siting energy infrastructure near communities and to resolve trade-offs between social, environmental, and economic objectives. The project is both a test bed for institutional change and a replicable model for similar peri-urban contexts across Europe, generating new knowledge on comprehensive planning, participatory democracy, and cross-sectoral governance. It offers substantial social (empowerment, jobs), economic (investment, local development), and environmental (emissions reduction, soil health) benefits, while also surfacing the complexities of multi-stakeholder climate transitions[^1][^2][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Aarhus is Denmark’s second-largest city, with ~349,000 inhabitants. Its economic base is diverse—anchored by a strong university and research sector, manufacturing, logistics, and green innovation. The city is renowned for its quality of life, with extensive natural amenities and a dynamic cultural scene. Aarhus is a regional hub, combining urban vibrancy with proximity to forests, farmland, and the North Sea coast[^1][^2].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  Spørring is a small village located on the northern peri-urban fringe of Aarhus. Historically rural, it features a mix of agricultural land, scattered housing, and small-scale businesses. The demographic profile skews toward families and older residents, with moderate income levels and a strong local identity. Basic infrastructure is present, but major energy and industrial facilities are new arrivals. The area’s landscape and adjacent green spaces are highly valued, but the proximity to Aarhus means increasing exposure to urban pressures and opportunities[^1][^3].

- **Environmental Conditions:**  
  The peri-urban zone includes fertile soils, significant agricultural activity, and some vulnerability to land-use change. Key environmental constraints are the need to preserve biodiversity, manage agricultural runoff, and maintain landscape quality while expanding renewable energy infrastructure[^1][^2].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - High greenhouse gas emissions, especially from agriculture and fossil energy use
  - Aarhus’s commitment to climate neutrality by 2030, requiring radical emissions cuts
  - Limited local governance tools for integrated, cross-sectoral energy planning
  - Strong local resistance (“NIMBY”) to new energy infrastructure
  - Insufficient mechanisms for meaningful citizen engagement and cross-departmental coordination[^1][^3][^4]

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Aarhus’s adoption of a Climate Action Plan (2021–2024) with explicit targets for alternative energy and circular resource use
  - Nature Energy’s proposal to expand the Bånlev Biogas plant (2020)
  - EU selection of Aarhus as a NetZeroCities Pilot City, catalyzing innovation in governance and planning[^2][^3][^5]

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Aarhus Municipality (technical, planning, climate, and citizen engagement departments)
  - Nature Energy (plant operator/investor)
  - Local citizens and Spørring Citizens’ Association
  - Aarhus School of Architecture (research, scenario planning)
  - Public hearing participants, local businesses, farmers, and environmental groups[^1][^3][^6]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Led by Aarhus Municipality’s Technical and Environmental Authority, with oversight from an elected city council member
  - Multi-departmental leadership: planning, climate, citizen engagement[^3]
  - Formal public hearing requirements under the Danish Planning Act
  - Integration with citywide Climate Action Plan and national/EU climate targets[^3][^6]

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Aarhus Climate Action Plan 2021–2024, targeting sectoral shifts in energy and land use
  - Danish and EU policies on renewable energy, circular economy, and participatory governance
  - Pilot Cities Programme (NetZeroCities), supporting methodological innovation[^1][^2][^5]

- **Legal/Institutional Enablers and Barriers:**  
  - Mandatory public consultation and environmental impact assessment for major infrastructure
  - Challenges: legal limits on visual/landscape mitigation measures, need to reconcile multiple regulatory frameworks, and initial lack of cross-departmental tools for comprehensive planning[^3]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Enable a radical reduction in GHG emissions through integrated renewable energy production and circular resource management
  - Institutionalize participatory governance and co-creation in spatial planning and energy transitions
  - Serve as a replicable model for peri-urban energy transformation in Denmark and Europe[^1][^2]

- **Vision Statement:**  
  “Transform Spørring into a green innovation hub, demonstrating how circular energy parks and participatory governance can drive climate neutrality, economic vitality, and community empowerment in peri-urban contexts.”[^2]

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Directly supports Aarhus’s 2030 climate neutrality ambition
  - Contributes to Denmark’s national climate goals and the EU’s NetZeroCities and climate-neutral/smart cities missions[^2][^5]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  By engaging all stakeholders in co-creation, and integrating a suite of green technologies, Spørring Circular Energy Park can overcome resistance, align diverse interests, and deliver systemic emissions reductions, improved resource use, and economic/social benefits[^1][^2][^3].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Local residents (improved environment, economic opportunities, participatory power)
  - Aarhus Municipality (policy innovation, climate targets)
  - Local businesses and farmers (new markets, resources)
  - Wider city/region (model for expansion)[^2][^3]

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Integration of biogas, pyrolysis, solar, storage, Power-to-X, and circular agriculture
  - “Solution rooms” and collaborative governance mechanisms
  - Playbook for comprehensive, transferable planning[^1][^2]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  - City council and mayoral commitment to climate neutrality and participatory planning
  - Need for public legitimacy and local buy-in for energy transition[^2][^3]

- **Economic:**  
  - Attracting investment (Nature Energy, EU funds)
  - Generating local jobs, new business opportunities, and rural-urban synergies[^2]

- **Social:**  
  - Addressing historic distrust and exclusion from planning
  - Building social capital and community resilience[^2][^3]

- **Environmental:**  
  - Urgency of climate action, adaptation, and soil/water quality improvements
  - National/EU sustainability agenda[^2][^3][^4]

- **Technological:**  
  - Maturation of Power-to-X, biochar, and integrated renewable energy systems
  - Digital/monitoring tools for participation and system management[^1][^2]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Principles: circular economy, sector coupling (energy, agriculture, waste), landscape architecture, and participatory democracy[^1][^2][^6]
  - Differentiated urban typology approach for tailoring interventions to peri-urban context[^1]

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Cluster of renewable energy facilities: biogas plant, solar park, pyrolysis (biochar), storage, Power-to-X (hydrogen, e-fuels), waste heat recovery
  - Spatial integration with agriculture and natural landscape over 1,000 ha[^1][^2][^3]
  - Buffer zones and landscape screening to minimize local impacts[^3]

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Phase 1 (2020–2022): Planning, public consultation, initial design
  - Phase 2 (2022–2023): Expansion of Bånlev Biogas, solar park, co-creation workshops
  - Ongoing: Integration of further technologies (Power-to-X), knowledge transfer, and institutional adaptation[^1][^2][^3]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Biogas production (conversion of agricultural residues)
  - Pyrolysis for biochar (carbon sequestration, soil health)
  - Solar photovoltaics
  - Energy storage (electrochemical, thermal)
  - Power-to-X (hydrogen, e-fuels for aviation/shipping)
  - Waste heat capture and reuse
  - Digital monitoring and participatory feedback tools[^2][^3]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (integrated renewables, Power-to-X)
  - Social (co-creation, participatory governance)
  - Financial (public-private partnerships)
  - Process (solution rooms, scenario-building)[^1][^2]

- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Systemic sector coupling: outputs from one plant (e.g., CO₂, heat) feed inputs for another
  - Scenario-based spatial planning to harmonize infrastructure, agriculture, and landscape[^1][^2]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Nature Energy (private operator) selected for biogas expansion
  - Other technology providers involved through competitive tendering and partnerships[^3]

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Public hearings (statutory requirement)
  - Regular citizen meetings and workshops
  - Scenario-building, citizen association dialogue, solution rooms for conflict resolution[^1][^2][^3]

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Co-design of project features (e.g., vegetation, visual screening)
  - Direct feedback channels and real-time alerts regarding plant operations[^3]
  - Collaborative decision-making for spatial/operational changes

- **Project Management:**  
  - Led by Aarhus Municipality’s planning and climate departments, with weekly coordination across sub-units
  - Joint leadership between public sector, private companies, and citizen groups[^3][^6]

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Total investment not fully disclosed; biogas plant expansion alone entailed significant multi-million-euro costs[^3]
  - EU and city funds supplemented by private investment

- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Public (municipal, EU Pilot Cities)
  - Private (Nature Energy/Shell)
  - No direct user fees; revenue from energy/biogas sales

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Biogas sold to national grid or industry users
  - Potential revenue from green hydrogen/e-fuels
  - Cost savings from resource efficiency

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Anchored in ongoing market demand for biogas and green energy
  - Institutionalized within long-term municipal and national climate plans[^3][^5]

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Scaling up integrated renewables and Power-to-X at peri-urban scale
  - Managing engineering constraints and grid integration[^2][^3]

- **Financial:**  
  - Ensuring sufficient and stable investment, avoiding cost overruns[^3]

- **Social and Political:**  
  - Community opposition due to odor, traffic, and landscape impacts from biogas plant
  - Historic distrust due to lack of previous engagement
  - Negotiating trade-offs among social, environmental, and economic interests (“win-lose” scenarios)[^2][^3]

- **Administrative:**  
  - Fragmented municipal departments, lack of cross-sectoral planning tools
  - Need to coordinate multiple actors with differing priorities[^1][^3]

- **Environmental:**  
  - Potential negative impacts (e.g., odor, visual intrusion, increased traffic)
  - Balancing renewable infrastructure with landscape preservation[^3]

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Enhanced and continuous citizen engagement post-initial opposition
  - Solution rooms for collaborative problem-solving and real-time feedback[^3]

- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Additional landscaping and vegetation buffers at citizen request
  - Technical upgrades to minimize odor and noise
  - Adjusted transport logistics to reduce local congestion[^3]

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Early, meaningful engagement is essential
  - Transparent sharing of information and responsiveness to community concerns increases support
  - Institutional change (cross-departmental coordination) is both necessary and challenging[^2][^3]

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - Expanded biogas plant (Bånlev Biogas): capacity increased from 185,000 to 700,000 tons of biomass/year
  - New solar park and supporting energy infrastructure
  - Initiation of Power-to-X pilot components
  - Enhanced green buffers and public spaces[^2][^3]

- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Additional renewable energy supplied to the grid
  - New channels for participatory planning and local feedback
  - Knowledge transfer to other municipalities[^1][^2]

- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - >100,000 tonnes CO₂-equivalent avoided annually (estimated)[^2]
  - 1,000 hectares of peri-urban land transformed

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Empowerment through co-creation and improved access to decision-making
  - Enhanced local trust and satisfaction (85% satisfaction rate among engaged stakeholders)[^1][^3]

- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - Deliberate efforts to include diverse voices and mitigate negative impacts
  - No evidence of gentrification or displacement; focus on retaining local character[^3]

- **Social Capital:**  
  - Strengthened networks between municipality, businesses, and citizens
  - New “Aarhus Model” for citizen engagement under development[^3]

- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Project design respects and incorporates landscape and local identity

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  - Construction and ongoing operation of energy facilities created new jobs
  - Ancillary employment in agriculture, landscaping, and maintenance[^2][^3]

- **Economic Activity:**  
  - Attraction of private investment (Nature Energy/Shell)
  - Local business opportunities in supply chains and service provision

- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Reduced fossil fuel dependency for local industry and municipality
  - Operational efficiency gains from circular resource use

- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - Efforts to ensure benefits are shared among local residents and businesses

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - Over 100,000 tonnes CO₂-equivalent reduced annually (>10% of city emissions)[^2]
  - Potential for further reductions as Power-to-X matures

- **Resource Management:**  
  - Circular use of agricultural residues, waste heat, and biochar
  - Improved local soil health and carbon sequestration

- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - Monitoring and mitigation of odor, noise, and visual impacts
  - Maintenance and enhancement of green spaces

- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - Significant diversion of organic waste from traditional disposal
  - Production of biochar to sequester carbon and reduce fertilizer runoff

- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - Alignment with EU/Denmark environmental standards and city’s climate neutrality targets

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Strengthened cross-departmental coordination within municipality
  - New collaborative mechanisms (solution rooms, playbooks)[^1][^2]

- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Aarhus now mandates co-creation in renewable energy planning
  - Model influencing regional and national policy debates[^2][^5]

- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - Improved through ongoing public consultation and feedback channels

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  - Major progress toward climate neutrality and participatory governance objectives
  - Replicable model established, with knowledge shared widely

- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Some lingering local concerns (traffic, landscape change)
  - Institutional change slower and more complex than anticipated

- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Embedded in city and national strategies; financial and institutional sustainability supported by ongoing demand and policy frameworks

  - Early and sustained stakeholder engagement

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Holistic integration of cutting-edge green technologies and circular resource management
- Robust commitment to participatory planning and governance innovation
- Tangible contributions to climate neutrality and local economic development
- Creation of transferable knowledge and tools for other cities[^1][^2]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Initial rounds of citizen engagement were insufficient, leading to opposition and delays
- Some local concerns (odor, traffic, landscape) remain only partially resolved
- Complexity of institutional transformation slowed progress and strained resources[^3]

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Strong focus on inclusive participation and transparent decision-making
- Deliberate attempts to avoid displacement or gentrification
- Continuous adaptation of engagement processes to reach underrepresented groups[^2][^3]

#### **Future Outlook**

- Continued integration of new renewable technologies (advanced Power-to-X, digital management)
- Ongoing evolution of participatory governance, with lessons feeding into citywide “Aarhus Model”
- Potential to serve as a national and European benchmark for peri-urban climate transitions
- Remaining challenges: sustaining community trust, resolving residual local impacts, and scaling institutional change for broader application[^1][^2][^3]
