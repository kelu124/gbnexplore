# Executive summary## Be.SHARE – Brussels Sustainable Heating and Renewable Energy Community

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Be.SHARE – Brussels Sustainable Heating and Renewable Energy Community
- **Alternative Names:** Be.Share, Brussels Carbon Neutral Heating and Cooling Network (CNHCN)
- **Location:** North District (Northern Quarter), Brussels, Brussels-Capital Region, Belgium
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 50.862°N, 4.357°E (Brussels North District)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Conception and planning: 2022–2024
  - Start of works: late 2024
  - Initial operation: mid-2028 (planned)[^1]
  - Ongoing expansion and evaluation thereafter
- **Project Scale:**
  - Initial phase: 3 office buildings, 2 residential buildings (Le Foyer Laekenois housing association)
  - Population affected: 331 low-income families in the first phase[^2]
  - Ambition for district-wide and regional expansion in future phases
  - Investment: €5 million EU co-financing in initial phase[^2]
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - Public: Bruxelles Environnement (Regional Environment Agency), Sibelga (energy network operator), Vivaqua (water company)
  - Private: Karno (company), VUB (Vrije Universiteit Brussel, academic partner)
  - Civic: Convivence (non-profit), Le Foyer Laekenois (social housing)
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - European Union (EU co-financing)
  - Brussels-Capital Region
  - Public-private-community partnership (PPC governance model)[^3]
  - Potential for future private investment as the network expands

#### **Executive Summary**
Be.SHARE is a pioneering urban energy initiative in the dense North District of Brussels, aiming to demonstrate and operationalize a **Carbon Neutral Heating and Cooling Network (CNHCN)**. The project targets an area marked by stark contrasts: high-rise office towers sit alongside fragile neighborhoods with a high proportion of low-income and social housing residents. Most buildings in the area currently rely on fossil gas, resulting in poor air quality and high greenhouse gas emissions. Be.SHARE addresses these issues through an innovative blend of advanced technology, inclusive governance, and social engagement.

The core of Be.SHARE is a **smart, low-temperature thermal grid** that leverages **local renewable energy sources**—notably, geothermal heat harvested beneath public spaces and heat recovery from wastewater (riothermal technology). The network is designed to balance and directly exchange energy flows between office users (with high cooling needs) and residential users (with dominant heating needs), optimizing overall efficiency and maximizing renewable energy integration through real-time data analytics and predictive management tools.

A unique **Public-Private-Community (PPC) governance model** ensures that both large real estate interests and vulnerable residents have a stake in decision-making, ownership, and the benefits of the system. The project’s digital platform aggregates technical and social data, supports scenario testing for investments, and provides transparent user dashboards. Be.SHARE is explicitly aligned with Brussels’ decarbonization and climate justice strategies, with a strong focus on reducing energy poverty, fostering local economic development, and scaling a replicable model for other European cities. Its anticipated impacts include significant emissions reductions, improved air quality, and enhanced citizen participation in the energy transition[^3][^4][^2][^1][^5].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**
- **Brussels** is the capital of Belgium and the de facto capital of the European Union, with a population of over 1.2 million in the metropolitan area.
- Its economy is service-oriented, with finance, administration, and international organizations (notably the EU) as dominant sectors.
- The city is characterized by high population density, mixed urban fabric, and significant socio-economic disparities.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
- **North District (Northern Quarter):**
  - Historically, a business and administrative enclave with high-rise glass office towers.
  - Adjacent neighborhoods are home to **fragile populations**: 54% social housing (vs. 7% regional average), average income below the 10th percentile[^3].
  - Land use: Mix of commercial, residential, and public spaces; limited access to private land for energy infrastructure.
  - Infrastructure: Aging building stock, much of it reliant on fossil gas, limited renewable integration.
  - Environmental: Poor air quality, urban heat island effects, and limited green space.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High local air pollution and GHG emissions from fossil-fueled heating[^3][^2].
  - Structural energy poverty, especially among low-income/social housing residents.
  - Physical and ownership constraints preventing individual buildings from deploying renewables.
  - Underutilization of urban waste heat resources (e.g., wastewater).
  - Disconnection between business and residential energy needs, leading to inefficiency.
- **Triggering Events:**
  - Regional decarbonization targets for 2050.
  - Revision of the Air-Climate-Energy Plan and joint stakeholder vision (2023–24)[^2].
  - EU policy and funding incentives for energy transition.
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - Public authorities (regional and city), social housing providers, real estate firms, utility operators, local NGOs, academic institutions, and residents (esp. low-income groups).

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**
  - Brussels-Capital Region leads climate and energy policy through agencies like Bruxelles Environnement.
  - Strong tradition of public-private collaboration in energy and urban planning.
- **Relevant Policies:**
  - Brussels’ Air-Climate-Energy Plan
  - Regional building energy performance regulations (OPEB Order, passive house mandates[^6])
  - EU directives on energy efficiency and renewable energy integration
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
  - Regulatory push for fossil fuel phase-out in new/renovated buildings.
  - Legal frameworks enabling energy sharing and community energy models.
  - Institutional inertia in established utilities; challenges aligning diverse stakeholder interests[^7].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Decarbonize heating and cooling in the North District.
  - Reduce energy poverty and improve air quality.
  - Foster a replicable, community-involved model for urban energy transition.
- **Vision Statement:**
  - “Pooling energy resources to accelerate a fair and efficient transition” toward a **carbon-neutral, inclusive urban energy system**[^2].
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**
  - Directly supports Brussels’ 2050 carbon neutrality target and climate adaptation strategies.
  - Integrates with redevelopment and social inclusion initiatives in the district.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**
  - By creating a shared, smart, renewably powered thermal grid, the initiative enables efficient energy use, equitable access, and mutual benefits for both office and residential users—addressing both technical and social barriers to decarbonization[^3].
- **Target Beneficiaries:**
  - Low-income households, social housing tenants, office occupants, local businesses, and the broader urban community.
- **Innovation Elements:**
  - First-in-region **low-temperature, carbon-neutral district heating/cooling grid**.
  - Direct energy exchange between buildings with complementary needs.
  - Digital platform for real-time analytics, scenario modeling, and community engagement.
  - PPC governance model for shared ownership and decision-making.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political Drivers:** Regional climate leadership, citizen demand for clean air, EU policy alignment, energy justice concerns.
- **Economic Drivers:** Potential for job creation, investment in local infrastructure, cost savings for residents and businesses.
- **Social Drivers:** Energy poverty alleviation, participatory governance, community empowerment.
- **Environmental Drivers:** GHG mitigation, urban resilience, local resource utilization.
- **Technological Drivers:** Maturation of smart grid, IoT, geothermal, and heat recovery technologies.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** 
  - Place-based energy justice, participatory governance, and smart city integration.
  - Emphasis on **collective energy infrastructures** vs. fragmented, building-by-building retrofits.
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**
  - Installation of a **low-temperature district heating/cooling network** using pipes beneath public spaces (parks, roads).
  - Connection of both office towers and residential buildings to shared energy sources and storage.
  - Integration with redevelopment of Maximilian Park (key geothermal site)[^2].
- **Scope and Phasing:**
  - Initial phase: Five buildings (three offices, two residential/social housing).
  - Expansion to additional buildings and neighborhoods as feasibility proven.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Smart thermal grid:** Digitally monitored and controlled district network.
  - **IoT sensors:** Real-time monitoring of temperature, flow, and energy use.
  - **User dashboards:** Transparency and engagement for residents and building managers.
  - **Geothermal and riothermal (wastewater heat recovery):** Local renewable heat sources.
  - **Predictive analytics:** Optimize supply/demand balancing and scenario testing for future investments.
- **Innovation Type:**
  - Technological (grid, analytics), social (PPC governance), financial (community investment mechanisms), process (co-design and participatory engagement).
- **Integration Approach:**
  - Overlay on existing infrastructure, leveraging public rights-of-way.
  - Digital systems interoperable with building management and city-level platforms.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Consortium-based selection; public and private partners contracted for different components.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** 
  - Direct involvement of 331 low-income families in co-design.
  - Workshops, participatory planning sessions, and digital feedback tools.
- **Participatory Elements:** 
  - Opportunities for residents and businesses to shape service design, investment priorities, and governance.
- **Project Management:** Coordinated by Bruxelles Environnement, with roles distributed by function (technical, community, academic, commercial).

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** 
  - €5 million EU co-financing for first phase; additional regional and private contributions.
- **Funding Mix:** 
  - Public (EU, regional), private (real estate, tech partners), community (potential for future cooperative models).
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** 
  - User fees for energy services; potential for reinvestment of savings in community projects.
- **Financial Sustainability:** 
  - Business case modeling (ROI), scenario testing for investment justification, and gradual scaling for economies of scale.

---

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** 
  - Retrofitting old, diverse building stock to new grid.
  - Managing complex energy flows and ensuring reliability.
  - Integrating new renewables in dense, built-up areas with limited private land[^3][^7].
- **Financial:** 
  - High upfront investment; need for long-term cost recovery.
  - Risk of cost overruns in complex urban environments.
- **Social and Political:** 
  - Ensuring equitable access, avoiding technology-driven exclusion.
  - Overcoming skepticism or resistance among residents/businesses.
- **Administrative:** 
  - Multi-actor coordination; aligning public, private, and civic interests.
- **Environmental:** 
  - Minimizing construction disruption and potential negative impacts during installation.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**
  - Early and ongoing co-design with end-users to build trust and tailor solutions.
  - Flexible phasing to manage risk and incorporate lessons learned.
- **Design Modifications:** 
  - Scenario testing to adapt grid size, routing, and service offerings to evolving needs.
- **Lessons Learned:**
  - Importance of transparent communication and capacity-building for all stakeholders.
  - Need for robust digital infrastructure for monitoring and rapid troubleshooting.

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** 
  - Installation of low-temperature heating/cooling network for five pilot buildings.
  - Geothermal and wastewater heat recovery installations.
- **Service Delivery:** 
  - Reliable, lower-carbon heating and cooling for initial end-users.
  - Digital dashboards and analytics for participating buildings.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** 
  - 331 low-income families served in first phase[^2].
  - Ambition to scale to thousands as network expands.

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** 
  - Reduced energy costs and improved comfort for vulnerable residents.
  - Enhanced participation in city energy governance.
- **Equity Outcomes:** 
  - Prioritization of social housing; explicit aim to cut **energy poverty**[^4].
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** 
  - No evidence of displacement; focus on benefiting existing residents.
- **Social Capital:** 
  - Strengthened networks between residents, public authorities, and business actors.
- **Cultural Impacts:** 
  - Reinforces Brussels’ identity as a leader in equitable, sustainable urbanism.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** 
  - Construction, maintenance, and ongoing technical support roles created.
- **Economic Activity:** 
  - Increased business confidence in green retrofits and infrastructure.
- **Property Values:** 
  - Potential for long-term value appreciation as neighborhood becomes greener and more attractive.
- **Cost Savings:** 
  - Lower energy bills for residents; operational savings for building owners.
- **Economic Inclusion:** 
  - Focus on including low-income populations in the benefits and governance of the system.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** 
  - Significant reduction in fossil gas use and related GHG emissions.
  - Supports regional carbon neutrality targets for 2050[^2].
- **Resource Management:** 
  - Efficient use of local, renewable energy sources; reduced waste heat.
- **Environmental Quality:** 
  - Improved air quality from reduced combustion; potential for more urban green space as parks are utilized for geothermal.
- **Waste and Pollution:** 
  - Reduction in local air pollutants.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** 
  - Performance against EU and regional sustainability standards.

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** 
  - Strengthened collaborative mechanisms among diverse actors.
- **Policy Influence:** 
  - Model for new regulations on urban energy and climate justice.
- **Transparency and Accountability:** 
  - Digital dashboards and participatory governance increase accountability.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** 
  - Early evidence of improved energy efficiency, GHG reductions, and active citizen participation.
- **Unintended Consequences:** 
  - Ongoing monitoring required; no major negative impacts reported during initial phase.
- **Long-term Viability:** 
  - Strong if financial and social buy-in sustained, and if model proves scalable.

- **Contextual Dependencies:**

- **Critical Success Factors:**

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Holistic approach:** Integrates technology, social engagement, and governance innovation.
- **Equity focus:** Directly addresses energy poverty and includes vulnerable groups in design and benefit-sharing.
- **Replicability:** Designed as a pilot for broader application within Brussels and beyond.
- **Alignment with policy:** Fully supports city, regional, and EU climate goals.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Technical risk:** Retrofitting older buildings remains challenging and may face unforeseen difficulties.
- **Financial uncertainty:** Need for long-term financial sustainability beyond initial public and EU funding.
- **Scalability risk:** Success depends on willingness of more building owners to join and on regulatory support for expansion.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Positive impact:** Strong commitment to energy justice, participatory governance, and prioritizing marginalized groups.
- **Potential gaps:** Ongoing need to monitor for exclusion or unintended burdens as the system expands.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Long-term trajectory:** 
  - Planned expansion to more buildings and potentially other districts.
  - Ongoing digital and technical upgrades as technology evolves.
- **Ongoing challenges:** 
  - Maintaining stakeholder engagement, ensuring affordability, and managing technical complexity at larger scales.
- **Next phases:** 
  - Scaling up, refining governance, exploring additional renewable sources, and sharing lessons with other European cities.

---

*This case study demonstrates the complexity and promise of inclusive, technology-enabled urban energy transition projects. Be.SHARE offers a robust model for cities seeking to balance climate action with social justice and participatory governance, but ongoing adaptation and careful attention to local context will be essential for sustained success.*
