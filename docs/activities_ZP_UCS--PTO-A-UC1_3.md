# Executive summary## Praça de Lisboa Green Roof & Fifth Façade Project

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Praça de Lisboa Green Roof & Fifth Façade Project
- **Alternative Names:** PQAP (Projeto Quinta Fachada), Praça de Lisboa Olive Grove Green Roof, Historic Square Revitalized with Olive Grove Green Roof
- **Location:** Praça de Lisboa, Centro Histórico, Porto, Portugal
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 41.1456° N, 8.6147° W
- **Timeline:**  
  - Project inception: 2007 (design competition and planning)  
  - Completion: 2013 (main construction finished)  
  - Fifth Façade Project formalization: 2016 (integration into urban policy and planning)[^1]
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Area: 4,500 m² (green roof surface)  
  - Direct users: Residents, tourists, commercial tenants (shops, cafes)
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - Architectural and Urban Concept: Balonas & Menano  
  - Landscape Project: Pedro Balonas  
  - Green Roof Consulting: Neoturf  
  - Developer: Francisco Rocha Antunes – Capital Urbano  
  - General Contractor & Construction Management: BragaParques  
  - Fifth Façade Policy Project: Portuguese Association for Green Roofs (ANCV), Porto City Council[^1]
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - Private developer investment (Capital Urbano, BragaParques)  
  - Public support for later policy integration (municipal, national environmental funds for broader Fifth Façade Project)[^1]

#### **Executive Summary**
The Praça de Lisboa Green Roof & Fifth Façade Project is a landmark urban revitalization initiative in Porto, Portugal. Situated in the city’s historic core, the project transformed a formerly derelict, unhygienic, and unsafe marketplace into a vibrant, multi-use public space featuring an extensive green roof and olive grove. This “fifth façade” integrates landscape architecture with urban design, creating a pedestrian-friendly zone that blends commercial, leisure, and ecological functions. The project not only reimagined the square’s spatial dynamics but also set a precedent for the integration of green infrastructure in Porto’s urban policy through the later Fifth Façade Project (PQAP), which formalized green roofs as a tool for resilience and sustainability in city planning. The initiative’s impacts span environmental (urban heat mitigation, stormwater management, biodiversity enhancement), social (public health, social cohesion), and economic (increased property values, business vitality) domains, making it a widely-cited model for sustainable urban regeneration in southern Europe[^1][^2].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Porto is Portugal’s second-largest city and a UNESCO World Heritage site, known for its historical architecture, dense urban fabric, and vibrant tourism sector. The city has a population of about 215,000 (metro area 1.7 million), a strong service economy, and faces typical challenges of Southern European urban centers: aging infrastructure, pressure from tourism, and climate adaptation needs.

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  Praça de Lisboa is located between the iconic Torre dos Clérigos and the Livraria Lello in Porto’s historic downtown.  
  - *Historical development:* Originally the site of Mercado do Anjo, the square was once a bustling but increasingly neglected market area—a crowded, unsanitary, and unsafe public space[^1].  
  - *Land use:* Mixed-use, with commercial (shops, cafes), cultural, and residential functions; adjacent to major tourist attractions.  
  - *Demographics:* Diverse, with local residents, students, and a large influx of tourists.  
  - *Infrastructure:* The square sits atop a pre-existing underground car park, and prior to intervention, suffered from poor accessibility, degraded surfaces, and limited green space.  
  - *Environment:* Urban heat island, limited green cover, poor surface permeability.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Deteriorated, unhygienic, and unsafe public realm  
  - Lack of green space and pedestrian connectivity  
  - Underutilization of central urban land  
  - Disconnection from the surrounding historic fabric and cultural heritage[^1]

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Increasing public dissatisfaction with the state of the square  
  - Urban renewal competition launched by the city in 2007  
  - Emerging policy interest in sustainable urban regeneration and climate resilience

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - City government (planning, environment, public works departments)  
  - Local residents and business owners  
  - Heritage and preservation groups  
  - Developers, architects, and green infrastructure specialists[^1]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Porto City Council (municipal authority) led on planning and permitting  
  - Coordination with national heritage and environmental agencies

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Initial project predated formal green roof mandates, but later integration with the Fifth Façade Project (PQAP) aligned with new municipal and national policies incentivizing green roofs for energy efficiency and ecosystem services[^1][^2]

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Need to balance historic preservation with modern interventions  
  - Absence of clear regulatory frameworks for green roofs at project inception; subsequently addressed through PQAP and national guidelines (ANCV)[^1]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Revitalize Praça de Lisboa as a safe, attractive, and multifunctional public space  
  - Demonstrate the value of green roofs for urban resilience and heritage integration  
  - Use the site as a pilot for citywide green infrastructure policy[^1]

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To restore the square’s civic and ecological function by creating a “fifth façade”—a living roof that reintroduces the arboreal character of medieval Porto and sets a new standard for sustainable urban design.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Supports Porto’s resilience and climate adaptation strategies  
  - Influenced development of national and municipal policies on sustainable buildings, energy efficiency, and ecological corridors

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  Introducing a green roof and redesigning public space would catalyze social, economic, and environmental regeneration by making the area more attractive, usable, and ecologically functional.

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Local residents (improved environment, well-being)  
  - Businesses (increased footfall, higher property values)  
  - Tourists (enhanced urban experience)  
  - City as a whole (model for future sustainable development)[^1][^2]

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Use of extensive green roof systems in a heritage context  
  - Integration of landscape and building design (“fifth façade” as both roof and public space)  
  - Policy innovation through formalizing green roofs in urban planning documents

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  - City leadership’s commitment to urban renewal and sustainability  
  - Desire to enhance Porto’s international image as a forward-looking city

- **Economic:**  
  - Revitalization of commercial activity in the square  
  - Anticipated increase in property values and business revenues

- **Social:**  
  - Community demand for safe, attractive public spaces  
  - Heritage advocacy for sensitive yet innovative renewal

- **Environmental:**  
  - Urban heat mitigation, stormwater management, biodiversity goals  
  - Alignment with national and EU sustainability targets

- **Technological:**  
  - Advances in green roof construction and maintenance techniques  
  - Collaboration with specialized consultants and academic researchers

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Urban acupuncture—targeted intervention in a key public space to catalyze broader regeneration  
  - Landscape urbanism—blending ecological systems with built form  
  - Respect for heritage through contemporary reinterpretation[^1]

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Creation of a new topography: an undulating green roof with grass and 50 olive trees, reflecting the city’s historical olive groves  
  - Integration of commercial spaces (shops, cafes) below the roof, with open pedestrian pathways above  
  - Reorganization of circulation to enhance permeability and connection to adjacent landmarks[^1]

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial design and competition (2007)  
  - Construction phase (c. 2012–2013)  
  - Policy and planning integration (2016 onward with PQAP)[^1]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - **Extensive green roof system:** lightweight soil, drainage, root barriers, and water retention layers  
  - **Advanced irrigation and stormwater management:** designed to handle Mediterranean climate extremes  
  - **Innovative structural engineering:** accommodating the weight and root systems of olive trees atop a car park[^1]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (green roof system, landscape engineering)  
  - Social (public space co-design, heritage-sensitive renewal)  
  - Governance (policy integration via PQAP)

- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Close collaboration between architects, landscape architects, engineers, and municipal authorities  
  - Coordination with heritage and planning bodies to ensure compliance and buy-in

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Selected via city-sponsored design competition; major contractors appointed through developer and municipal processes

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Consultation with residents, business owners, and heritage organizations  
  - Ongoing dialogue facilitated by the city and project champions[^1]

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - While direct co-design was limited, public and expert feedback shaped design refinements

- **Project Management:**  
  - Multidisciplinary project teams, regular coordination meetings, and phased implementation milestones

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Not publicly disclosed in detail; involved substantial private investment for commercial elements and subsequent public funding for policy integration

- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Developer-led financing for construction  
  - Public funding and incentives for broader green roof policy roll-out (PQAP, national environmental funds)[^1]

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Rental income from commercial units  
  - Increased land value and possible municipal revenue from improved property assessments

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Commercial uses provide ongoing revenue for maintenance  
  - Policy incentives aim to make green roofs economically attractive for future developments

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Engineering complexities of supporting olive trees and soil on a rooftop above a car park  
  - Balancing green roof performance with historic preservation requirements[^1]

- **Financial:**  
  - Upfront costs for green infrastructure higher than conventional roofing  
  - Need for long-term maintenance funding

- **Social and Political:**  
  - Initial resistance from heritage bodies and some local stakeholders  
  - Skepticism about modern interventions in historic context[^1]

- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination across multiple municipal departments  
  - Lack of precedent for green roof permitting and standards

- **Environmental:**  
  - Ensuring drought resilience for Mediterranean conditions  
  - Managing microclimate impacts and biodiversity goals

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Demonstration of benefits through pilot implementation  
  - Engagement with heritage authorities to co-develop sensitive design solutions[^1]

- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Iterative adjustments to structural and planting schemes based on technical studies and stakeholder feedback

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Importance of visible, high-quality examples to overcome skepticism  
  - Need for clear maintenance and monitoring plans

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- **Model integration** of ecology and public space in historic context
- **Catalyst for policy change**—instrumental in shaping city and national green roof incentives
- **Highly visible demonstration** that built public and political support[^1][^2]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- **Initial resistance** from heritage and local stakeholders required substantial negotiation
- **Upfront cost and technical complexity** higher than traditional projects
- **Ongoing maintenance** needs require stable funding and expertise

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- **Broad public accessibility** is a strong point, though vigilance on gentrification is warranted
- **No evidence of displacement** to date, but commercial revitalization could impact affordability

#### **Future Outlook**
- The project has become a **benchmark for urban green infrastructure** in Portugal and beyond.
- **Ongoing challenges:** Sustaining maintenance, monitoring social impacts, expanding policy uptake.
- **Next phases:** Potential for further upscaling of green roofs citywide, integration with smart city and climate adaptation frameworks, and deeper community co-design in future projects[^1][^2].
