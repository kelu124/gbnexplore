# Executive summary## Høje Taastrup 4th Generation District Heating Network (4GDH)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Høje Taastrup 4th Generation District Heating Network (4GDH)
- **Alternative Names:** COOL DH Høje Taastrup demonstration site; Low Temperature District Heating (LTDH) in Høje Taastrup
- **Location:** Høje Taastrup Municipality, Greater Copenhagen Region, Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 55.6500° N, 12.3000° E
- **Timeline:**  
  - Project conception and EU Horizon 2020 funding: 2017  
  - Major retrofitting and construction: 2018–2021  
  - Monitoring and evaluation phase: Ongoing since 2021  
  - Pit Thermal Energy Storage (PTES) completed: 2023[^1]
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Population affected: Over 52,000 inhabitants[^1]  
  - Area: Multiple residential districts including Østerby  
  - Investment: Multi-million EUR, EU co-funded (exact figures not public)
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - Høje Taastrup Municipality  
  - Høje Taastrup Fjernvarme (HTF, the local DH utility)[^2]  
  - VEKS (Greater Copenhagen’s district heating transmission company)[^1]  
  - COOL DH consortium (EU Horizon 2020 project)[^3]
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - EU Horizon 2020 research and innovation program[^3]  
  - Danish Energy Agency  
  - Local municipal funds  
  - Private investments (e.g., CITY2 mall)

#### **Executive Summary**

The Høje Taastrup 4th Generation District Heating Network (4GDH) is a pioneering retrofit of a legacy district heating system in one of Denmark’s most sustainability-oriented municipalities. Designed and implemented within the EU COOL DH project, this initiative demonstrates the transformation of conventional high-temperature district heating into a flexible, low-temperature, and renewable-rich energy system. The project integrates advanced technologies such as large-scale solar thermal, heat pumps that recover surplus heat from local cooling demands (e.g., malls, data centers), and a pit thermal energy storage (PTES) facility with a capacity of 3,300 MWh for seasonal balancing[^1]. Smart metering and rigorous Key Performance Indicator (KPI) monitoring (including SCOP and SEER) enable continuous data-driven optimization of both heating and cooling supply.

This strategic retrofit addresses Denmark’s national decarbonization targets and the municipality’s ambitions to phase out fossil fuels in favor of renewables. By lowering distribution temperatures and harnessing local waste heat, the initiative reduces energy losses by 20%, increases the renewable share above 70%, and significantly cuts carbon emissions. The project’s outcomes include lower heating costs for residents, improved system reliability, and enhanced adaptability to variable demand. Challenges encountered include technical complexities in retrofitting legacy infrastructure, engaging consumers, and balancing the grid amid fluctuating renewable supply. The initiative is recognized as a best practice in sustainable urban energy transition and offers valuable lessons for similar European contexts[^3][^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Høje Taastrup is a suburban municipality within the Greater Copenhagen region, comprising approximately 52,000 residents[^1]. The local economy is characterized by retail, logistics, and services, with major facilities such as the CITY2 shopping mall, corporate offices, and transport hubs. The city is well-connected to Copenhagen and forms part of Denmark’s advanced metropolitan infrastructure.
  
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - Historical development: Suburban expansion from Copenhagen, with post-1970s development of residential districts and commercial zones[^3].  
  - Land use: Residential neighborhoods (Østerby, Sønderby), commercial centers (CITY2 mall, Copenhagen market), light industrial, and public services.  
  - Demographics: Mixed-income, with a significant proportion of middle-class families and elderly residents; notable ethnic diversity due to migration trends.  
  - Existing infrastructure: Pre-existing district heating network supplied primarily by high-temperature steam, with legacy buildings and some modern developments.  
  - Environmental conditions: Cold winters, moderate summers; Denmark’s push for climate resilience and renewable energy integration.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - High distribution losses and inefficiency in legacy district heating infrastructure[^3].  
  - Dependence on fossil-fueled heat sources (natural gas, oil) and insufficient renewable integration[^1].  
  - Rising energy costs and lack of adaptability to variable demand.
  
- **Triggering Events:**  
  - EU and Danish policy shifts toward decarbonization and renewable energy.  
  - Availability of Horizon 2020 funding for demonstration projects.  
  - Local advocacy for sustainable development and energy security.
  
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Municipality (policy leadership).  
  - District heating utilities (HTF, VEKS).  
  - Local businesses (CITY2 mall, data centers).  
  - Residents and housing associations.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  Multi-level governance: municipal, regional, and national authorities coordinate, supported by EU directives[^3][^1].
  
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Danish Heat Supply Act.  
  - EU Renewable Energy Directive.  
  - Local climate and energy action plans.
  
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Supportive legal framework for renewables and waste heat recovery.  
  - Regulatory complexity in grid modification and data privacy for smart metering.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Guided by principles of circular economy, low-carbon urbanism, and smart city innovation. Focused on integrating distributed energy resources and flexible grid management[^3][^1].
  
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Retrofitting of distribution pipes for low-temperature operation.  
  - Installation of PTES facility (70,000 m³, 3,300 MWh)[^1].  
  - Deployment of solar thermal arrays and heat pumps at CITY2 mall and other sites.
  
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial retrofitting in Østerby and selected districts.  
  - Gradual expansion to other neighborhoods.  
  - Monitoring and optimization as an ongoing process.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - **Low-temperature distribution:** Reduces losses and enables integration of diverse heat sources.  
  - **Heat pumps:** Recover surplus heat from commercial cooling (e.g., CITY2 mall, data centers)[^3][^1].  
  - **Solar thermal integration:** Large rooftop arrays provide renewable heat input.  
  - **Pit Thermal Energy Storage (PTES):** Enables seasonal balancing and peak shaving[^1].  
  - **Smart metering:** Real-time data collection for system optimization.
  
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (PTES, heat pumps, solar thermal, smart meters).  
  - Governance (multi-stakeholder collaboration).  
  - Process-oriented (KPI-based continuous improvement).
  
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Existing infrastructure retrofitted for compatibility.  
  - New tech layered onto legacy systems with phased implementation.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Competitive tendering for construction and technology supply.  
  - Partnerships with private sector (CITY2 mall, local businesses).
  
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Public consultations and workshops.  
  - Involvement of housing associations and local businesses.
  
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Residents informed and consulted on system changes and benefits.
  
- **Project Management:**  
  - Managed by municipal energy department, with technical coordination by HTF and VEKS.
  
#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Multi-million EUR investment (EU co-funded); exact breakdown not fully public[^3].
  
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - EU Horizon 2020 grant  
  - Danish Energy Agency subsidies  
  - Municipal budgetary allocations  
  - Private investment (e.g., CITY2 mall’s solar arrays)
  
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - District heating user fees  
  - Potential energy sales to regional grid
  
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Lower operating costs through efficiency and renewables  
  - Long-term maintenance funded by user fees and public budget

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Retrofitting legacy infrastructure for low-temperature operation[^3].  
  - Integrating diverse heat sources with variable supply (waste heat, solar, heat pumps)[^1].  
  - System balancing amid fluctuating demand.
  
- **Financial:**  
  - High upfront investment.  
  - Uncertainty in long-term maintenance costs.
  
- **Social and Political:**  
  - Engaging residents; managing expectations.  
  - Navigating regulatory changes.
  
- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination between municipal, regional, and private actors.
  
- **Environmental:**  
  - Ensuring no negative impact from new technologies (e.g., land use for PTES).

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Phased implementation to manage risk and complexity.  
  - Joint ownership of PTES for shared responsibility[^1].
  
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Adjustments in network layout to optimize flow and minimize losses.
  
- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Early and continuous stakeholder engagement is critical.  
  - Flexible design supports integration of future technologies.
