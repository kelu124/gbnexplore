# Executive summary## Tivoli GreenCity

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** Tivoli GreenCity  
- **Alternative Names:** Tivoli Eco-district, Tivoli Sustainable Neighbourhood  
- **Location:** Laeken, Brussels, Belgium  
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 50.8763° N, 4.3488° E  
- **Timeline:**  
  - Project conception: Early 2010s  
  - Planning and design: 2008–2012  
  - Construction: 2013–2019  
  - Inauguration: Phased, in 2016 and 2019  
  - Ongoing: Impact studies and continuous monitoring from 2019 onward  
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Area: ~47,000–50,000 m²  
  - Housing: 397–406 units (271 subsidized, 126 social housing)  
  - Population: Estimated 1,000+ residents  
  - Commercial/functional: 10,000 m² of public space, Greenbizz incubator, retail, nurseries  
  - Investment: €100–120 million total[^1]  
- **Leading Organization(s):** citydev.brussels (public developer), MSA Architecture & Urbanism, Greenbizz incubator, Community Land Trust Brussels  
- **Funding Sources:** Public funding (citydev.brussels), regional government, private partners, European grants, PPPs

#### **Executive Summary**

Tivoli GreenCity, located in the Laeken district of Brussels, stands as one of Europe’s most ambitious and awarded sustainable urban neighborhoods. Conceived in the early 2010s and inaugurated in phases between 2016 and 2019, the project transformed a former industrial brownfield into a vibrant, multifunctional eco-district integrating passive housing, renewable energy, rainwater harvesting, urban agriculture, and extensive public green spaces. The neighborhood comprises nearly 400 mixed-tenure housing units, community facilities, two eco-crèches, retail outlets, and the Greenbizz business incubator, all tied together by a strong commitment to social mixing, inclusivity, and ecological performance.

A key innovation of Tivoli GreenCity is its “living lab” approach: not only were advanced technologies and participatory design deployed from the outset, but a comprehensive post-occupancy impact study—using a 71-indicator open-data evaluation matrix—was commissioned to assess the project’s real-world social, technical, and environmental outcomes[^2][^3]. This approach enables iterative learning, allowing for adaptation and continual improvement based on resident feedback and real performance data.

Tivoli GreenCity’s achievements include substantial reductions in energy and water use, high levels of biodiversity, and lower living costs for residents. The project has also faced challenges around sustaining resident engagement and shared space management, generating vital lessons for future eco-districts. Overall, Tivoli GreenCity is a model for integrated, resilient, and participatory urban redevelopment in Europe[^2][^3][^4][^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Brussels, the capital of Belgium and the European Union, is a city of over 1.2 million inhabitants. It is economically diverse, with a strong services sector, significant immigrant communities, and pronounced socio-economic disparities between districts. Laeken, in northwest Brussels, historically features mixed industrial and residential uses, with dense working-class neighborhoods adjacent to logistics and port infrastructure[^4][^1].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - *Historical development:* The site was an industrial brownfield, previously used by Belgacom and other industries, bordering dense residential blocks and the Port of Brussels.  
  - *Current land use:* Mixed-use redevelopment—housing, retail, business incubator, public spaces.  
  - *Demographics:* Diverse, with a significant low-to-middle-income population and multicultural makeup.  
  - *Infrastructure:* Previously under-served; new development included upgrades to mobility, energy, and water systems.  
  - *Environmental conditions:* Canal-proximate, with prior contamination and limited green space; now substantially improved[^4][^5][^1].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Underutilized brownfield suffering from disuse and environmental degradation  
  - Housing shortages, especially for low-income households  
  - Social fragmentation between industrial and residential zones  
  - Poor environmental performance (energy, water, biodiversity)  
  - Limited community amenities and green spaces

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Regional policy focus on canal area regeneration  
  - Strategic land acquisition by citydev.brussels in the mid-2000s  
  - Political impetus for sustainability and social inclusion from 2009 onward[^4]

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - citydev.brussels (landowner, developer)  
  - Regional and city governments  
  - Local residents and neighborhood associations  
  - Social housing agencies, commercial partners, NGOs, future residents

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Regional government of Brussels-Capital Region  
  - citydev.brussels as a key public development agency  
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - BREEAM Communities certification  
  - Brussels’ Sustainable Development Plan and Canal Plan  
  - Social housing and mixed-tenure mandates  
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Supportive regulatory framework for eco-districts  
  - Procurement and land use regulations enabled public-private collaboration[^4][^5]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Create an exemplary sustainable neighborhood integrating social, environmental, and economic goals  
  - Foster social diversity and inclusion  
  - Demonstrate innovative green building and neighborhood management practices  
  - Provide affordable housing and promote community empowerment[^3][^1]

- **Vision Statement:**  
  - To build a living laboratory for sustainable urban living, setting a new standard for eco-districts in Europe

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Integral to Brussels’ Canal Area Masterplan and regional sustainability strategies  
  - Supports the city’s commitments to climate adaptation, social equity, and economic innovation[^2][^3]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  - Transform a brownfield into a sustainable, socially mixed neighborhood to address urban fragmentation, housing needs, and environmental challenges  
  - Use participatory design, advanced building standards, and post-occupancy evaluation to ensure long-term success[^2][^3][^1]

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Low- and middle-income households  
  - Local entrepreneurs (particularly in the green/circular economy)  
  - Broader urban community (via green space and public amenities)

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Living lab approach with continuous feedback loops  
  - Open-data evaluation matrix (71 indicators)  
  - Integration of business incubation, urban agriculture, and social participation[^2][^3]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Strong regional leadership in sustainable urbanism; policy mandates  
- **Economic:** Urban regeneration, investment attraction, job creation, business incubation  
- **Social:** Addressing housing inequality, fostering social cohesion, empowering residents  
- **Environmental:** Climate resilience, low-carbon living, biodiversity enhancement  
- **Technological:** Adoption of passive house standards, smart water and energy systems, digital monitoring[^2][^4][^1]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Based on principles of sustainability, social mixing, and participation  
  - BREEAM Communities (Outstanding) as certification framework[^4]  
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - 397–406 housing units in a mix of tenures  
  - 10,000 m² of public spaces, including Jardin des Justes central square  
  - Cohousing, community land trust, and incubator (Greenbizz)  
  - Integration of slow mobility, traffic calming, and green corridors[^4][^5][^1]
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Two main phases: initial housing and infrastructure (2016), completion and business/retail (2019)  
  - Further small-scale developments ongoing (cohousing, CLT)

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Passive building standards (ultra-low energy use)  
  - Rooftop gardens, communal laundry, rainwater harvesting systems  
  - Urban heat network for collective heating  
  - Open-data evaluation dashboard (71 sustainability indicators)[^2][^3][^1]
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (building, energy, water)  
  - Social (participatory design, cohousing, CLT)  
  - Governance (living lab, open-data matrix)[^2][^3]
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Technologies embedded from the design phase, aligned with sustainability charter  
  - Monitoring and evaluation integrated into management and planning

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Public competitive tendering for main construction and services  
  - Land sold or leased to housing and business partners under sustainability covenants[^4][^1]
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Participatory planning workshops  
  - Resident involvement in design and management (e.g., cohousing group)  
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Community land trust model for affordable home ownership  
  - Ongoing resident feedback through living lab and evaluation matrix[^2][^3][^6]
- **Project Management:**  
  - Oversight by citydev.brussels  
  - Scientific and technical committees for post-occupancy evaluation

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Total investment: €100–120 million, including Greenbizz[^1]  
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Primarily public/regional funds, citydev.brussels, with additional private and EU support  
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Sale/lease of housing, retail, business spaces  
  - Incubator rental income  
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Lower operating costs for residents  
  - Ongoing value creation via business incubation, increased land values

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Integrating advanced building systems across multiple developers  
  - Maintenance and management of shared spaces, communal facilities[^2]
- **Financial:**  
  - High upfront costs for sustainable building  
  - Ensuring affordability for target groups[^4][^1]
- **Social and Political:**  
  - Sustaining resident engagement post-occupancy  
  - Managing conflicts over shared spaces (e.g., access to rooftop gardens, collective laundry)[^2][^6]
- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination among multiple actors  
  - Navigating procurement and regulatory hurdles
- **Environmental:**  
  - Remediation of contaminated brownfield site  
  - Balancing biodiversity goals with urban density

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Continuous monitoring via living lab and open-data dashboard  
  - Ongoing support for resident committees and social cohesion activities[^2][^3]
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Adjustments to access arrangements for shared spaces  
  - Incorporation of feedback into building management and programming[^2]
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
  - Need for long-term engagement and support for residents  
  - Importance of transparent communication about shared responsibilities

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - ~400 housing units (social, subsidized, cohousing)  
  - Greenbizz incubator, retail units, eco-crèches  
  - 10,000 m² public spaces, urban agriculture sites[^3][^4][^1]
- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Affordable, high-quality housing  
  - Business support services, childcare, community programming
- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - 71 performance indicators tracked via open-data matrix  
  - BREEAM Communities Outstanding (95% rating)[^4]

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Enhanced quality of life, lower living costs (energy/water savings)  
  - Improved health and well-being via green spaces, walkability[^2][^1]
- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - High proportion of subsidized and social housing  
  - Inclusion of vulnerable groups via CLT and cohousing[^6][^1]
- **Displacement and Gentrification:**  
  - No major displacement reported; focus on inclusion[^1]
- **Social Capital:**  
  - Increased resident participation, though sustaining engagement remains a challenge[^2]
- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Support for multiculturalism and community identity

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  - Construction jobs, ongoing employment via Greenbizz and retail  
- **Economic Activity:**  
  - Attraction of green and circular economy businesses  
  - Increased investment in the area
- **Property Values:**  
  - Positive effect on neighborhood values, though affordability preserved for many units
- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Residents benefit from energy and water efficiency[^2]
- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - Opportunities for lower-income residents and entrepreneurs[^1]

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - Significant reductions in energy use and associated emissions (passive standards)  
  - Climate resilience via green infrastructure, water management[^2][^4]
- **Resource Management:**  
  - Rainwater harvesting, efficient energy systems  
- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - Improved air quality, biodiversity, noise reduction  
- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - Enhanced recycling, construction waste minimization  
- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - BREEAM certification, 71-indicator dashboard[^2][^4]

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Strengthened capacity for sustainable development at citydev.brussels  
  - New tools for monitoring and evaluation[^2][^3]
- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Informs future eco-districts in Brussels and beyond  
- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - Open-data approach increases transparency and learning

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  - Most technical and environmental objectives achieved  
  - Social outcomes mixed—need for sustained engagement post-occupancy[^2][^3]
- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Some under-use of shared facilities, highlighting need for ongoing community-building
- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Well-positioned for lasting impact, with adaptable management structures

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Integrated sustainability**—addressing social, economic, and environmental objectives  
- **Participatory design and living lab approach** enable real-time learning and adaptation  
- **High performance in energy, water, and biodiversity metrics**  
- **Model for institutional learning and open-data transparency**[^2][^3][^4]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Sustaining long-term resident engagement** post-occupancy remains challenging  
- **Initial complexities in managing shared spaces** and resident expectations  
- **Potential for social fragmentation if ongoing support is lacking**[^2][^1]

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Significant provision of affordable housing** and emphasis on inclusion  
- **Community land trust and cohousing models** support access for lower-income and marginalized groups  
- **Continuous improvement needed to ensure all groups benefit equally**[^1]

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Ongoing monitoring and adaptation** via open-data matrix and resident feedback  
- **Potential for further expansion of participatory models** (e.g., more CLT/cohousing units)  
- **Opportunity to inform wider urban policy in Brussels and other cities**  
- **Need for continued investment in social infrastructure and resident support mechanisms**[^2][^3][^1]
