# Executive summary## Cherrywood Urban Development – Barrington Building & TC1

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Cherrywood Urban Development – Barrington Building & TC1
- **Alternative Names:** Cherrywood SDZ (Strategic Development Zone), Cherrywood New Town
- **Location:** Cherrywood, South Dublin, Ireland
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 53.2478° N, 6.1467° W
- **Timeline:**
  - Planning initiated: 2010 (SDZ designation)
  - Major construction: 2016–present (ongoing, phased delivery)
  - Key milestones: Town centre approval and work (2018), major residential and infrastructure delivery (2020s)[^1][^2]
- **Project Scale:**
  - Area: 388 acres (approx. 157 hectares)
  - Population capacity: 25,000–26,000
  - Up to 9,000 housing units, 585,000 sq ft of retail/office, 10,000 worker capacity[^3][^1]
  - Investment: Over €1 billion[^1]
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown County Council (DLRCC)
  - Major private developers: Hines, Cairn Homes, Quintain, Evara, others
  - Dedicated Cherrywood Development Agency Project Team[^2]
- **Funding Sources:** Primarily private investment, with significant public sector funding for infrastructure; public-private partnerships (PPP) for specific components[^2][^1]

#### **Executive Summary**
Cherrywood Urban Development is Ireland’s largest greenfield new town project, transforming 388 acres in South Dublin into a future-proofed, sustainable neighborhood built to international green building standards. The Barrington Building & TC1 are flagship phases, exemplifying Cherrywood’s commitment to *BREEAM* and *WELL* Community standards, integrating high-performance building envelopes, green roofs, solar panels, and low-emission materials. The masterplan delivers a mix of over 7,500 new homes, 585,000 sq ft of retail and office space, and 150 acres of public parks and sports facilities, aiming to house up to 25,000 residents[^3][^1][^2].

Cherrywood’s innovation lies in its infrastructure-first approach—delivering roads, parks, schools, and public transport *before* major residential occupation. Digital tools manage building performance and resident feedback, while participatory planning and data-driven maintenance optimize ongoing operations. Key benefits include reduced embodied carbon, improved air quality, energy savings, enhanced biodiversity, and vibrant social and commercial amenities. The project also faces complex coordination challenges among multiple stakeholders and ongoing adaptation to changing post-pandemic urban needs. As Ireland’s first WELL Community, Cherrywood sets a new benchmark for sustainable and healthy urban living, with lessons for large-scale, mixed-use developments worldwide[^2][^4][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Cherrywood is part of South Dublin, within the Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown County. Dublin is Ireland’s capital and largest city, a major economic hub with a strong tech, finance, and services sector. The southern suburbs are characterized by affluent residential areas, excellent transport links, and proximity to both mountains and coast[^1][^4].
  
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - Historically, the area was agricultural and semi-rural, with fragmented land ownership and limited development.
  - Current land use: Mixed-use, including residential, retail, office, leisure, and extensive green space[^3][^5].
  - Demographic profile: Designed for a broad social mix—families, young professionals, students, and downsizers.
  - Existing infrastructure: New, high-quality roads, three major public parks (Tully, Beckett, Ticknick), new schools, Luas light rail station, cycling and pedestrian networks[^6][^3].
  - Environmental conditions: Scenic location at the base of the Dublin Mountains, near Dublin Bay; greenfield development with significant biodiversity and sustainability ambitions[^4].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Chronic housing shortages and affordability crisis in Greater Dublin.
  - Traffic congestion and car-dependent suburban patterns.
  - Lack of integrated, future-proofed neighborhood design.
  - Absence of high-quality green public space in new developments.
  - Fragmented landownership impeding coherent development[^1][^2].

- **Triggering Events:**  
  - National housing crisis and policy shift toward SDZs (Strategic Development Zones).
  - Local authority and government prioritization of infrastructure-led, sustainable urban expansion.
  - Private sector appetite for large-scale mixed-use investment[^2].

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Multiple private landowners and developers.
  - DLRCC (planning and infrastructure leadership).
  - State agencies (transport, education).
  - Local communities and interest groups.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Cherrywood designated as a Strategic Development Zone (SDZ) under Irish planning law.
  - Dedicated project team within DLRCC to coordinate all development[^2].

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - National Planning Framework (Project Ireland 2040).
  - DLRCC County Development Plan.
  - Cherrywood SDZ Planning Scheme (phased delivery, infrastructure-first).
  - EU directives on environmental assessment and sustainable urban development[^2][^6].

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - SDZ status enabled fast-tracked, integrated planning and delivery.
  - Requirement for environmental impact and sustainability assessment for all phases.
  - Complexities in aligning multiple landowners and adapting to evolving market/policy needs[^2][^1].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Deliver a sustainable, healthy, and vibrant new town in South Dublin.
  - Meet urgent housing needs with high-quality, mixed-tenure homes.
  - Integrate green infrastructure, sustainable transport, and community amenities from the outset.
  - Achieve leading sustainability certifications (BREEAM, WELL Community)[^4].

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To create a future-proofed, inclusive urban community that balances economic growth, environmental stewardship, and social well-being, setting a new benchmark for Irish and European green neighborhoods[^4][^3].

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Embeds national strategic housing and sustainability goals.
  - Advances DLRCC’s local development and climate strategies.
  - Aligns with EU climate and smart city targets[^2][^6].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  Infrastructure-led, sustainability-driven urban expansion will create livable, low-carbon communities, attract investment, and enhance quality of life while preventing the pitfalls of piecemeal or car-dependent sprawl[^2][^3].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - New and existing South Dublin residents (families, singles, elderly, students).
  - Local businesses and service providers.
  - Wider Dublin region through congestion relief and economic stimulus[^3][^1].

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Infrastructure-first phasing.
  - Use of digital tools for performance and engagement.
  - Green building certifications at neighborhood scale.
  - Participatory and adaptive planning.
  - Significant urban greening and biodiversity interventions[^4][^5].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** National and local priority on housing and climate.
- **Economic:** Demand for homes and commercial space; opportunity for major investment.
- **Social:** Need for community-oriented, inclusive neighborhoods.
- **Environmental:** Commitments to emissions reduction, biodiversity, and resilience.
- **Technological:** Adoption of prefabrication, digital monitoring, and renewable energy systems[^4][^7][^2].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Sustainable neighborhood and 10-minute city principles.
  - Placemaking: walkability, mixed-use, green space, and social cohesion.
  - Phased, infrastructure-first delivery[^2][^3].

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Mixed-use town center (retail, offices, leisure).
  - Multiple residential typologies (houses, duplexes, apartments, student housing).
  - Three major parks (Tully, Beckett, Ticknick), sports facilities, greenways, schools[^3].
  - High-density, but with rich green and public space fabric[^4][^3].

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Early delivery of roads, parks, schools, transport.
  - Sequential roll-out of residential and commercial components.
  - Ongoing adaptation of land use mix and density to market and social needs[^2].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Prefabricated building elements and timber frames to reduce embodied carbon[^7].
  - On-site renewable energy: solar PV on buildings, A-rated homes.
  - Digital platforms for building performance monitoring and resident feedback.
  - Green roofs, advanced insulation, low-emission materials.
  - Smart infrastructure: EV charging, data-driven maintenance[^7][^4].

- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological: Prefabrication, renewables, digital tools.
  - Social: Participatory planning, placemaking.
  - Process: Integrated, collaborative project management[^7][^2].

- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Seamless connection with light rail (Luas), cycling and pedestrian greenways.
  - Green and blue infrastructure embedded throughout masterplan.
  - Cross-sectoral coordination among developers, state, and council[^6][^2].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Mix of direct delivery by major developers and competitive tendering for contractors.
  - Council-managed infrastructure contracts[^2][^1].

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Regular forums and workshops hosted by DLRCC.
  - Community consultation processes, especially for parks, schools, and mobility.
  - Early and ongoing engagement with landowners, agencies, and businesses[^2].

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Resident and stakeholder input on amenities, design features, and services.
  - Feedback loops supported by digital tools for performance and satisfaction[^4].

- **Project Management:**  
  - Dedicated multidisciplinary project team within DLRCC, acting as a “one-stop shop”[^2].
  - Phased project management with strict milestones for infrastructure and housing delivery.

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Total estimated investment over €1 billion[^1].
  - Major early outlays on infrastructure (roads, parks, Luas extension, schools).
  - Additional costs for green building features and digital systems.

- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Majority from private developers.
  - Public sector funding for transport, schools, public parks.
  - PPP elements for specific infrastructure[^2][^1].

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Commercial and residential sales/rentals.
  - Developer contributions to public infrastructure.
  - Potential for land value uplift and tax increment financing.

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Ongoing management and maintenance costs supported by service charges and council budgets.
  - Long-term value creation through high-quality, desirable urban environment.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Soil stabilization required due to valley geography, resolved through innovative reuse of on-site soil (reduced waste and CO₂)[^7].
  - Coordination of complex, multi-phase construction and utility integration.
  - Ensuring green infrastructure and digital tools function as designed[^7].

- **Financial:**  
  - High upfront costs for infrastructure, with risk of budget overruns.
  - Market fluctuations affecting housing and commercial uptake[^2][^1].

- **Social and Political:**  
  - Need to balance diverse stakeholder interests (multiple landowners, public agencies).
  - Maintaining community engagement throughout a long, multi-phase build.
  - Equity and affordability challenges in a rising market[^2].

- **Administrative:**  
  - Bureaucratic delays from planning and permitting, especially with 12+ landowners[^1].
  - Ensuring compliance with SDZ scheme and environmental regulations.

- **Environmental:**  
  - Managing biodiversity and habitat impacts during major earthworks.
  - Ensuring green spaces and stormwater systems are resilient to climate change[^6].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Dedicated project team for streamlined coordination.
  - Early and regular stakeholder engagement for alignment and problem-solving[^2].
  - Responsive amendments to the planning scheme in light of post-COVID shifts (e.g., revising car parking requirements)[^2].

- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Soil stabilization replaced traditional aggregate fill, reducing truck journeys and emissions[^7].
  - Adjustments to housing/commercial mix to meet changing demand, especially for flexible work and retail space[^2].

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Infrastructure-first approach avoids years-long lag in services.
  - Early involvement of all stakeholders prevents costly conflicts.
  - Flexibility in masterplanning is essential to respond to changing urban trends.

  - New public schools (sites transferred, phased delivery).

- **Quantitative Metrics:**

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Visionary integration** of sustainability, technology, and placemaking[^4][^7].
- **Infrastructure-first model** delivers instant neighborhood quality and supports rapid community formation[^2].
- **Robust stakeholder engagement** and transparent governance increase buy-in and project resilience[^2].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **High complexity** of multi-owner delivery and ongoing coordination needs[^1].
- **Affordability risk** as property values rise in a high-amenity setting[^1].
- **Market uncertainty**—post-pandemic shifts in office/retail demand require flexible adaptation[^2].

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Broad social mix** designed in, but ongoing vigilance needed to maintain affordability and access.
- **Little risk of displacement** due to greenfield context, but economic inclusion must be monitored[^3][^1].

#### **Future Outlook**

- Cherrywood will continue to evolve over the next decade, with further phases of housing, commercial space, and green infrastructure.
- Ongoing adaptation to demographic shifts, work patterns, and climate targets will be necessary.
- Project’s success as a sustainable, healthy neighborhood will depend on maintaining high standards, inclusive growth, and responsive governance.
