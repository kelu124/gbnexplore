# Executive summary## LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN Project: Falcão Primary School

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN (LIFE17 CCA/ES/000088) - Falcão Primary School Pilot
- **Alternative Names:** Escola do Falcão; Nature-Based Solutions in Educational Buildings; myBUILDINGisGREEN Project
- **Location:** Porto, Portugal; also implemented in Badajoz (Spain) and Évora (Portugal)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Porto, Northern Portugal (coastal region)
- **Timeline:** Project duration 2017-2023; Falcão School implementation completed 2021; ongoing monitoring through After-LIFE period (2024-2028)
- **Project Scale:** 13,642 m² of building surface area across three pilot schools; 700 m² of green area at Falcão School; 3,911.61 m² of green areas created across all three schools
- **Leading Organization(s):** European Union (funding body); Porto City Council (local implementation partner); Royal Botanical Gardens; CARTIF Foundation (technical partner); Spanish National Research Council (CSIC); multiple regional and local authorities
- **Funding Sources:** European Union's LIFE Programme (co-financing mechanism for environmental projects); public sector contributions from municipal and regional authorities in Spain and Portugal

#### **Executive Summary**

The LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN project represents a comprehensive European initiative designed to enhance the climate resilience and operational efficiency of educational buildings through nature-based solutions (NBS). The Falcão Primary School in Porto serves as one of three pilot buildings demonstrating innovative approaches to climate adaptation. Implemented between 2017 and 2023, with ongoing monitoring through 2028, the project addresses the critical challenge of urban buildings' vulnerability to climate change impacts, particularly heat stress affecting children's academic performance and well-being.

The initiative implements an integrated suite of nature-based solutions including extensive green roofs, green façades, permeable paving systems, water management infrastructure (including the Horta da Oliveira pond), and complementary technologies such as photovoltaic panels and optimized ventilation systems. These interventions work synergistically to reduce building surface temperatures, lower cooling energy demands, manage stormwater infiltration, and enhance biodiversity. The Falcão School specifically encompasses 700 m² of green area and functions as a demonstration model building for sustainable climate adaptation strategies.

Beyond its physical interventions, the project emphasizes educational engagement, transforming the school into a living laboratory where students participate in environmental monitoring, biodiversity assessment, and sustainability learning. The initiative operates within a broader European policy framework addressing climate change adaptation, circular economy principles, and sustainable development goals. Its significance lies in demonstrating transferable methodologies for retrofitting existing educational infrastructure—a critical need given the age and thermal performance inadequacies of many European school buildings. The project has generated best practices guides, influenced building code discussions, and catalyzed commitments from eight municipalities in Badajoz province to replicate similar solutions in public buildings.[^1][^2][^3]

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:** Porto is Portugal's second-largest city, located on the Atlantic coast in the northern region. The city functions as a major economic, cultural, and educational hub with a population of approximately 1.6 million in the metropolitan area. Porto's economy traditionally centered on maritime commerce and port activities, with contemporary diversification into tourism, technology services, and creative industries. The city occupies a distinctive topography characterized by steep hills descending to the Douro River, creating both aesthetic appeal and infrastructure challenges. As a coastal Atlantic city, Porto experiences a temperate maritime climate with mild winters and moderately warm summers, though increasingly subject to extreme weather events and temperature variations associated with climate change.[^2]

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The Falcão School is located in the Campanha area of Porto. This district represents a typical urban neighborhood with mixed residential and institutional land use. The area contains primarily mid-rise residential buildings, small commercial establishments, and educational facilities. The neighborhood reflects Porto's characteristic dense urban fabric with limited green space prior to intervention. Demographic patterns include working and middle-class residents, with the school serving the local community's educational needs. The existing infrastructure includes conventional street networks, conventional drainage systems, and standard building stock largely predating modern energy efficiency standards. Environmental conditions prior to the intervention reflected typical urban challenges: limited vegetation, conventional impervious surfaces contributing to stormwater runoff, and buildings with thermal performance inadequacies typical of structures constructed before adoption of thermal regulations (pre-1990 in Portugal).[^1][^4]

**Pre-Initiative Conditions:** The Falcão Primary School, constructed prior to the adoption of Portugal's thermal regulation standards (Decreto-Lei n.º 40/90), exemplified broader challenges affecting European educational infrastructure. Research documented that only 30% of teaching hours in Madrid schools achieved adequate indoor hygrothermal conditions, with adequate air quality maintained during only 64% of instructional time.[^4] The school building experienced thermal stress during warm months, with indoor temperatures frequently exceeding comfort thresholds. Conventional flat roof construction, while structurally sound, lacked insulation properties to mitigate solar radiation absorption. Conventional façade systems provided minimal shading or thermal buffering. Stormwater management relied on conventional drainage systems with limited infiltration capacity. The school lacked integrated approaches to biodiversity, with limited green space or ecological habitat within the built environment. Energy consumption for cooling and heating remained high relative to modern efficiency standards, creating operational cost burdens for municipal budgets while contributing to greenhouse gas emissions.[^2][^4]

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:** The initiative addressed multiple interconnected challenges:

The thermal performance inadequacy of existing school buildings represented the primary technical problem. Buildings constructed before thermal regulation adoption generated excessive cooling demands during summer months and heating demands during winter, while interior conditions frequently fell outside comfort ranges. This directly impacted educational outcomes—thermal discomfort correlates with reduced student concentration, increased absenteeism, and diminished academic performance.

Climate change intensification presented an escalating challenge. Heat waves and extreme temperature events were increasing in frequency and intensity across Southern Europe, creating acute risks for vulnerable populations including children. Urban heat island effects in cities like Porto exacerbated temperature extremes. Conventional building stock lacked adaptive capacity to manage these emerging climate realities.

Stormwater management challenges reflected conventional urban infrastructure limitations. Impervious surfaces throughout cities prevented rainwater infiltration, overwhelming drainage systems during intense precipitation events and contributing to combined sewer overflows. This created both infrastructure strain and water quality degradation.

Biodiversity loss in urban environments represented an ecological challenge. Dense urban development with minimal green space and ecosystem connectivity fragmented habitats and reduced species diversity. Educational institutions, despite their significant land footprints, typically contributed minimally to urban ecological functions.

Energy consumption and associated greenhouse gas emissions from building operations created both environmental and economic challenges. Schools, as public institutions, consumed significant energy resources while operating under budget constraints, creating pressure to reduce operational costs.

Educational gaps concerning environmental sustainability and climate adaptation existed among student populations. Limited exposure to nature-based solutions and ecological principles constrained development of environmental literacy and sustainability consciousness among future generations.[^1][^2][^4]

**Triggering Events:** The LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN project emerged from convergence of policy drivers rather than a single catalytic event. The European Union's recognition of climate change as a critical challenge prompted development of the LIFE programme specifically supporting environmental and climate adaptation initiatives. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's Fifth Assessment Report (2014) highlighted particular vulnerability of urban centers to climate impacts, triggering policy attention to urban adaptation strategies. Recognition that educational buildings represented critical infrastructure requiring climate resilience prompted focus on schools as pilot sites. The availability of European funding specifically supporting innovative climate adaptation approaches created financial opportunity for municipal authorities to undertake otherwise resource-constrained interventions. Porto City Council's commitment to sustainability and climate adaptation, reflected in municipal planning frameworks, positioned the city as receptive to innovative demonstration projects.[^2]

**Stakeholder Landscape:** Pre-initiative stakeholder composition included municipal authorities (Porto City Council) responsible for school infrastructure and operations; educational institution leadership and teachers concerned with indoor environmental quality and educational outcomes; students and families affected by thermal comfort and educational quality; technical experts in building science and environmental engineering; environmental organizations advocating for sustainability; and European policy institutions establishing climate adaptation frameworks. Initial stakeholder interests varied: municipal authorities sought cost-effective solutions to operational challenges; educators prioritized student well-being and learning conditions; environmental advocates promoted ecological restoration; technical experts pursued innovation opportunities; and European institutions promoted policy-relevant demonstration projects.[^1][^2]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:** The initiative operated within multi-level governance structures. The European Union established the LIFE programme as the primary governance mechanism, providing funding, establishing eligibility criteria, and monitoring compliance with EU environmental and climate objectives. At national level, Portuguese government ministries responsible for education, environment, and energy established frameworks within which municipal authorities operated. Porto City Council functioned as the primary local governance entity, responsible for school operations, urban planning, and implementation of municipal sustainability commitments. Educational institution governance included school leadership, teachers, and parent organizations. Technical governance involved specialized expertise from research institutions (CSIC, CARTIF Foundation) providing scientific oversight and monitoring.[^1][^2]

**Relevant Policies:** Multiple policy frameworks shaped the initiative:

The European Union's climate adaptation strategy established overarching policy direction, recognizing climate change adaptation as essential to EU policy objectives. The LIFE programme specifically supported projects demonstrating innovative approaches to environmental challenges, including climate adaptation through nature-based solutions.

Portugal's national sustainability commitments, including targets under the Paris Agreement and EU climate directives, created policy impetus for municipal-level climate adaptation initiatives. National building regulations (Decreto-Lei n.º 40/90 and subsequent revisions) established thermal performance standards, though existing buildings predated these requirements.

Porto's municipal sustainability strategy and urban planning frameworks prioritized environmental quality, climate resilience, and green infrastructure development. Municipal commitment to sustainability informed receptiveness to innovative demonstration projects.

European Union research and innovation policies, including Horizon 2020 frameworks, supported development and testing of novel approaches to environmental challenges, creating policy alignment with LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN's research and demonstration objectives.

Building performance standards and energy efficiency directives at EU level created policy context supporting energy-efficiency improvements in buildings, aligning with project objectives.[^2][^4]

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** Legal frameworks facilitated project implementation through EU LIFE programme eligibility criteria, which specifically supported innovative environmental projects demonstrating transferable solutions. Existing building regulations permitted retrofitting and renovation activities, enabling green roof and façade installations. Municipal authority over school facilities enabled implementation decisions without requiring private property owner consent.

Institutional enablers included availability of technical expertise through research partnerships (CSIC, CARTIF), which provided scientific credibility and technical guidance. Multi-level governance coordination between EU, national, and municipal authorities created aligned policy support. Educational institution engagement and community acceptance facilitated implementation.

Barriers included technical complexity of retrofitting existing structures, requiring specialized design and engineering expertise. Regulatory uncertainty regarding incorporation of novel nature-based solutions into building codes created some implementation constraints, though the project actively engaged with regulatory authorities to address this barrier. Maintenance requirements for green infrastructure created long-term operational obligations requiring institutional commitment and budget allocation. Initial capital costs, while addressed through EU funding, represented substantial investment requiring municipal financial commitment. Technical knowledge gaps regarding design, implementation, and maintenance of nature-based solutions necessitated capacity building.[^2][^5]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:** The LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN project articulated explicit objectives addressing climate adaptation and building resilience:

The overarching objective was to increase climate resilience of educational and social service buildings by implementing nature-based solutions as prototypes of climate adaptation approaches. This encompassed both physical resilience (buildings' capacity to withstand climate stresses) and operational resilience (sustained functionality and performance under changing climatic conditions).

Specific technical objectives included improving knowledge on vulnerability assessment methodologies for buildings facing climate change impacts; analyzing and verifying effectiveness of nature-based solutions as climate adaptation measures; and promoting sustainable ecosystem-based adaptation approaches transferable across European regions.

Governance objectives emphasized promoting adoption of nature-based solutions within regulatory frameworks, building codes, and professional practice standards. The project explicitly targeted transformation of building sector governance to normalize integration of nature-based solutions in climate adaptation strategies.

Knowledge transfer objectives included developing best practices guides, disseminating lessons learned, and building capacity among specialized professionals and building managers. The project recognized that innovation alone was insufficient; systematic knowledge transfer to practitioners was essential for scaling impact.

Educational objectives, particularly evident in the Falcão School implementation, included using the pilot building as a demonstration and learning resource, engaging students in environmental education and sustainability learning, and developing educational curricula integrating nature-based solutions and climate adaptation concepts.[^1][^2]

**Vision Statement:** The project's aspirational vision positioned nature-based solutions as fundamental to sustainable urban development and climate adaptation in European cities. Rather than viewing climate adaptation as purely technical engineering challenge, the vision integrated ecological, educational, and social dimensions. The vision emphasized that buildings could simultaneously serve climate adaptation, biodiversity conservation, educational enrichment, and community well-being functions. Falcão School exemplified this vision—functioning simultaneously as an educational facility, climate adaptation demonstration, ecological habitat, and community resource.[^2]

**Alignment with Broader Plans:** The initiative aligned with multiple strategic frameworks:

European Union climate adaptation strategy positioned nature-based solutions as critical to meeting climate objectives. EU commitment to climate neutrality by 2050 required widespread adaptation measures across building stock, making demonstration projects like LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN strategically important for policy development.

The circular economy action plan emphasized resource efficiency and waste reduction, with nature-based solutions contributing to these objectives through water infiltration, reduced cooling demands, and enhanced ecosystem services.

Urban development strategies across European cities increasingly recognized green infrastructure as essential to livability, climate resilience, and biodiversity conservation. LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN contributed to emerging consensus that sustainable cities required integration of nature-based solutions in urban fabric.

Portugal's national climate adaptation strategy and Porto's municipal sustainability commitments directly aligned with project objectives. Municipal engagement reflected broader political commitment to climate action.

Educational policy frameworks increasingly emphasized environmental literacy and sustainability education. Integration of nature-based solutions into school infrastructure aligned with educational policy objectives.[^2][^4]

**Theory of Change:** The project's logic model operated through several causal pathways:

Implementation of nature-based solutions (green roofs, green façades, water management systems) would directly reduce solar radiation absorption, lower surface temperatures, decrease cooling energy demands, and enhance thermal comfort within buildings. These physical changes would produce measurable environmental benefits.

Reduced energy consumption would generate operational cost savings for schools, improving financial sustainability and freeing resources for educational purposes. Lower greenhouse gas emissions from reduced energy use would contribute to climate mitigation objectives.

Enhanced green infrastructure would create habitat for plants, insects, and other species, increasing urban biodiversity. Colonization of green roofs and façades by native species would enhance ecological functions and aesthetic qualities.

Improved stormwater infiltration through green infrastructure would reduce runoff volumes, decreasing strain on conventional drainage systems and improving water quality. Reduced combined sewer overflows would protect aquatic ecosystems.

Educational engagement with nature-based solutions would enhance students' environmental literacy, sustainability awareness, and connection to nature. Schools functioning as demonstration sites would influence broader community understanding and acceptance of nature-based solutions.

Visible success of pilot projects would influence policy makers, building professionals, and property owners, encouraging adoption of similar approaches. Documentation and dissemination of best practices would reduce barriers to replication.

Engagement with regulatory authorities would facilitate incorporation of nature-based solutions into building codes and standards, institutionalizing these approaches in building sector practice.[^1][^2]

Broader community beneficiaries included residents in neighborhoods surrounding pilot schools, who experienced improved urban microclimate, enhanced aesthetics, and increased biodiversity. Municipal authorities benefited from demonstration of effective climate adaptation approaches and cost-effective solutions to infrastructure challenges.

Sectoral beneficiaries included building professionals, architects, engineers, and construction companies gaining knowledge and experience with nature-based solutions design and implementation. Regulatory authorities benefited from evidence base for updating building codes and standards.

Societal beneficiaries encompassed all residents of cities implementing similar approaches, through improved urban climate resilience, enhanced ecosystem services, and reduced greenhouse gas emissions. Future generations benefited from demonstration of sustainable development pathways and preservation of urban biodiversity.[^1][^2]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:** The project's design reflected several interconnected planning philosophies:

**Nature-based solutions framework:** Rather than conventional engineering approaches to building performance (mechanical cooling, conventional insulation), the project embraced ecological design principles where natural processes—vegetation shading, evaporative cooling, soil infiltration—provided primary performance functions. This represented philosophical shift from technology-dominant to nature-integrated approaches.

**Bioclimatic design principles:** The design process employed bioclimatic analysis, evaluating specific climate characteristics of each location and designing interventions tailored to local conditions. Extensive modeling using EnergyPlus software enabled evaluation of different intervention scenarios and optimization of solutions for specific climatic contexts. This contrasted with one-size-fits-all approaches, reflecting sophisticated understanding of climate-specific adaptation requirements.[^4]

**Integrated building systems approach:** Rather than isolated interventions, the project designed complementary systems working synergistically. Green roofs provided thermal buffering and water infiltration; green façades provided shading and evaporative cooling; ventilation optimization provided passive cooling; photovoltaic panels provided renewable energy. This systems integration reflected understanding that building performance resulted from multiple interacting factors.

**Adaptive capacity and resilience framework:** The design emphasized building resilience—capacity to withstand climate stresses and maintain functionality under changing conditions. Nature-based solutions enhanced adaptive capacity through multiple mechanisms: thermal buffering, water storage and infiltration, biodiversity supporting ecosystem resilience.

**Educational integration framework:** Particularly at Falcão School, design incorporated educational functions. Green infrastructure was deliberately designed as learning resource, with accessible observation areas, interpretive signage, and integration with curriculum. This reflected philosophy that sustainability infrastructure should educate and engage communities, not merely function invisibly.[^1][^2][^4]

**Physical/Spatial Design:** At Falcão Primary School, interventions encompassed:

**Green roofs:** The project implemented extensive green roofs with diverse native plant species. Design incorporated substrate depth, drainage layers, and vegetation selection optimized for Porto's maritime climate. The green roof system provided thermal insulation, reduced surface temperatures, managed stormwater, and created habitat for colonizing species.

**Green façades:** Vertical green structures with vegetation provided façade shading, particularly on east and west-facing elevations experiencing morning and afternoon thermal stress. These structures combined aesthetic enhancement with thermal performance functions.

**Water management systems:** The Horta da Oliveira pond functioned as both water retention and biodiversity enhancement feature. Permeable paving installations allowed vegetation growth and water infiltration, reducing stormwater runoff volumes. These systems addressed conventional drainage limitations while creating ecological features.

**Outdoor area development:** Alameda de Cartes Park, adjacent to school, received enhancements including additional vegetation, improved accessibility, and biodiversity habitat creation. This extended green infrastructure beyond building envelope to surrounding landscape.

**Ventilation optimization:** Design incorporated free-cooling ventilation systems enabling passive cooling through natural air circulation, reducing mechanical cooling demands.

**Photovoltaic integration:** Solar panels provided renewable electricity generation, complementing energy efficiency measures.[^1][^2][^6]

**Scope and Phasing:** The project operated across multiple phases:

**Design phase (2017-2019):** Vulnerability assessment, climate analysis, stakeholder engagement, and detailed design of nature-based solutions for each pilot building. Extensive modeling and simulation informed design optimization.

**Implementation phase (2019-2021):** Construction and installation of nature-based solutions at pilot buildings. Falcão School implementation was completed in 2021, enabling monitoring during project final years.

**Monitoring and evaluation phase (2021-2023):** Intensive performance monitoring during project final years, with measurement of temperature, energy consumption, water infiltration, and biodiversity changes.

**After-LIFE period (2024-2028):** Extended monitoring and evaluation continuing beyond project formal completion, ensuring long-term performance assessment and adaptive management. This extended timeline reflected recognition that nature-based solutions required extended observation periods for full effects to manifest.[^1][^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**

**Green roof systems:** Multiple design variations were tested, including:
- Extensive green roofs (mBiGCUVE 1) with shallow substrate supporting low-growing vegetation
- Green roofs with inner air chambers (mBiGCUVE 2) creating additional insulation layers
- Native plant species (over 25 species) selected for local climate adaptation and ecological value

**Green façade systems:** Vertical vegetation structures providing shading and evaporative cooling, with species selection reflecting local conditions.

**Water management infrastructure:** Permeable paving allowing vegetation growth and water infiltration; retention ponds (Horta da Oliveira) providing water storage and biodiversity habitat; drainage systems designed for infiltration rather than rapid runoff.

**Ventilation systems:** Free-cooling ventilation enabling passive thermal regulation through natural air circulation patterns, reducing mechanical cooling demands.

**Photovoltaic panels:** Building-integrated solar panels providing renewable electricity generation, complementing energy efficiency improvements.

**Monitoring and assessment technologies:**
- Temperature sensors (multiple locations at different building levels) measuring indoor and surface temperatures
- Energy monitoring equipment tracking electricity and fuel consumption
- Water monitoring systems measuring infiltration rates and runoff volumes
- Biodiversity sampling protocols documenting species colonization and ecosystem development
- Building performance simulation software (EnergyPlus) enabling performance modeling and optimization

**Digital platforms:** Online training resources, data management systems, and communication platforms supporting knowledge transfer and stakeholder engagement.[^1][^2][^4]

**Innovation Type:** Innovations encompassed multiple categories:

**Technological innovations:** Green roof design variations, integration of renewable energy, advanced monitoring systems.

**Social innovations:** Participatory design processes, educational engagement transforming schools into demonstration resources, community involvement in monitoring and data collection.

**Governance innovations:** Engagement with regulatory authorities to incorporate nature-based solutions into building codes; municipal declarations of interest for solution replication; development of best practices guides for professional guidance.

**Financial innovations:** Cost-benefit analysis demonstrating economic viability of nature-based solutions; documentation of operational cost savings; models for financing similar retrofits.

**Process innovations:** Standardized vulnerability assessment methodologies, long-term monitoring protocols, capacity-building approaches for professional training.[^2][^5]

**Integration Approach:** Technologies were integrated through systems design approach:

Building performance simulation enabled evaluation of how different interventions interacted. Green roofs provided primary thermal buffering; green façades provided complementary shading; ventilation optimization provided passive cooling; photovoltaic panels addressed remaining energy demands. This integration reflected understanding that optimal performance required multiple complementary systems rather than single interventions.

Water management systems integrated with green infrastructure, using vegetation and permeable surfaces to manage infiltration while creating biodiversity habitat. This integration addressed multiple objectives (thermal performance, water management, biodiversity) through unified infrastructure.

Monitoring systems integrated diverse data streams (temperature, energy, water, biodiversity) enabling comprehensive performance assessment and understanding of system interactions. This integration supported adaptive management and evidence-based refinement of approaches.

Educational integration incorporated green infrastructure into curriculum and learning activities, transforming technological systems into educational resources. This reflected philosophy that sustainability infrastructure should engage and educate communities.[^1][^2]

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges:**

The retrofitting of existing buildings with green infrastructure presented significant technical complexities. Existing roof structures required structural assessment to determine load-bearing capacity for green roof systems, substrate, and vegetation. Falcão School's flat roof, while suitable for green roof installation, required careful engineering to ensure adequate drainage and prevent water infiltration into building interior. Some existing buildings required structural reinforcement to accommodate green roof loads, adding cost and complexity.

Integration of green infrastructure with existing building systems required careful coordination. Ventilation systems needed modification to enable free-cooling ventilation while maintaining adequate indoor air quality. Electrical systems required integration with photovoltaic panels, necessitating safety systems and grid connection protocols. Water management systems required integration with existing plumbing and drainage infrastructure, sometimes requiring modification of existing systems.

Vegetation establishment and maintenance presented ongoing technical challenges. Green roofs required careful plant selection, substrate composition, and irrigation management during establishment phase. In Porto's maritime climate, salt spray and wind exposure created additional challenges for vegetation survival on elevated structures. Initial plant mortality rates required replanting and adaptive species selection. The project documented that 14 months after implementation, green covers were colonized by 16 additional autochthonous vegetable species, indicating successful establishment but requiring extended observation periods.[^2]

Monitoring technology deployment and data management created technical challenges. Multiple sensor types required calibration and maintenance. Data collection systems required reliable power supply and data transmission infrastructure. Integration of diverse data streams (temperature, energy, water, biodiversity) required sophisticated data management systems and analytical approaches.

**Financial Challenges:**

Capital cost requirements for retrofitting existing buildings represented significant financial burden for municipal authorities operating under budget constraints. While EU LIFE funding provided primary financing, municipal co-financing requirements created budget pressures. Initial cost estimates sometimes proved conservative, with unforeseen site conditions or technical complications requiring additional expenditure.

Operational cost sustainability created ongoing financial challenges. While energy efficiency improvements generated cost savings, maintenance requirements for green infrastructure created new operational costs. Vegetation management, substrate replacement, drainage system maintenance, and monitoring activities required dedicated funding. Schools operated under tight budgets, creating tension between maintenance requirements and other educational priorities.

Long-term financing for extended monitoring (through 2028) required sustained commitment beyond typical project timelines. Securing funding for After-LIFE period activities proved challenging, requiring explicit planning and commitment from municipal authorities and research institutions.[^2][^7]

**Social and Political Challenges:**

Initial community skepticism regarding novel approaches created social challenges. Some stakeholders questioned effectiveness of nature-based solutions compared to conventional engineering approaches. Concerns about maintenance burdens and long-term viability required extensive education and demonstration of successful implementation.

Educational community concerns regarding potential disruption during construction required careful project scheduling and communication. Construction activities during school operation created noise, dust, and access constraints requiring mitigation measures and community communication.

Displacement of existing vegetation and habitat during construction created environmental justice concerns. The project addressed these through careful planning to minimize habitat loss and compensate through new habitat creation through green infrastructure.

Political support fluctuations created uncertainty regarding project continuation and long-term commitment. Changes in municipal leadership or political priorities could have jeopardized project support. The project addressed this through documentation of benefits and engagement with multiple political stakeholders to build broad political support transcending individual political cycles.

Regulatory uncertainty regarding nature-based solutions in building codes created legal and institutional challenges. Building professionals unfamiliar with green roof and façade design and maintenance created resistance from conventional building sector. The project actively engaged with regulatory authorities and professional organizations to address these barriers.[^2][^5]

**Administrative Challenges:**

Coordination across multiple governmental levels (EU, national, regional, municipal) and multiple organizations (research institutions, municipal authorities, construction companies) created administrative complexity. Differing administrative procedures and reporting requirements across organizations required careful coordination and communication.

Procurement processes for novel technologies and approaches sometimes proved cumbersome, as conventional procurement frameworks were designed for established technologies. Adapting procurement procedures to accommodate innovative approaches required administrative flexibility and creativity.

Data management and reporting requirements across multiple organizations and jurisdictions created administrative burden. Ensuring consistent data collection, quality assurance, and reporting across three pilot sites in two countries required robust administrative systems.

**Environmental Challenges:**

Climate variability and extreme weather events created challenges for vegetation establishment and system performance. Porto's maritime climate created salt spray exposure and wind stress affecting vegetation survival. Extreme heat events tested cooling system effectiveness. Heavy precipitation events tested water infiltration and drainage system performance.

Unforeseen ecological interactions created challenges. Species colonization of green roofs proceeded at variable rates and in unexpected patterns. Some invasive species colonized green infrastructure, requiring management interventions. Predator-prey relationships and ecological succession proceeded differently than anticipated, requiring adaptive management.[^2]

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:**

**Technical problem-solving:** When structural load-bearing challenges emerged, engineering teams conducted detailed structural assessments and designed reinforcement approaches as needed. When vegetation establishment proved challenging in some locations, plant species were adjusted based on performance monitoring, and irrigation management protocols were refined. When monitoring technology malfunctions occurred, redundant sensors and backup data collection systems were implemented to ensure data continuity.

**Financial problem-solving:** Cost overruns were addressed through value engineering—identifying cost-saving opportunities without compromising performance. Operational cost sustainability was addressed through integration of maintenance into standard school budgets and demonstration of cost savings offsetting maintenance expenses. Long-term monitoring financing was secured through explicit After-LIFE planning and commitment from research institutions to continue monitoring activities.

**Social and political problem-solving:** Community skepticism was addressed through extensive education, site visits, and demonstration of successful implementation at pilot sites. Educational community concerns were addressed through careful project scheduling, communication, and involvement of school staff in project planning. Political support was secured through documentation of benefits, engagement with multiple political stakeholders, and integration of project objectives into municipal sustainability strategies.

**Regulatory problem-solving:** Regulatory uncertainty was addressed through active engagement with building code authorities, provision of technical documentation supporting nature-based solutions, and development of best practices guides for professional implementation. Professional resistance was addressed through training workshops, site visits, and demonstration of successful implementation.[^2][^5]

**Design Modifications:**

**Green roof design:** Initial green roof designs were refined based on performance monitoring. Substrate composition was adjusted to optimize plant establishment and drainage. Plant species selection was modified based on actual performance and colonization patterns, incorporating native species that demonstrated superior adaptation to local conditions.

**Water management systems:** Drainage system design was refined based on infiltration performance monitoring. The Horta da Oliveira pond design was adjusted to optimize water retention and biodiversity functions. Permeable paving specifications were refined based on performance data.

**Ventilation systems:** Free-cooling ventilation protocols were adjusted based on monitoring of indoor air quality and thermal comfort. Seasonal operation schedules were refined based on climate data and performance monitoring.

**Monitoring protocols:** Monitoring approaches were refined based on initial data collection experience. Sensor placement was optimized based on initial results. Data collection frequency and analytical approaches were adjusted to maximize information value while managing data management burden.[^2]

**Lessons Learned During Implementation:**

**Nature-based solutions require extended observation periods:** The project recognized that full effects of nature-based solutions emerge over extended timescales. Initial 6-month monitoring in Porto showed promising results (>5°C temperature reduction, 31% rainwater loss reduction), but extended monitoring through 2028 was planned to capture long-term performance and ecological succession.

**Vegetation establishment and biodiversity colonization are dynamic processes:** Rather than static systems, green infrastructure evolved over time as vegetation established and species colonized available habitat. The project documented 16 additional plant species and 77 additional animal species colonizing green infrastructure at Solana de los Barros site, indicating dynamic ecological processes requiring adaptive management rather than static maintenance.

**Context-specific design optimization is essential:** Climate-specific design proved critical—solutions optimal for warm, dry climates (Badajoz, Évora) differed from solutions optimal for mild, humid climates (Porto). Extensive modeling and site-specific analysis proved more effective than standardized approaches.

**Participatory engagement enhances project success:** Active involvement of educational communities in design, implementation, and monitoring created ownership, enhanced educational value, and improved project outcomes. Participatory approaches proved more effective than top-down implementation.

**Institutional commitment and maintenance capacity are critical:** Long-term success of nature-based solutions required sustained institutional commitment to maintenance and management. Schools with strong commitment to sustainability and adequate maintenance capacity achieved better outcomes than those with marginal commitment.

**Policy engagement and regulatory change are essential for scaling:** Pilot projects alone were insufficient for widespread adoption. Active engagement with regulatory authorities, incorporation of lessons into building codes, and development of policy frameworks supporting nature-based solutions proved essential for scaling impact beyond demonstration sites.[^1][^2][^5]

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies:**

Several project elements were context-specific and would require adaptation for other locations:

**Climate context:** The project explicitly designed different solutions for different climate zones. Badajoz and Évora, with warm, dry climates, required greater protection on roofs and façades receiving maximum radiation, with emphasis on passive measures like night ventilation and evaporative cooling. Porto, with milder, more humid climate, required different design optimization emphasizing roof interventions with complementary ventilation measures. Other locations would require similar climate-specific analysis and design optimization rather than direct replication of specific solutions.[^4]

**Building characteristics:** The pilot buildings shared specific characteristics (flat roofs in good condition, façades suitable for green system anchoring, pre-regulation construction requiring retrofitting) that facilitated nature-based solutions implementation. Buildings with different structural characteristics, roof slopes, or construction methods would require adapted design approaches.

**Institutional context:** The availability of EU funding through LIFE programme, municipal commitment to sustainability, and research institution capacity were context-specific enablers. Other locations would require comparable institutional support and funding mechanisms.

**Regulatory environment:** The regulatory frameworks governing building retrofitting, green infrastructure standards, and sustainability certifications varied across jurisdictions. Replication in other contexts would require adaptation to local regulatory requirements.

**Professional capacity:** The availability of technical expertise in nature-based solutions design and implementation was context-specific. Locations lacking such expertise would require capacity-building investments.[^2][^5][^4]

**Critical Success Factors:**

Analysis of project outcomes identified several critical success factors essential to positive results:

**Technical design quality:** Rigorous climate analysis, building simulation, and design optimization were critical to achieving performance objectives. Sites with less rigorous design processes likely experienced less optimal outcomes.

**Institutional commitment:** Strong municipal commitment to sustainability, adequate maintenance capacity, and long-term financial commitment proved essential to successful implementation and sustainability.

**Stakeholder engagement:** Active involvement of educational communities, regulatory authorities, and professional networks enhanced project success. Sites with limited stakeholder engagement likely experienced greater implementation challenges.

**Research partnership:** Collaboration with technical experts from research institutions (CSIC, CARTIF) provided scientific rigor and quality assurance. Sites lacking such expertise faced greater technical risks.

**Extended monitoring:** Commitment to long-term performance monitoring and adaptive management enhanced project success. Sites treating implementation as one-time intervention rather than ongoing process likely experienced less optimal long-term outcomes.

**Policy engagement:** Active engagement with regulatory authorities and policy makers enhanced prospects for policy influence and scaling. Sites treating projects as isolated interventions rather than policy catalysts likely experienced limited broader impact.[^2][^5]

**Scalability:**

The project demonstrated scalability potential at multiple levels:

**Building-scale scalability:** The solutions implemented at pilot schools could be replicated in other educational buildings and potentially other building types (offices, residential, institutional). The project's focus on educational buildings was partly arbitrary—solutions were technically applicable to diverse building types.

**Geographic scalability:** The project demonstrated feasibility across different European climate zones (Mediterranean, Atlantic). Solutions would be transferable to other European regions with adaptation to local climate conditions. Potential for application in non-European contexts would require additional climate-specific analysis and adaptation.

**Sectoral scalability:** While focused on educational buildings, solutions were applicable to other institutional buildings (hospitals, administrative offices, cultural facilities) and potentially to residential and commercial buildings.

**Systemic scalability:** Rather than isolated building retrofits, nature-based solutions could be integrated into broader urban green infrastructure systems, creating synergies and enhanced ecosystem services.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Rigorous scientific approach:** The project employed sophisticated climate analysis, building performance simulation, and long-term monitoring protocols. This scientific rigor distinguished the project from many green infrastructure initiatives lacking rigorous evaluation. The commitment to extended monitoring through 2028 ensures long-term performance assessment and evidence generation.

**Integrated systems design:** Rather than isolated green roof projects, the initiative designed complementary solutions working synergistically. Integration of green roofs, façades, water management, and ventilation optimization created comprehensive performance improvements exceeding what any single intervention could achieve.

**Climate-specific adaptation:** The project recognized that optimal solutions differed for different climate zones and employed rigorous analysis to optimize designs for specific contexts. This sophisticated understanding of climate-specific adaptation requirements represented best practice compared to standardized approaches.

**Educational integration:** Transformation of school buildings into demonstration and learning resources represented innovative approach to sustainability education. Students' participation in environmental monitoring and biodiversity assessment created educational value extending beyond conventional curriculum.

**Multi-level governance coordination:** The project successfully coordinated across EU, national, and municipal governance levels, research institutions, and construction sector. This multi-level coordination demonstrated effective governance approaches for complex sustainability initiatives.

**Policy engagement:** Active engagement with regulatory authorities and policy makers positioned the project to influence policy development and support scaling. Engagement with eight municipalities in declarations of interest demonstrated policy influence.

**Comprehensive knowledge dissemination:** Development of best practices guides, online training resources, and systematic documentation of lessons learned represented commitment to knowledge transfer supporting professional practice and scaling.

**Measurable environmental benefits:** Documented energy savings (11.2% electricity reduction for cooling; 30% diesel reduction for heating), water management improvements (10-31% reduction in rainwater loss), and biodiversity enhancements (16-77 additional species) demonstrated substantial environmental benefits.[^2][^5]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Limited geographic scope:** While the project addressed three pilot sites across Spain and Portugal, this represented limited geographic coverage. Generalization of findings to other European regions requires caution, particularly for regions with different climate characteristics.

**Building-focused scope:** The project focused on educational buildings, limiting applicability to other building types. Whether solutions transfer to residential, commercial, or industrial buildings requires additional investigation.

**Extended timeline uncertainty:** While extended monitoring through 2028 was planned, the project's long-term trajectory remains uncertain. Performance degradation, maintenance challenges, or ecological succession might produce different long-term outcomes than initial monitoring suggested.

**Maintenance capacity concerns:** Long-term success depends on sustained institutional commitment to maintenance and financial resources. Schools operating under budget constraints may struggle to maintain green infrastructure adequately over extended timescales.

**Scaling barriers not fully addressed:** While the project demonstrated feasibility at pilot sites, significant barriers to scaling remain. Capital cost requirements for retrofitting existing buildings, knowledge gaps in building sector, and regulatory uncertainty regarding nature-based solutions represent substantial barriers to widespread adoption.

**Limited equity analysis:** While the project served public institutions and benefited diverse student populations, the project's analysis of equity outcomes was limited. Effects on different socioeconomic groups, vulnerable populations, and distributional equity could have received greater attention.

**Displacement risks not fully analyzed:** While the project did not involve residential displacement, potential secondary effects (property value changes, gentrification pressures) in surrounding neighborhoods received limited analysis.

**Limited cost-benefit analysis:** While the project documented energy savings and operational cost reductions, comprehensive cost-benefit analysis comparing costs of implementation and maintenance to economic benefits was not fully detailed in available documentation.

**Uncertain transferability:** While the project identified lessons learned and best practices, the extent to which solutions transfer to different contexts (different climates, building types, institutional contexts) remains uncertain. Context-specific adaptations required for different locations have not been fully specified.

**Biodiversity permanence uncertain:** While the project documented substantial biodiversity colonization of green infrastructure, long-term persistence of colonizing species and sustainability of biodiversity gains remain uncertain. Extended monitoring will provide crucial data on this question.[^2][^5]

The LIFE-myBUILDINGisGREEN project, exemplified by the Falcão Primary School in Porto, represents sophisticated, well-designed demonstration of nature-based solutions for climate adaptation in educational buildings. The project's integration of rigorous scientific analysis, innovative design, participatory engagement, and policy influence distinguishes it from many isolated green infrastructure projects. Documented environmental benefits (energy savings, water management improvements, biodiversity enhancements) demonstrate substantial positive outcomes.

Extended monitoring through 2028 will provide crucial data on long-term performance trajectory and sustainability. Policy integration and regulatory change will be essential for scaling from demonstration projects to widespread adoption across building stock.

For practitioners considering similar initiatives in green building neighborhoods or other urban development contexts, the project offers valuable lessons regarding climate-specific design optimization, integrated systems approaches, rigorous monitoring and evaluation, participatory engagement, and policy engagement. Adaptation to different contexts will require attention to local climate characteristics, building types, institutional capacities, governance structures, and regulatory frameworks. The project demonstrates that nature-based solutions represent viable, effective approaches to urban climate adaptation and sustainability, while also highlighting the complexity of achieving lasting, scaled impact in urban development.
