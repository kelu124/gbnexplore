# Executive summary## Smarter Together – Sustainable Urban Districts (Lyon)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Smarter Together – Sustainable Urban Districts
- **Alternative Names:** Connected Green Neighbourhoods in Lyon, Lyon-Confluence Smart Community, Smarter Together Lyon
- **Location:** Lyon-Confluence District, Lyon, Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, France
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 45.7450° N, 4.8194° E (center of Lyon-Confluence)
- **Timeline:** 
  - EU Horizon 2020 funding period: 2016–2021
  - Demonstration phase: 2016–2021, with continued operation and monitoring post-2021
  - Key milestones: Building renovations (2016–2018), deployment of energy systems and ICT, community engagement activities (2017–2020)
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Area: 150 hectares (ha)
  - Existing floor area: 600,000 m²
  - New constructions: 1,000,000 m²
  - Population: ~15,000 residents in the demonstration area
  - Investment: €24 million (total project, of which Lyon received a significant portion)
- **Leading Organization(s):** Ville de Lyon (City of Lyon), SPL Lyon-Confluence, Métropole de Lyon, Bouygues Immobilier, local public and private partners
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - European Union (Horizon 2020)
  - City of Lyon
  - Métropole de Lyon
  - Public-private partnerships
  - National and regional funds

#### **Executive Summary**
The **Smarter Together** project in Lyon represents a flagship European effort to create connected, green, and resilient urban neighborhoods by integrating advanced energy, mobility, and digital technologies into a large-scale urban regeneration process. Concentrated in the Lyon-Confluence district—a former industrial area undergoing one of France’s largest urban redevelopments—Smarter Together has aimed to decouple economic and demographic growth from environmental impacts by targeting zero-carbon objectives even as population and built area increase[^1]. The initiative encompasses comprehensive building retrofits (35,000 m²), deployment of district-scale renewable energy solutions (including 1 MWp photovoltaic capacity and wood-fired cogeneration), and a full suite of smart mobility services such as electric car sharing, smart charging, and autonomous shuttles[^1][^2]. Advanced metering and neighborhood-scale energy management systems feed real-time data to a central urban platform, enabling both system optimization and community engagement.

A defining feature of the project has been its participatory approach: residents and stakeholders co-design smart city solutions in an urban living lab, with particular attention to social inclusion and quality of life. Despite significant implementation challenges—including integrating new technologies into aging infrastructure and motivating sustained citizen involvement—Smarter Together has realized measurable reductions in energy use and emissions, improved air quality, and established replicable models for sustainable urban transformation. The Lyon experience contributes vital insights for cities worldwide seeking to harness digitalization and stakeholder participation for climate-smart, equitable urban futures.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Lyon is France’s third-largest city, a major economic hub in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region, and a leading center for banking, biotech, and digital innovation. With a metropolitan population exceeding 1.5 million, it is strategically located at the confluence of the Rhône and Saône rivers. The city has a rich industrial history, significant cultural heritage, and has recently prioritized sustainable urban redevelopment as part of its broader metropolitan strategy.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics (Lyon-Confluence):**
- **Historical development:** Formerly an industrial and logistics zone, Lyon-Confluence has been the focus of a multi-decade transformation aiming to extend the city center, create new housing, and restore riverfront access.
- **Current land use:** Mixed-use, featuring residential, commercial, cultural, and public spaces; ongoing infill and redevelopment.
- **Demographics:** Diverse, with a mix of income groups (including social housing), a growing young professional population, and increasing international residents.
- **Infrastructure:** Legacy industrial infrastructure, now upgraded with modern transit (tram, bus), cycling and pedestrian networks, and high-quality public spaces.
- **Environmental conditions:** Bounded by two rivers, the area faces flood risks, heat island effects, and legacy pollution, but also offers significant opportunities for green and blue infrastructure.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**
- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High energy consumption and carbon emissions from aging building stock.
  - Poor air quality and urban heat island effects.
  - Traffic congestion and limited sustainable mobility options.
  - Social fragmentation and risk of gentrification amid rapid redevelopment.
  - Need for resilient, adaptable infrastructure to address climate change.

- **Triggering Events:**
  - Major urban redevelopment masterplan for Confluence.
  - EU Horizon 2020 call for smart, inclusive, and sustainable districts.
  - National and city-level commitments to climate action and energy transition.

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - City and regional authorities (urban planning, energy, mobility)
  - Private developers (Bouygues Immobilier, etc.)
  - Social housing providers (Grand Lyon Habitat)
  - Utilities, technology firms, research partners
  - Local residents, businesses, and civic organizations

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**
- **Governance Framework:**  
  The project operated under the governance of the City of Lyon and Métropole de Lyon, with SPL Lyon-Confluence as a key implementation agency.
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - French national energy transition law (LTECV)
  - Lyon’s local climate and energy plan
  - EU directives for sustainable urban development and digital innovation
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Supportive national/EU regulations for renewables and energy efficiency
  - Complexities in retrofitting protected or social housing
  - Need for data governance and privacy safeguards for smart city systems

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**
- **Primary Goals:**
  - Achieve zero-carbon growth in the Confluence district[^1]
  - Reduce energy consumption and emissions despite increased density
  - Enhance quality of life and social inclusion through participatory urbanism
  - Demonstrate scalable, replicable solutions for European cities

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To create a connected, resilient, and inclusive urban neighborhood that leverages smart technologies and citizen engagement to achieve climate neutrality and high quality of urban life.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Supports Lyon’s metropolitan masterplan, France’s climate commitments, and the EU’s Smart Cities and Communities strategy.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**
- **Theory of Change:**  
  By combining deep building retrofits, local renewable generation, advanced digital management, and participatory governance, the district can achieve significant emissions reductions, improve resource efficiency, and foster social innovation. These interventions are mutually reinforcing—data-driven management enables optimization, while citizen engagement boosts adoption and behavioral change[^1][^2][^3].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  Residents (including those in social housing), local businesses, city authorities, urban planners, and the wider metropolitan area.

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Neighborhood-scale energy monitoring and optimization
  - Integration of district heating, PV, and cogeneration
  - Smart mobility with real-time optimization and car sharing
  - Participatory living labs and digital engagement platforms

#### **Drivers and Motivations**
- **Political:**  
  Commitment to climate action and smart city leadership.
- **Economic:**  
  Urban regeneration, job creation, and investment in innovation sectors.
- **Social:**  
  Social inclusion, improved public health, and community empowerment.
- **Environmental:**  
  Emissions reduction, air quality improvement, climate resilience.
- **Technological:**  
  Advances in smart metering, energy management, EVs, and IoT.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**
- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Integrated urban regeneration, climate action, “smart city” systems, and participatory governance.
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Retrofit of 35,000 m² of existing buildings (housing, offices, public facilities)[^1]
  - Construction of new energy-positive buildings (e.g., Hikari complex)[^2]
  - District-scale deployment of PV and cogeneration, integration with district heating
  - Mobility hubs, EV charging, and shared electric vehicles
  - Creation of high-quality public spaces and green corridors

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  Sequenced: building retrofits and infrastructure upgrades first, followed by system integration, data platform deployment, and participatory activities.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**
- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Smart metering** (electricity, heat, water) and energy management systems[^1][^3]
  - **Photovoltaic (PV) systems** (1 MWp new capacity)[^1][^2]
  - **Wood-fired cogeneration plant** (2 MWe/4 MWth) linked to district heating[^1]
  - **Electric mobility:** car sharing, smart charging, autonomous shuttles[^1][^2]
  - **Data platforms:** Real-time data collection, visualization, and urban analytics[^1][^2][^3]
  - **Building management systems (BEMS/HEMS):** Optimize energy use, storage, and comfort[^2]
  - **User interfaces:** Tablets, digital dashboards for residents to monitor energy[^2]
  - **Urban living lab:** Co-design and participatory engagement tools[^1]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  Technological (smart energy, mobility, data), social (participatory design), governance (living lab), and financial (integrated EU-city-private funding).

- **Integration Approach:**  
  Technologies were integrated via a central urban data platform, enabling neighborhood-scale optimization and feedback loops between systems[^1][^3].

#### **Implementation Methodology**
- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  Public tenders for construction and ICT systems; competitive selection of private providers; partnerships with research institutions.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Urban living lab for co-design (citizens, experts, firms)[^1][^4]
  - Local caretakers as intermediaries
  - Regular community workshops and feedback events
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Resident input on building retrofits and public space design
  - Energy use visualization and comparison tools for households[^2]
  - Community management system (CMS) for collective energy planning[^2]
- **Project Management:**  
  Led by the City and SPL Lyon-Confluence, with cross-sector coordination and iterative review processes.

#### **Financing Model**
- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Lyon’s share: ~€7.5 million (EU funding), matched by city, regional, and private partners
  - Total investments across project area: over €20 million[^5]
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - ~30% EU (Horizon 2020), remainder from local government, private developers, and in-kind partner contributions
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - User fees for shared mobility, energy cost savings, potential for value capture via increased property values
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Focus on reducing operating costs (energy, mobility)
  - Knowledge transferred to future PPPs and city programs

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**
- **Technical:**  
  - Integrating new systems with legacy infrastructure[^1]
  - Achieving interoperability of digital platforms and energy networks
  - Ensuring reliability of new technologies (e.g., smart meters, EV charging optimization)[^2]
- **Financial:**  
  - Managing cost overruns and aligning diverse funding sources
- **Social and Political:**  
  - Motivating resident engagement, especially in social housing[^2][^4]
  - Maintaining inclusivity and avoiding gentrification
- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination among numerous partners and governance levels
  - Ensuring regulatory compliance and data privacy
- **Environmental:**  
  - Managing construction impacts, legacy pollution, and riverine/flood risks

#### **Adaptive Strategies**
- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Iterative piloting and feedback in living labs
  - Use of local caretakers and intermediaries to foster trust and engagement[^4]
  - Adjustments to retrofit and systems integration based on technical feedback
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Flexible phasing to resolve unforeseen technical issues
  - Tailored engagement for less-involved or skeptical resident groups[^2]
- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - High value of “target-based” appeals and comparative feedback for energy saving[^2]
  - Critical role of local leadership and continuous stakeholder interaction

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**
- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - Retrofit of 35,000 m² of buildings
  - New positive-energy building complexes (e.g., Hikari complex)[^2]
  - 1 MWp new PV capacity; wood-fired cogeneration plant[^1][^2]
  - Smart mobility hubs, charging stations, autonomous shuttle deployment[^1]
- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Enhanced energy services (district heating/cooling)
  - Electric car sharing and improved public mobility options[^1][^2]
- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - Annual GHG emissions stabilized or reduced despite increased built area and activity[^1]
  - Verified reduction in energy use in retrofitted buildings (up to 25% in some pilots)[^5]
  - High user participation in mobility and energy monitoring pilots[^2]

#### **Social Impacts**
- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Improved air quality and public health[^1]
  - Increased comfort, safety, and convenience in housing and mobility
  - Enhanced social cohesion through participatory processes[^4]
- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - Attention to social housing retrofits and inclusive engagement
  - Risk of gentrification monitored; no major displacement reported
- **Displacement and Gentrification:**  
  - Project design sought to minimize negative impacts; continued monitoring recommended
- **Social Capital:**  
  - Strengthened local networks, ongoing civic involvement
- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Preservation of industrial heritage, integration of public art and green spaces

#### **Economic Impacts**
- **Job Creation:**  
  - Direct construction, retrofitting, and ICT employment
  - Indirect stimulus to green tech and smart city sectors[^5]
- **Economic Activity:**  
  - Growth in local business, increased investment attractiveness
- **Property Values:**  
  - Likely increases, especially for high-performance, well-connected properties
- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Documented reductions in energy bills for participating households[^2]
- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - Efforts to ensure access for social housing and lower-income groups

#### **Environmental Impacts**
- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - GHG stabilization despite growth; reductions in per-capita emissions[^1]
- **Resource Management:**  
  - Improved energy and water efficiency via smart monitoring[^2]
- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - Better air quality, reduced noise, expanded green space
- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - Focus on sustainable construction, recycling, and legacy site remediation
- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - Performance tracked against EU and national standards, with regular reporting[^3]

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**
- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Enhanced city capabilities in smart urban management
- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Lyon’s model influenced policy dialogue at city, national, and EU levels[^1][^3]
- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - Open data platforms increased transparency; participatory governance improved accountability

#### **Performance Against Objectives**
- **Success Metrics:**  
  - Most energy, emissions, and mobility targets met or exceeded[^1][^2]
- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Difficulty sustaining resident engagement post-demonstration
  - Complexity in upscaling some solutions citywide
- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Strong prospects due to integrated systems and institutional learning

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**
- **Contextual Dependencies:**  
  - Large-scale, centrally located redevelopment area with public leadership was key; some elements less transferable to polycentric or fragmented urban settings.
- **Critical Success Factors:**  
  - Strong public leadership and cross-sector partnerships
  - Availability of EU and city funding
  - Robust digital/data infrastructure
- **Scalability:**  
  - Core solutions (energy retrofits, smart mobility, participatory labs) are scalable, but require adaptation to local governance and market conditions

#### **Comparative Analysis**
- **Similar Initiatives:**  
  - Smarter Together sister projects in Munich and Vienna[^5][^4]
  - Other EU “lighthouse cities” in the Smart Cities Marketplace
- **Best Practices:**  
  - Integrated retrofits, neighborhood-scale energy management, participatory urbanism
- **Cautionary Lessons:**  
  - Need for sustained engagement beyond initial pilots
  - Risks of technological “solutionism” without social buy-in

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- **Integrated approach:** Combined spatial, technological, and social innovation for holistic impact.
- **Strong outcomes:** Demonstrated reductions in energy use and emissions; improved quality of life.
- **Replicability:** Developed scalable models for other European cities.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- **Sustained engagement:** Difficulty in maintaining high levels of resident participation over time[^2][^4].
- **Complexity:** High coordination demands and interoperability challenges for new and legacy systems.
- **Gentrification risk:** Need for ongoing vigilance to ensure social inclusion.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- **Positive steps:** Inclusive retrofits, participatory design, focus on social housing.
- **Remaining gaps:** Maintaining affordability and preventing displacement as district attractiveness increases.

#### **Future Outlook**
- **Long-term trajectory:** Continued monitoring and scaling of solutions; embedding learnings into citywide policy.
- **Ongoing challenges:** Ensuring financial sustainability, adapting governance for continued innovation, expanding engagement.
- **Next phases:** Wider rollout of energy and mobility solutions, leveraging data for continuous improvement, and further strengthening social inclusion.
