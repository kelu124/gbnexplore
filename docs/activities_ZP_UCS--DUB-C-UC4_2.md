# Executive summary## Aarhus Smart Building Elevator Usage Analytics

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Aarhus Smart Building Elevator Usage Analytics
- **Alternative Names:** Aarhus Smart City Elevator Optimization; Green Lift (specific intervention within the broader smart building context)[^1].
- **Location:** Aarhus, Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 56.1629° N, 10.2039° E (central Aarhus)
- **Timeline:**  
  - Early pilot phase: c. 2012 (as part of broader smart building research infrastructure)[^1].  
  - Green Lift intervention: 1-month trial in 2014–2015[^1].  
  - Ongoing smart city program integration: 2012–present[^2].
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Building-scale: Grundfos Dormitory Lab (test bed with 3,000+ sensors, 100+ apartments, ~200 residents)[^1].  
  - City-scale: Integration with Aarhus municipal buildings (multiple sites involved in the broader Smart Aarhus strategy)[^2].
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - Aarhus Municipality (Smart Aarhus unit)[^2].  
  - Aarhus University (CITIES project, VPP4SGR project in collaboration)[^1].  
  - Grundfos (Dormitory Lab, technology partner)[^1].
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - Public sector (municipality, national R&D funding)[^1][^2].  
  - EU research grants (e.g., ESPON, Horizon 2020)[^2].  
  - Private partnership (Grundfos, tech suppliers)[^1].

#### **Executive Summary**
Aarhus, Denmark, has positioned itself as a leading European city in the implementation of smart building technologies, with a particular focus on optimizing elevator usage in municipal and residential buildings. The Aarhus Smart Building Elevator Usage Analytics initiative leverages IoT sensors, real-time data collection, and cloud-based analytics to monitor elevator trips, usage patterns, and downtime. Facility managers use this data to identify inefficiencies, inform targeted maintenance, and reduce unnecessary energy consumption.

A flagship intervention, known as “Green Lift,” was piloted in the Grundfos Dormitory Lab—a living lab equipped with thousands of sensors and advanced energy management systems. This project sought not only to measure but also to actively influence resident behavior, using visual prompts (LED indicators) to encourage stair use during periods of high carbon-intensity electricity. Outcomes included improved awareness of energy sustainability among residents, modest shifts in behavior, and valuable insights into the challenges of integrating advanced analytics with legacy elevator systems.

The initiative is embedded within the city’s broader Smart Aarhus strategy, which coordinates digitalization and sustainability actions across municipal departments. The project exemplifies Aarhus’s commitment to carbon neutrality, digital transformation, and participatory governance. It demonstrates the feasibility and limitations of data-driven, user-engagement approaches to urban sustainability, providing scalable lessons for smart building management in other European cities[^1][^2].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Aarhus is Denmark’s second-largest city, with a population of ~350,000, a robust knowledge economy anchored by the university, and a strong tradition of public-private collaboration. It sits on the Jutland peninsula, featuring a temperate coastal climate, and is recognized as a regional center for innovation, education, and sustainable urban development[^3].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - **Grundfos Dormitory Lab** is located in a mixed-use urban district with a predominance of modern residential complexes.
  - **Historical Patterns:** Aarhus has seen rapid urban growth, densification, and a shift toward mixed-use, sustainable developments over the last two decades.
  - **Current Land Use:** Primarily residential with some educational and commercial facilities.
  - **Demographics:** Young, diverse, with high student population; income levels above national average; international residents due to academic presence.
  - **Infrastructure Quality:** High, with widespread district heating, smart grid pilots, and modern building stock; accessibility is generally excellent[^3].
  - **Environmental Conditions:** Commitment to carbon neutrality; district heating from renewable sources; citywide emphasis on green public spaces[^3].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Inefficient elevator operations leading to unnecessary energy consumption and peak load issues[^1].
  - Lack of actionable data on building system performance.
  - Low occupant awareness regarding the environmental impact of elevator use.
  - Legacy equipment limiting integration with new digital systems.
- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Adoption of the Smart Aarhus Strategy (2012–2014) and city’s carbon-neutral targets for 2030[^2].
  - National and EU smart city funding opportunities.
  - Availability of a sensor-rich “living lab” environment at the Grundfos Dormitory[^1].
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Municipality (policy, funding, oversight).
  - University/researchers (technical design, monitoring, evaluation).
  - Technology partners (hardware/software).
  - Residents/students (end-users, co-creators in behavioral interventions).

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Smart Aarhus unit as cross-departmental coordinator[^2].
  - Policy alignment with national energy and digitalization strategies.
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Aarhus Smart City Action Plan.
  - National building energy performance regulations.
  - EU directives on smart grids and digital infrastructure.
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Data privacy laws (GDPR) required careful handling of occupant data.
  - Public procurement rules shaped technology selection.
  - Institutional support facilitated integration, but legacy systems presented technical constraints.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions from elevator use.
  - Improve reliability and efficiency of building elevator systems.
  - Empower facility managers and occupants with actionable performance data.
- **Vision Statement:**  
  Enable smarter, more sustainable buildings through data-driven management and user engagement—contributing to Aarhus’s status as a leading green and digital city.
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Fully integrated with Aarhus’s citywide aim to be carbon-neutral by 2030 and a European model for smart urbanism[^2].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  - Install sensors and analytics to generate granular elevator usage data.
  - Analyze data to identify inefficiencies and peak demand periods.
  - Deploy behavioral interventions (e.g., Green Lift LED prompts) to nudge users toward more sustainable choices.
  - Use insights to optimize maintenance schedules and reduce downtime.
- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Building occupants (improved experience, reliability, awareness).
  - Facility managers (data-driven decision-making, cost savings).
  - City and environment (reduced emissions, lower energy demand).
- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Real-time IoT monitoring in legacy buildings.
  - Behavioral “nudge” interventions based on carbon intensity data.
  - Integration of elevator analytics with broader building management systems[^1].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Commitment to sustainability, smart city leadership, and public sector innovation.
- **Economic:** Energy cost savings; maintenance optimization.
- **Social:** Enhanced occupant experience, participatory engagement, health benefits of stair use.
- **Environmental:** Climate targets, resource efficiency, demand response integration.
- **Technological:** Exploiting IoT, cloud analytics, and behavioral science in operational settings.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - “Living lab” experimentation: real-world testing of digital technologies in active buildings.
  - Data-driven management: continuous monitoring and feedback loops.
  - User-centric design: behavioral interventions to complement technical measures[^1].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Retrofitting of legacy elevator systems with IoT sensors.
  - LED indicators and posters in elevator lobbies for behavioral interventions.
  - Centralized analytics dashboard for facility managers.
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial sensor deployment and baseline data collection.
  - Pilot intervention (Green Lift) with user prompts and monitoring.
  - Ongoing refinement and citywide scaling.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Wireless ZigBee sensor network for elevator, door, and common area monitoring.
  - Cloud-based data analytics and visualization platform.
  - LED visual indicators linked to real-time carbon intensity of grid electricity.
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (IoT, analytics).
  - Social (behavioral nudges).
  - Process (integrated facility management).
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Sensors and analytics added atop existing building infrastructure.
  - Data streams fed into building management systems and used for both reporting and real-time interventions[^1].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Public procurement for hardware/software; academic partnership for research and evaluation.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Residents involved in “co-creation” workshops; regular feedback cycles.
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Behavioral interventions designed with resident input; direct feedback on intervention effectiveness[^1].
- **Project Management:**  
  - Cross-sectoral steering group (municipality, university, building operators).
  - Agile process for piloting, monitoring, and scaling.

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Not publicly itemized; estimated mid-6-figure EUR for pilot phase, including sensors, software, personnel, and evaluation.
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Public sector (municipality, national agencies); EU research funding; private sector in-kind contributions.
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Cost savings from reduced energy/maintenance; no direct user fees.
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Long-term cost recovery through operational savings and potential for citywide scaling.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Retrofitting sensors into legacy elevator systems with limited digital interfaces.
  - Ensuring reliable data transmission in multi-story buildings.
- **Financial:**  
  - High upfront investment for sensor networks; unclear payback period.
- **Social and Political:**  
  - Privacy concerns over monitoring occupant movements.
  - Resident skepticism about behavioral interventions.
- **Administrative:**  
  - Coordination across city departments and building managers.
- **Environmental:**  
  - Minimal negative impacts; focus was on reducing environmental footprint.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Close collaboration with technology partners for retrofitting.
  - Transparent communication with residents on data privacy and goals.
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Iterative refinement of LED prompts and feedback mechanisms based on resident responses.
- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Direct behavioral prompts can motivate change, but require clear communication.
  - Technical integration is more complex in older buildings; early stakeholder involvement is critical[^1].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - Installation of 3,000+ sensors across building systems; elevator-specific monitoring in Grundfos Dormitory[^1].
- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Real-time reporting dashboards for facility managers.
- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - Elevator accounts for 4% of building electricity use; 7% for shared services (elevator, laundry, ventilation)[^1].

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Improved awareness of sustainability concepts among residents.
  - Enhanced reliability and user satisfaction for elevators.
- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - Benefits distributed equally among residents; no evidence of displacement or exclusion.
- **Social Capital:**  
  - Strengthened collaboration between residents, operators, and city officials.
- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Helped foster a culture of sustainability and digital innovation.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  - Temporary jobs in installation, analytics, and project management.
- **Economic Activity:**  
  - Increased demand for smart building technology services.
- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Lowered energy bills and reduced elevator maintenance costs.
- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - No direct evidence of new income opportunities, but indirect benefits from lower operating costs.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - Incremental reduction in building carbon footprint.
- **Resource Management:**  
  - Improved energy efficiency; data-driven demand response potential explored[^1].
- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - No direct change in air/noise quality; indirect benefits through lower energy demand.
- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - No significant impact reported.
- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - In line with city’s carbon-neutral trajectory.

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Enhanced capacity for data-driven facility management within municipal government.
- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Provided empirical evidence to support further smart city investments.
- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - Open reporting and participatory evaluation with stakeholders.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  - Achieved real-time data collection, improved reliability, and increased awareness.
  - Direct energy savings from behavioral intervention (Green Lift) were modest—no significant reduction in elevator electricity use, but increased stair use was noted[^1].
- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Some residents confused by interventions that sometimes encouraged elevator use (when grid was “green”)[^1].
- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Platform and lessons scalable across city’s building stock.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Strong integration of technology and behavioral science.
- Embedded in a comprehensive, citywide smart city strategy.
- Demonstrated positive impacts on occupant awareness and operational efficiency.
- Provided a replicable model for data-driven building management.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Technical integration with legacy systems proved challenging.
- Modest direct energy savings from behavioral interventions; some user confusion.
- Upfront costs and uncertain payback in certain contexts.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- The initiative promoted inclusive benefits and avoided negative distributional impacts.
- Privacy was carefully managed; participatory methods helped ensure equity.

#### **Future Outlook**

- Continued scaling across Aarhus municipal buildings.
- Further refinement of behavioral interventions and analytics.
- Potential integration with broader smart city systems (e.g., district energy, emergency management).
- Ongoing need for user education and addressing technical integration challenges as the building stock evolves.
