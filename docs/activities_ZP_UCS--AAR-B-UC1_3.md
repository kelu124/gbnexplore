# Executive summary

## CO-SHAPE – Peri-Urban Circular Energy Hub

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** CO-SHAPE (Co-Shaping Areas in Peri-Urban Environments)
- **Alternative Names:** CO-SHAPE Pilot Project, Energy Park Spørring
- **Location:** Spørring, Aarhus (Aarhus Municipality), Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Peri-urban area on the northern edge of Aarhus
- **Timeline:** Pilot phase 2024-2027 (postdoc research project component); ongoing implementation with learnings compilation and knowledge transfer activities
- **Project Scale:** 1,000 hectares in the Spørring peri-urban area; serves a city of 349,873 inhabitants (Denmark's second-largest city)
- **Leading Organization(s):** Aarhus Municipality (primary lead), Aarhus School of Architecture (research partner), with collaboration involving citizens, private developers, energy operators, and architects
- **Funding Sources:** EU's Pilot Cities Programme (NetZeroCities facilitation); builds on postdoc research project funding and integration of experiences from previous projects (REGREEN 2019-2023, Invest4Nature 2022-2026, MOSAIC 2018-2020)

#### **Executive Summary**
CO-SHAPE represents a transformative approach to peri-urban energy transition that extends far beyond technological innovation to fundamentally reimagine how cities make decisions about complex infrastructure development.[^1][^2] The initiative transforms Spørring, a village on Aarhus's northern periphery, into a circular energy park integrating biogas production, biochar generation, grass protein production, waste heat recovery, solar power, and future Power-to-X technologies across 1,000 hectares.[^1][^2] The project's defining characteristic is its emphasis on governance innovation and participatory planning, bringing together citizens, landowners, developers, and municipal staff in "solution rooms" where stakeholders negotiate trade-offs collaboratively from the project's inception rather than at the end of planning processes.[^2] This approach directly addresses what project leaders identify as the primary barrier to climate transition: not technology, but governance.[^2] The initiative is designed to reduce emissions by over 100,000 tonnes of CO₂ annually—approximately 10% of Aarhus's total emissions—while simultaneously pioneering replicable governance models for integrated spatial planning and stakeholder engagement applicable to similar peri-urban contexts across Europe.[^1][^2] Rather than attempting to solve all challenges within the two-year pilot timeframe, CO-SHAPE positions itself as a proof of concept for demonstrating that co-creation can work in complex, high-stakes situations involving multiple stakeholders with competing interests, with the ultimate goal of institutionalizing these practices beyond the pilot period.[^2]

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile**

Aarhus is Denmark's second-largest city with a population of 349,873 inhabitants.[^1] The city occupies an exceptional geographic and cultural position within Denmark and Northern Europe, characterized by a vibrant student population and university culture, ancient architecture interwoven with forests, agricultural land, and water bodies.[^2] This integration of urban settlement with natural surroundings contributes to what is often described as an exceptionally high quality of life, positioning Aarhus as a leader in environmental and smart city rankings.[^2] The city's economy is diversified, with significant academic and research institutions anchoring knowledge-based sectors alongside traditional industries and services. Aarhus's location on the Jutland Peninsula provides access to both coastal and rural resources, creating a distinctive peri-urban landscape that blends urban, agricultural, and natural systems.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics**

Spørring is a peri-urban village on Aarhus's northern edge, positioned in the transitional zone between the dense urban core and the rural hinterland.[^2] This peri-urban typology is characterized by several distinctive features: it experiences the dynamic tension between urban expansion and agricultural preservation; it contains multi-functional land use including farms, open space, and emerging residential development; and it faces the complexity of managing infrastructure needs for a dispersed population while maintaining landscape character.[^1] The area exhibits the typical challenges of peri-urban zones: decreasing property values in some areas due to proximity to industrial facilities, unequal distribution of land uses with some neighborhoods positioned near energy or agricultural infrastructure while others maintain primarily residential character, and the friction between densifying urban expansion and preservation of agricultural and natural systems.[^1] Historically, Spørring has maintained agricultural and village character, but increasing urbanization pressure and the municipality's climate goals have positioned it as an ideal location for testing integrated energy and land-use solutions.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified**

Aarhus faced multiple interconnected barriers to achieving its ambitious goal of climate neutrality by 2030.[^1] These challenges extended beyond technological deficits to encompass governance, social acceptance, and institutional capacity:

- **Governance Deficits:** Insufficient governance tools for comprehensive planning that integrated energy, land use, landscape, and architectural considerations; municipal departments operated in silos with limited cross-departmental cooperation on energy planning, business involvement, and spatial development.[^1]
- **Social Acceptance:** Residents' reluctance to live near or accept energy projects in their proximity, manifesting as "Not in My Backyard" (NIMBY) opposition to renewable energy facilities, biogas plants, and other infrastructure perceived as aesthetically unpleasant, noisy, or odorous.[^1][^2]
- **Institutional Capacity:** Lack of competencies for cross-departmental cooperation in energy planning; limited experience with integrated approaches that simultaneously addressed energy production, agricultural productivity, landscape quality, and community well-being.[^1]
- **Planning Fragmentation:** Traditional planning processes where developers submitted proposals, cities reviewed them, and public consultation occurred largely as an afterthought, rather than participatory processes involving stakeholders from inception.[^2]

**Triggering Events**

Aarhus's commitment to achieving climate neutrality by 2030—established over a decade prior—provided the strategic impetus for seeking innovative solutions.[^1][^3] The city's Climate Action Plan (CAP21-24) and the strategic shift in 2023 toward "more radical interventions for climate neutrality by 2030" created urgency and openness to experimental governance approaches.[^1] The availability of EU funding through the NetZeroCities Pilot Cities Programme provided both resources and external validation for testing new models, creating a window of opportunity to pilot integrated approaches in a contained geographic area before scaling.

**Stakeholder Landscape**

Before the initiative, stakeholders operated in largely separate spheres with competing interests: municipal departments (energy, planning, agriculture, environment) worked independently; farmers and landowners prioritized agricultural productivity and land values; developers focused on project economics and regulatory compliance; energy operators sought sites for infrastructure; and residents were primarily positioned as subjects of planning decisions rather than co-creators.[^1][^2] This fragmentation meant that energy transition planning proceeded without adequate consideration of landscape impacts, agricultural opportunities, or community concerns, while agricultural planning ignored energy possibilities, and land use planning lacked integration with climate goals.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework**

Aarhus Municipality operates within Denmark's decentralized governance system, where municipalities hold substantial authority over land use planning, building regulation, and local climate action.[^1][^3] The city has established itself as a leader in climate governance through explicit commitment to climate neutrality by 2030 and integration of sustainability considerations across municipal functions. The municipality's administrative structure historically organized energy, planning, agriculture, and environment as separate departments, reflecting traditional siloed governance patterns common across European municipalities.

**Relevant Policies**

The CO-SHAPE initiative operates within and seeks to reshape several policy frameworks:

- **Climate Action Plans (CAP21-24 and beyond):** Aarhus's municipal climate strategy provides the overarching mandate for emissions reductions and renewable energy integration.[^1]
- **Comprehensive Planning Framework:** Danish planning law requires municipalities to develop comprehensive plans for spatial development; CO-SHAPE aims to pioneer new approaches to comprehensive planning that integrate energy, landscape, and social dimensions.[^1]
- **Renewable Energy Policy:** Danish national policy supports renewable energy development, but implementation at the local level often encounters social resistance and coordination challenges that CO-SHAPE addresses.[^1]
- **Circular Economy Principles:** Broader Danish policy trends toward circular economy (evidenced by initiatives like Circle House) inform CO-SHAPE's design of closed-loop systems for energy and agricultural outputs.[^4][^5]

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers**

**Enablers:** Danish legal frameworks provide municipalities with substantial planning authority and flexibility to experiment with governance innovations; EU funding mechanisms through NetZeroCities create resources and legitimacy for pilot projects; the strong Danish tradition of consensus-building and stakeholder engagement provides cultural support for participatory approaches; and existing postdoc research at Aarhus School of Architecture provided intellectual foundation and credibility.[^1]

**Barriers:** Traditional municipal organizational structures and departmental boundaries create institutional inertia; existing planning procedures and permitting processes were designed for conventional development pathways rather than integrated co-creation; regulatory frameworks for energy infrastructure, agriculture, and land use operate under different legal regimes requiring coordination; and limited precedent for the governance innovations CO-SHAPE proposes meant uncertainty about legal viability and institutional acceptance.[^1][^2]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals**

CO-SHAPE pursues multiple interconnected objectives operating at different scales:[^1][^2]

1. **Energy Transition:** Establish a functional circular energy park reducing emissions by over 100,000 tonnes CO₂ annually (approximately 10% of Aarhus's total emissions) through integrated renewable energy sources and closed-loop systems.[^2]
2. **Governance Innovation:** Pioneer comprehensive energy planning as a governance tool that facilitates increased local renewable energy, efficient land use, and holistic sector coupling.[^1]
3. **Institutional Change:** Demonstrate and institutionalize cross-departmental cooperation within municipal government, breaking down silos between energy, planning, agriculture, and environment departments.[^1][^2]
4. **Social Acceptance:** Develop and validate participatory processes that transform NIMBY opposition into stakeholder buy-in through genuine co-creation and shared decision-making.[^1][^2]
5. **Replicability:** Create transferable models and playbooks applicable to similar peri-urban contexts across Europe.[^1]

**Vision Statement**

CO-SHAPE envisions peri-urban areas transformed from zones of conflict between urban expansion and agricultural preservation into integrated spaces where energy production, food/feed generation, landscape quality, and community well-being advance simultaneously through deliberative governance processes that distribute decision-making power among public, private, and civil society actors.[^1][^2]

**Alignment with Broader Plans**

The initiative directly supports Aarhus's commitment to climate neutrality by 2030 and aligns with the city's Climate Action Plan emphasis on radical interventions.[^1][^3] It operationalizes the European Green Deal's objectives for climate neutrality and circular economy principles through concrete demonstration. The project builds on and integrates learnings from previous initiatives (REGREEN, Invest4Nature, MOSAIC) that addressed nature-based solutions, stakeholder engagement, and co-creation methodologies.[^1] At the EU level, CO-SHAPE contributes to the NetZeroCities mission's goal of supporting climate-neutral and smart cities through innovative governance and planning approaches.[^6]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change**

CO-SHAPE's theory of change operates on the insight that technological solutions to climate transition are necessary but insufficient; governance innovation and social acceptance are equally critical barriers.[^2] The causal pathway proceeds as follows:

1. **Diagnosis:** Municipal departments work in silos → fragmented planning → missed opportunities for integrated solutions and community alienation
2. **Intervention:** Establish cross-departmental "solution rooms" and participatory co-creation processes bringing together diverse stakeholders from project inception
3. **Mechanism:** Inclusive deliberation → shared understanding of trade-offs → negotiated solutions reflecting multiple values → institutional learning → behavioral and cultural change
4. **Outcomes:** Integrated energy park design → emissions reduction; transformed governance processes → replicable institutional models; community co-ownership → social acceptance and long-term sustainability
5. **Impact:** Proof of concept for governance innovation → scaling to other peri-urban areas and cities → systemic transformation of how municipalities approach energy transition

The theory explicitly recognizes that transformation requires time, acceptance of risk, and willingness to adapt based on experience rather than rigid adherence to predetermined plans.[^2]

**Target Beneficiaries**

- **Direct Beneficiaries:** Residents of Spørring and surrounding peri-urban areas who benefit from local energy production, improved landscape design, economic opportunities, and genuine voice in decisions affecting their community
- **Indirect Beneficiaries:** Aarhus citizens through emissions reductions and demonstrated pathway to climate neutrality; Danish municipalities and European cities through transferable governance models and planning methodologies
- **Systemic Beneficiaries:** Future generations through demonstrated integration of climate action with quality of life and social inclusion

#### **Innovation Elements**

CO-SHAPE's innovations operate across multiple dimensions:[^1][^2]

**Governance Innovation:** The "solution room" model represents a fundamental departure from conventional planning processes. Rather than sequential phases (developer proposal → municipal review → public consultation), solution rooms bring all parties together from inception to negotiate trade-offs continuously. This extends both externally (involving citizens, landowners, developers, energy operators) and internally (breaking municipal silos through cross-departmental collaboration).[^2]

**Integrated Spatial Planning:** The comprehensive planning approach simultaneously addresses energy production, agricultural productivity, landscape architecture, recreational opportunity, and community well-being as interconnected rather than competing objectives.[^1][^2]

**Sector Coupling:** The circular energy park design couples previously separate systems—biogas production from agricultural waste, grass protein production, biochar generation, waste heat recovery, and future Power-to-X technologies—so outputs from one process become inputs for another, creating systemic efficiency and resilience.[^2]

**Participatory Governance:** The explicit integration of citizen co-creation as a requirement in renewable energy projects, now embedded in Aarhus's Climate Action Plan and made mandatory by local politicians, represents institutionalization of participatory approaches.[^2]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers**

Aarhus's political leadership has made climate neutrality by 2030 an explicit municipal commitment, creating political incentive for demonstrating progress and innovation.[^1][^3] The city's positioning as a leader in environmental rankings creates political motivation to maintain and advance this reputation. EU funding through NetZeroCities provided both resources and external validation for experimental approaches, reducing political risk of innovation.

**Economic Drivers**

The circular energy park model creates economic opportunities through local energy production (reducing energy imports and associated costs), agricultural valorization (converting waste into valuable products), job creation in renewable energy and circular economy sectors, and potential cost reductions through sector coupling efficiencies.[^1][^2] The integration of renewable energy with agricultural productivity creates multiple revenue streams rather than single-purpose energy infrastructure, improving project economics.

**Social Drivers**

Community desire for genuine voice in decisions affecting their neighborhoods; concern about quality of life impacts from industrial facilities; interest in local economic development and employment; and broader cultural values in Denmark emphasizing consensus-building and participatory democracy all motivated the co-creation approach.[^2] The explicit framing of NIMBY concerns as legitimate issues to be addressed through dialogue rather than dismissed as obstruction reflects respect for community agency.

**Environmental Drivers**

Climate change urgency and Aarhus's commitment to climate neutrality by 2030 provide overarching environmental motivation.[^1][^2] The opportunity to demonstrate that energy transition can simultaneously improve landscape quality, enhance agricultural productivity through circular systems, and preserve peri-urban character (rather than requiring sacrifice of these values) motivates the integrated approach.[^2]

**Technological Drivers**

The maturation of renewable energy technologies (biogas, solar, Power-to-X), waste valorization techniques (biochar production), and digital platforms for stakeholder engagement and data management enabled the technical feasibility of the proposed systems.[^2]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework**

CO-SHAPE draws on multiple planning and design philosophies:[^1][^2]

- **Landscape Urbanism:** Integration of landscape architecture with urban planning to create multifunctional spaces serving energy, agricultural, recreational, and ecological functions simultaneously
- **Circular Economy Principles:** Design of closed-loop systems where waste from one process becomes input for another, minimizing extraction and disposal
- **Participatory/Deliberative Democracy:** Emphasis on inclusive decision-making processes that distribute power among diverse stakeholders rather than concentrating it in government or private developers
- **Differentiated Urban Typology Approach:** Recognition that peri-urban areas require distinct planning strategies different from dense urban cores or rural areas, tailored to their specific characteristics and challenges
- **Systems Thinking:** Integration of energy, agriculture, landscape, community, and economic systems as interconnected rather than separate domains
- **Wicked Problems Framework:** Recognition that complex societal challenges like climate transition cannot be solved through technical means alone but require ongoing dialogue, negotiation, and adaptive management[^2]

**Physical/Spatial Design**

The Energy Park Spørring design integrates multiple technologies and land uses across 1,000 hectares:[^1][^2]

- **Biogas Production:** Conversion of organic agricultural residues (farm waste) into biogas for energy generation, utilizing existing biogas plant infrastructure
- **Biochar Facility:** Pyrolysis plant converting leftover organic material into biochar, a substance that stores carbon and improves soil health when applied to agricultural fields
- **Grass Protein Production:** Processing grass into green animal feed and potentially human food, creating agricultural value from grasslands
- **Waste Heat Recovery:** Capture and reuse of thermal energy from biogas and other processes to reduce overall energy demand
- **Solar Power:** Integration of photovoltaic systems across appropriate locations in the park
- **Power-to-X Technologies:** Future installations combining captured CO₂ from biogas production with green hydrogen from offshore wind to produce sustainable fuels (e-fuels) for aviation and shipping[^2]

The spatial design emphasizes landscape quality and multifunctionality: facilities are designed and landscaped to minimize visual and olfactory impacts on surrounding communities; recreational and ecological functions are integrated with energy production; and the layout facilitates closed-loop material flows where outputs from one facility become inputs for others.

**Scope and Phasing**

The project is structured in phases:[^1][^2]

- **Phase 1 (2024-2027):** Pilot demonstration in Spørring, including co-creation workshops, technical energy planning, landscape architectural scenario-building, comprehensive planning, and institutional change within municipality
- **Phase 2:** Compilation of learnings and development of transferable playbooks and governance models
- **Phase 3 (Ongoing):** Scaling and replication in neighboring municipalities (already successfully replicated in 5 neighboring municipalities according to preliminary reports[^1]) and other European cities

Rather than attempting to fully resolve all challenges within the pilot phase, the project accepts that transformation requires extended timeframes and treats the pilot as a proof of concept for demonstrating feasibility of the governance and planning innovations.[^2]

**Innovation Type**

CO-SHAPE innovations span multiple categories:

- **Technological Innovation:** Integration of multiple renewable energy and circular economy technologies into a coherent system; Power-to-X applications for hard-to-decarbonize sectors
- **Social Innovation:** Co-creation and participatory governance models; "solution rooms" for stakeholder negotiation; integration of citizen agency into infrastructure planning
- **Governance Innovation:** Cross-departmental integration within municipalities; institutional mechanisms for breaking silos; reflexive governance models[^1]
- **Financial Innovation:** Circular business models where multiple revenue streams (energy sales, agricultural products, biochar, potential e-fuels) improve project economics compared to single-purpose energy infrastructure
- **Spatial Planning Innovation:** Comprehensive planning integrating energy, agriculture, landscape, and community as interconnected rather than competing objectives[^1]

**Integration Approach**

Technologies are integrated through:

1. **Material Flows:** Waste streams from one process become inputs for another (agricultural residues → biogas; biogas residue → biochar; grass processing byproducts → animal feed)
2. **Energy Flows:** Waste heat from biogas production is captured and reused; renewable electricity from solar and biogas powers other processes
3. **Spatial Organization:** Facilities are physically arranged to facilitate efficient material and energy flows while maintaining landscape quality
4. **Governance Integration:** Cross-departmental coordination ensures that energy planning, agricultural development, landscape design, and community engagement proceed as integrated process rather than sequential or parallel independent activities[^2]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Stakeholder Engagement**

The participatory approach represents a fundamental departure from conventional planning:[^2]

- **Solution Rooms:** Spaces where city staff, citizens, landowners, and developers work through the "messy realities" of large-scale infrastructure projects, characterized by trust, informal dialogue, and shared understanding rather than heavy bureaucracy
- **Co-Creation Workshops:** Structured engagement sessions involving diverse stakeholders in scenario development, trade-off negotiation, and solution generation
- **Continuous Dialogue:** Rather than discrete consultation phases, ongoing engagement throughout project development allows stakeholders to shape design evolution
- **Internal Municipal Solution Rooms:** Cross-departmental working groups within municipality addressing typically separate topics (energy, planning, agriculture, environment) to generate integrated discussions and break institutional silos[^2]
- **External Stakeholder Engagement:** Involvement of energy operators, private developers, farmers, landowners, and residents in negotiating project design and implementation approaches

**Participatory Elements**

The project emphasizes genuine power-sharing rather than token consultation:[^1][^2]

- Stakeholders participate from project inception rather than after major decisions are made
- Multiple scenarios are developed collaboratively, allowing stakeholders to understand trade-offs and express preferences
- Landscape architectural visualizations and scenario-building tools make abstract planning concepts tangible and discussable
- Satisfaction metrics demonstrate success: 85% satisfaction rate among participating stakeholders; 40% increase in public trust toward municipal decision-making processes; 60% decrease in NIMBY sentiments[^1]

**Project Management**

Martin Kaae Riis leads the CO-SHAPE project in Aarhus Municipality, providing consistent leadership and coordination.[^2] Project management structures include:

- Coordination between municipality departments (energy, planning, agriculture, environment)
- Integration with Aarhus School of Architecture research team
- Regular engagement with external stakeholders through solution rooms and workshops
- Adaptive management allowing design modifications based on stakeholder input and technical learnings
- Documentation and knowledge compilation for transferability to other contexts

#### **Financing Model**

**Funding Sources**

CO-SHAPE is funded through:

- **EU Pilot Cities Programme (NetZeroCities):** Primary funding source providing resources for pilot demonstration and knowledge transfer
- **Postdoc Research Project:** Aarhus School of Architecture research component (April 2024-March 2027) examining sustainability transition of cities and urban areas[^1]
- **Municipal Budget:** Aarhus Municipality contributes staff time, coordination, and administrative resources
- **Private Sector Investment:** Energy operators, developers, and farmers contribute capital and operational expertise for specific components (biogas plant, solar installations, agricultural facilities)
- **Integration of Previous Project Learnings:** CO-SHAPE builds on and leverages experiences from REGREEN (2019-2023), Invest4Nature (2022-2026), and MOSAIC (2018-2020), reducing need for foundational research[^1]

**Revenue Mechanisms and Financial Sustainability**

The circular energy park model creates multiple revenue streams improving financial viability:[^2]

- **Energy Sales:** Revenue from biogas and solar electricity generation
- **Agricultural Products:** Income from grass protein, biochar, and other valorized agricultural outputs
- **Future e-Fuels:** Potential revenue from Power-to-X sustainable fuel production for aviation and shipping
- **Sector Coupling Efficiencies:** Cost reductions through waste heat recovery and integrated systems reduce operational expenses
- **Land Value:** Improved landscape design and multifunctional use may enhance surrounding land values, potentially captured through tax mechanisms or developer contributions

Long-term financial sustainability depends on stable revenue from energy and agricultural products, which requires functional markets for renewable energy and circular economy products. The project's design as a circular system with multiple revenue streams provides greater resilience than single-purpose energy infrastructure.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Governance and Institutional Challenges**

The most significant challenges relate to governance innovation rather than technology:[^2]

- **Departmental Silos:** Breaking down institutional barriers between municipal departments accustomed to independent operation proved more difficult than anticipated, requiring sustained effort to establish cross-departmental solution rooms and collaborative decision-making[^2]
- **Competing Departmental Interests:** Energy, planning, agriculture, and environment departments operate under different mandates, timelines, and performance metrics, making coordination challenging
- **Bureaucratic Procedures:** Existing municipal permitting, approval, and planning procedures were designed for conventional development pathways and required adaptation to accommodate participatory co-creation processes
- **Institutional Inertia:** Resistance from established bureaucratic structures and personnel accustomed to conventional planning approaches; uncertainty about legal viability of innovative governance models

**Social and Political Challenges**

- **NIMBY Opposition:** Initial resident reluctance regarding proximity to biogas plants, agricultural processing facilities, and other infrastructure perceived as aesthetically unpleasant, noisy, or odorous, requiring sustained dialogue and landscape design solutions to transform opposition into acceptance
- **Stakeholder Alignment:** Diverse stakeholders (farmers, developers, energy operators, residents, municipal staff) bring competing interests and priorities; finding negotiated solutions required extensive dialogue and willingness to modify designs
- **Political Support Variability:** Municipal political support for experimental governance approaches can shift with electoral cycles; maintaining political commitment through implementation requires ongoing engagement with elected officials
- **Equity Concerns:** Risk that benefits (jobs, energy, agricultural products) might concentrate among certain stakeholders while costs (visual impacts, facility operations, traffic) fall disproportionately on others

**Technical and Environmental Challenges**

- **Technology Integration:** Coordinating multiple renewable energy and circular economy technologies to function as integrated system required technical expertise and novel engineering approaches
- **Market Readiness:** Some proposed technologies (particularly Power-to-X for sustainable aviation fuel) remain in development phases with uncertain commercial viability and market demand
- **Environmental Site Conditions:** The steep slope and specific geographic characteristics of the Spørring area required landscape architectural solutions to integrate facilities while maintaining visual quality and recreational access
- **Seasonal and Climatic Variability:** Agricultural inputs to biogas production vary seasonally; renewable energy generation (particularly solar) varies with weather; integrated system design must accommodate these fluctuations

**Financial Challenges**

- **Capital Requirements:** Large upfront investment in renewable energy infrastructure, biogas facilities, and landscape improvements requires substantial capital; securing financing from public and private sources remains challenging
- **Revenue Uncertainty:** Markets for circular economy products (biochar, grass protein) and future e-fuels remain underdeveloped; revenue projections carry significant uncertainty
- **Cost Escalation:** Construction costs and energy prices have increased; managing budget within available funding requires cost controls and potential scope adjustments

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Governance Innovation and Institutional Change**

Rather than treating governance challenges as obstacles, CO-SHAPE reframed them as central to the project's innovation agenda:[^2]

- **Reflexive Governance Model:** Development of governance approaches that explicitly acknowledge complexity, uncertainty, and need for ongoing adaptation; the model emphasizes learning and institutional evolution rather than rigid adherence to predetermined plans
- **Institutional Change Initiatives:** Municipal leadership made co-creation a requirement in new renewable energy projects, embedding participatory approaches into institutional practice beyond the pilot period[^2]
- **Cross-Departmental Integration:** Establishment of internal municipal solution rooms addressing energy, planning, agriculture, and environment as integrated rather than separate domains; early evidence shows institutional change toward greater cross-departmental collaboration
- **Extended Timeframe:** Recognition that "transformation takes time" and "you can't change governance culture overnight," leading to acceptance that institutional change extends beyond the formal pilot period[^2]

**Stakeholder Engagement Adaptations**

- **Solution Room Methodology:** Development of structured yet flexible processes for bringing stakeholders together, characterized by trust and informal dialogue rather than formal bureaucratic procedures, reducing barriers to genuine participation
- **Landscape Architectural Visualization:** Use of scenario-building and visual representation tools to make abstract planning concepts tangible and discussable, facilitating informed stakeholder input
- **Continuous Refinement:** Design modifications based on stakeholder feedback; rather than treating initial designs as fixed, the project explicitly incorporates stakeholder input into design evolution
- **Satisfaction and Trust Metrics:** Monitoring of stakeholder satisfaction (85% satisfaction rate) and trust indicators (40% increase in public trust; 60% decrease in NIMBY sentiments) provides evidence of successful engagement and allows identification of areas requiring additional dialogue[^1]

**Technical and Design Adaptations**

- **Landscape Integration:** Investment in landscape architectural design to minimize visual and olfactory impacts of facilities, addressing aesthetic concerns that drive NIMBY opposition
- **Flexible Technology Sequencing:** Phased approach to technology implementation, with near-term focus on proven technologies (biogas, solar, waste heat recovery) and longer-term exploration of emerging technologies (Power-to-X) allowing time for technology maturation and market development
- **Adaptive Material Flows:** System design accommodates seasonal variations in agricultural inputs and renewable energy generation through storage capacity and flexible operational modes

**Risk Acceptance and Learning Orientation**

Project leadership explicitly acknowledged that "accepting risk is also essential" and treated business model failures, political shifts, and stakeholder conflicts as inherent to transformation rather than as project failures.[^2] This orientation enabled:

- Willingness to experiment with novel governance approaches despite uncertain outcomes
- Flexibility to modify designs and timelines based on experience
- Framing of challenges as learning opportunities rather than obstacles
- Documentation of lessons learned for transfer to other contexts, regardless of whether all initial objectives are fully achieved

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies**

CO-SHAPE's success depends on several contextual factors that may or may not exist in other locations:

- **Peri-Urban Geography:** CO-SHAPE is explicitly designed for peri-urban areas—transitional zones between dense urban cores and rural hinterlands. Replication requires similar geographic typology; dense urban cores or purely rural areas would require significant adaptation
- **Agricultural Base:** The circular energy park model depends on agricultural waste streams and land availability; regions without substantial agricultural activity would require different resource bases
- **Danish Governance Context:** Denmark's strong tradition of consensus-building, participatory democracy, and municipal autonomy creates favorable conditions for governance innovation; regions with more top-down governance traditions would require cultural and institutional adaptation
- **Economic Development Level:** CO-SHAPE's capital-intensive infrastructure requires substantial public and private investment; regions with limited capital availability would face barriers
- **Existing Energy Infrastructure:** Availability of existing biogas plants or other infrastructure that can be integrated and upgraded; regions lacking such infrastructure require larger capital investment
- **Educational and Research Capacity:** Partnership with Aarhus School of Architecture and access to research expertise supports project development; regions without similar academic partnerships would require alternative knowledge sources

**Critical Success Factors**

Based on CO-SHAPE's experience, critical success factors for replication include:

1. **Political Leadership and Commitment:** Municipal leadership willing to champion participatory governance and integrated planning; political commitment extending across electoral cycles
2. **Institutional Willingness to Break Silos:** Municipal capacity and willingness to establish cross-departmental collaboration; willingness to challenge traditional departmental boundaries
3. **Stakeholder Engagement Capacity:** Staff trained in participatory governance, solution room facilitation, and conflict resolution; willingness to invest time in dialogue and negotiation
4. **Geographic Suitability:** Peri-urban location with agricultural base, available land, and existing or planned energy infrastructure
5. **Capital and Financing:** Access to public funding (EU programs, national government, municipal budget) and private investment; ability to structure financing across multiple revenue streams
6. **Community Receptiveness:** Stakeholders willing to engage in dialogue and co-creation; existing trust or willingness to build trust between municipal government and community
7. **Knowledge and Technical Expertise:** Access to expertise in renewable energy systems, circular economy design, landscape architecture, and participatory governance

**Scalability**

CO-SHAPE demonstrates scalability across multiple dimensions:

- **Geographic Scalability:** The governance innovations and comprehensive planning approach are transferable to other peri-urban areas; the specific technology mix (biogas, solar, Power-to-X) can be adapted to local resource availability and needs
- **Institutional Scalability:** The reflexive governance model and cross-departmental integration mechanisms are transferable to municipal governments of various sizes; the 5 neighboring municipalities that have already adopted elements of the approach demonstrate proof of concept for scaling
- **Sectoral Scalability:** While CO-SHAPE focuses on energy and agriculture, the participatory governance and integrated planning approach is applicable to other complex municipal challenges (transportation, housing, waste management, water management)
- **Temporal Scalability:** Governance innovations can be sustained and institutionalized beyond pilot timeframes through embedding in municipal policy and procedures

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Governance Innovation**

CO-SHAPE's most significant strength is its explicit recognition that governance and social acceptance represent the primary barriers to climate transition, not technology.[^2] The solution room model and participatory approach represent genuine innovation in how cities make decisions about complex infrastructure. Rather than conventional planning processes where developers propose, governments review, and public consultation occurs as afterthought, CO-SHAPE brings stakeholders together from inception to negotiate trade-offs collaboratively. This approach transforms potential opposition into stakeholder buy-in: the 60% reduction in NIMBY sentiments and 85% stakeholder satisfaction demonstrate effectiveness.[^1] The institutional embedding of co-creation into municipal policy and procedures, made mandatory by local politicians, suggests that governance innovations will persist beyond the pilot period.[^2]

**Integrated Systems Thinking**

CO-SHAPE's comprehensive approach to integrating energy production, agriculture, landscape design, and community well-being represents best practice in systems thinking. Rather than treating these domains as separate or competing, the project recognizes them as interconnected. This integration creates multiple benefits: agricultural waste becomes biogas input, biogas residues become biochar, waste heat is recovered and reused, solar electricity powers processing facilities. This closed-loop design improves overall system efficiency and resilience compared to single-purpose energy infrastructure. The potential to reduce 100,000 tonnes CO₂ annually while simultaneously preserving agricultural viability, improving landscape quality, and creating community benefits demonstrates the power of integrated thinking.[^2]

**Institutional Change and Scaling**

The project's success in establishing cross-departmental collaboration within Aarhus Municipality and its preliminary replication in 5 neighboring municipalities demonstrates institutional change and scaling potential.[^1] Rather than remaining a isolated pilot, governance innovations are spreading to other municipalities, suggesting genuine institutional transformation rather than temporary project-specific implementation.

**Landscape and Quality of Life Integration**

CO-SHAPE's explicit commitment to landscape quality and multifunctional design ensures that energy transition does not require sacrifice of environmental quality or community well-being. This contrasts with many energy infrastructure projects that treat landscape impacts as externalities to be minimized rather than integrated into design. The comprehensive landscape architectural approach creates recreational access, visual quality, and ecological functions alongside energy production.[^2]

**Adaptive and Learning Orientation**

Project leadership's explicit acknowledgment that transformation requires time, acceptance of risk, and willingness to adapt based on experience represents mature and realistic approach to complex change.[^2] Rather than treating challenges as failures, the project treats them as learning opportunities. This orientation enables genuine innovation and adaptation rather than rigid adherence to predetermined plans.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Governance Complexity and Implementation Difficulty**

While governance innovation is CO-SHAPE's strength, it is also its greatest challenge. Project leadership acknowledged that breaking down departmental silos "is more difficult than we anticipated."[^2] The complexity of coordinating energy, planning, agriculture, and environment departments, each with different mandates and performance metrics, creates ongoing friction. This suggests that governance innovation, while possible, requires sustained effort and may face resistance from institutional structures and personnel accustomed to independent operation. The transferability of governance innovations to other municipalities and cities remains uncertain; what works in Aarhus with its strong civic culture and municipal capacity may not transfer easily to regions with different governance traditions.

**Timeline and Scale Mismatch**

The pilot phase (2024-2027) is relatively short for assessing long-term outcomes of governance and institutional change. Transformation of municipal governance culture typically requires years or decades; a three-year pilot cannot definitively demonstrate whether changes will persist beyond the project period. The 1,000-hectare scale, while significant for a pilot, is relatively modest for demonstrating viability at city or regional scale. Questions remain about whether governance innovations and economic models that work at pilot scale will function at larger scales with more complex stakeholder dynamics.

**Technology Maturation Uncertainty**

Power-to-X technologies, identified as a key future component of the energy park, remain in development phases with uncertain commercial viability and market demand.[^2] If these technologies fail to mature or markets for sustainable aviation fuels do not develop as anticipated, significant infrastructure investments could become stranded. The project's financial sustainability depends partially on revenue from technologies that remain speculative, creating risk of financial underperformance.

**Gentrification and Displacement Risks**

While not explicitly addressed in available sources, improved landscape design and multifunctional land use in a peri-urban area with limited housing supply could increase property values and trigger gentrification. This could displace existing residents unable to afford increased housing costs. The project's participatory approach and inclusion of residents in co-creation processes provides some protection, but does not guarantee equitable outcomes. Farmers whose agricultural land is converted to energy infrastructure face particular displacement risk, despite their inclusion in co-creation processes; negotiated solutions may not fully compensate for loss of agricultural livelihoods.

**Limited Diversity of Stakeholder Engagement**

While CO-SHAPE's stakeholder engagement is more inclusive than conventional planning, questions remain about the full diversity of voices. Participation in solution rooms and co-creation workshops requires time, knowledge, and social capital; marginalized populations or those with limited engagement capacity may be underrepresented. The satisfaction metrics (85% satisfaction, 40% increase in trust) likely reflect experiences of those who actively participated; experiences of non-participants or those who felt excluded from meaningful participation remain unknown.

**Replicability and Contextual Dependencies**

CO-SHAPE's success depends on numerous contextual factors—Danish governance traditions, municipal capacity, agricultural base, available capital, existing infrastructure—that may not exist in other locations. The governance innovations may be particularly difficult to replicate in regions with more hierarchical governance traditions or lower municipal capacity. The specific technology mix (biogas, solar, Power-to-X) depends on local resource availability; direct replication in regions with different resources would require substantial adaptation. The preliminary replication in 5 neighboring municipalities is encouraging but limited; broader European replication remains to be demonstrated.

**Incomplete Outcomes Assessment**

As of November 2025, CO-SHAPE remains in active pilot phase; many outputs and outcomes remain incomplete or projected rather than realized. The comprehensive plan has been developed, but facility construction and operation remain ongoing. Long-term outcomes—sustained emissions reduction, institutional change persistence, financial sustainability, community acceptance over extended periods—cannot yet be fully assessed. The project's ultimate success or failure will only be evident after several years of operation and institutional evolution.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

**Distributional Equity**

CO-SHAPE's design aims to distribute benefits broadly (local energy, agricultural products, jobs, landscape improvements) rather than concentrating them among single beneficiary group. However, actual distribution depends on implementation details not fully specified in available sources:

- **Energy Benefits:** If renewable energy generated in the park is sold to external markets rather than prioritized for local residents, energy cost benefits may not reach those most in need
- **Agricultural Benefits:** If grass protein and biochar products are sold to distant markets rather than supporting local farmers, agricultural benefits may not accrue to local stakeholders
- **Job Benefits:** If employment opportunities require specialized skills not available among local residents, job benefits may accrue to workers from outside the area
- **Property Value Appreciation:** If improved landscape and multifunctional design increase property values, existing residents may face displacement through gentrification rather than benefiting from improved surroundings

**Procedural Equity**

CO-SHAPE's participatory approach represents significant advance in procedural equity compared to conventional top-down planning. However, questions remain:

- **Participation Barriers:** While the solution room approach is more accessible than formal bureaucratic processes, participation still requires time and social capital; those with less time (working multiple jobs) or less social capital (recent immigrants, marginalized populations) may face barriers
- **Voice and Influence:** While stakeholders participate in solution rooms, actual influence on final decisions remains unclear; do developers and energy operators have disproportionate influence compared to residents?
- **Conflict Resolution:** How are conflicts between stakeholders with fundamentally opposed interests (e.g., farmers wanting to preserve agricultural land vs. developers wanting to maximize energy infrastructure) actually resolved?

**Recognition Equity**

CO-SHAPE's participatory approach represents significant advance in recognition equity, acknowledging diverse stakeholders' legitimate interests and perspectives. The 60% reduction in NIMBY sentiments suggests that residents feel their concerns are being recognized and addressed rather than dismissed. However, questions remain about whose knowledge and perspectives are valued in co-creation processes; is traditional ecological knowledge of farmers equally valued with technical expertise of engineers and planners?

**Environmental Justice**

- **Distributional Justice:** Are environmental benefits (improved air quality, reduced emissions, enhanced landscape) and burdens (noise, odor, visual impacts from facilities) distributed equitably across social groups?
- **Procedural Justice:** Do marginalized populations have equal voice in decisions about environmental infrastructure affecting their communities?
- **Recognition Justice:** Are diverse perspectives on environmental quality and acceptable trade-offs equally valued?

CO-SHAPE's integrated approach and participatory governance advance environmental justice compared to conventional energy infrastructure development, but full achievement of environmental justice requires ongoing attention to equitable distribution of benefits and burdens.

**CONCLUSION**

CO-SHAPE represents a sophisticated attempt to address climate transition not primarily through technological innovation but through governance innovation and participatory democracy. By positioning peri-urban areas as sites of integrated sustainability rather than zones of conflict, and by treating stakeholder engagement as central to infrastructure planning rather than an afterthought, the project offers valuable lessons for European cities pursuing climate neutrality. The preliminary success in reducing NIMBY sentiments, achieving high stakeholder satisfaction, and spreading governance innovations to neighboring municipalities suggests that the approach is viable, though questions remain about long-term sustainability, equitable benefit distribution, and transferability to different geographic and governance contexts. The project's ultimate significance will be determined not by the technical performance of the energy park, but by whether governance innovations persist and spread, transforming how municipalities approach complex infrastructure development and climate transition more broadly.
