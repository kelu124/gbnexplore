# Executive summary## mySMARTLife – Positive Energy Block Demonstration

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** mySMARTLife – Positive Energy Block Demonstration
- **Alternative Names:** mySMARTLife Vienna Positive Energy Block; Vienna PED; Positive Energy District Vienna (occasionally referenced as part of broader PED initiatives)
- **Location:** Vienna, Austria (specific demonstration site in an urban district, frequently referenced as part of Vienna’s “smart city” and “Zukunftsquartier” efforts)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 48.2082° N, 16.3738° E (central Vienna; specific coordinates depend on the demonstration block)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Project Launch: 2016 (as part of the EU Horizon 2020 mySMARTLife program)
  - Implementation: 2017–2021 (major demonstration activities)
  - Status: Completed, ongoing monitoring and follow-up
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Area: Several interconnected buildings (exact m² not always specified, but typically on the scale of a city block)
  - Population: Several hundred residents, plus businesses
  - Investment Value: Multi-million euro (mySMARTLife program total ~€18 million across sites)
- **Leading Organization(s):** 
  - City of Vienna (Smart City Agency)
  - mySMARTLife EU consortium (coordinated by CARTIF Technology Centre)
  - Local partners (Vienna utility, research institutes, housing associations)
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - European Union Horizon 2020
  - Vienna city government
  - Private sector (utility and tech partners)
  - Additional in-kind and co-financing from local stakeholders[^1][^2]

#### **Executive Summary**
The mySMARTLife Positive Energy Block in Vienna is a flagship demonstration under the EU Horizon 2020 mySMARTLife project, aiming to show how urban neighborhoods can achieve net-positive energy status. The initiative targets a cluster of buildings in Vienna, integrating deep energy retrofits, on-site renewable energy (primarily solar PV), advanced building management systems, and real-time data collection to optimize energy use and production. It pioneers the “Positive Energy Block” (PEB) model—where a district produces more renewable energy annually than it consumes—and serves as a living laboratory for smart technologies and resident engagement.

Key objectives include drastically reducing building emissions, improving thermal comfort, and lowering energy costs, all while ensuring technology interoperability and robust citizen participation. The initiative’s significance lies in its demonstration of how innovative retrofits, smart infrastructure, and digitalization can be combined at the block scale to support Vienna’s—and Europe’s—climate and energy goals. It delivers scalable results and lessons for other cities, advancing the broader vision of Positive Energy Districts (PEDs) as a pathway toward urban decarbonization, resilience, and quality of life[^1][^2][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Vienna is Austria’s capital and largest city, home to about 1.9 million people. It is an economic and cultural hub in Central Europe, known for its high quality of life, strong public services, and ambitious climate targets. As a “Smart City,” Vienna prioritizes innovation in energy, mobility, and governance, with a long-term vision to become climate-neutral by 2040[^2].

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
The Positive Energy Block demonstration is situated in a mixed-use urban neighborhood featuring:
- **Historical Patterns:** Typical of Vienna’s dense, mid-to-late 20th-century urban fabric, with multi-story residential buildings and some commercial uses.
- **Current Land Use:** Primarily residential, with supporting local services and infrastructure.
- **Demographic Profile:** Mixed-income residents, with Vienna generally maintaining affordable housing and social diversity.
- **Infrastructure Quality:** Good public transport, district heating, established utilities, but with aging building stock requiring modernization.
- **Environmental Conditions:** Urban heat island effects, air quality concerns, and the need to reduce carbon emissions from buildings and mobility.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**
- High energy consumption and emissions from older buildings.
- Insufficient integration of renewables at the neighborhood scale.
- Fragmented building management systems lacking interoperability.
- Limited resident engagement in sustainability.

**Triggering Events:**
- EU and Vienna’s climate policy commitments.
- Availability of Horizon 2020 funding for demonstration projects.
- Growing recognition of the need for district-scale solutions.

**Stakeholder Landscape:**
- City authorities (urban planning, energy)
- Utility providers (electricity, district heating)
- Private technology partners
- Local housing associations and residents

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**  
Project led by the City of Vienna, in partnership with EU and private sector stakeholders, under the governance of the Horizon 2020 mySMARTLife consortium.

**Relevant Policies:**  
- Vienna Smart City Framework Strategy
- EU Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy Directives
- Local zoning and building energy standards, updated to permit innovative retrofits and renewables.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
- Supportive legal frameworks for energy sharing and data collection.
- Some regulatory complexity regarding interconnection and market participation for local energy production[^2][^3].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:**
- Transform a city block into a net-positive energy producer.
- Integrate renewable energy, digital technologies, and resident engagement for optimized energy management.
- Demonstrate scalable and replicable models for other European cities[^1][^2][^3].

**Vision Statement:**
A resilient, smart, and sustainable urban neighborhood where residents co-create a positive energy future, supporting climate neutrality and high quality of life.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:**
Directly supports Vienna’s “Smart City” strategy and the EU’s push for Positive Energy Districts as a climate action tool.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:**
- Retrofit buildings and upgrade systems → integrate local renewables → implement smart controls and data monitoring → engage residents → optimize energy flows and achieve net-positive status → reduce emissions and costs → demonstrate replicability.

**Target Beneficiaries:**
- Local residents (energy savings, comfort)
- City and utilities (emissions reduction, system efficiency)
- Broader urban development community (knowledge transfer)

**Innovation Elements:**
- District-level integration of smart infrastructure.
- Real-time energy and environmental monitoring.
- Resident-centered engagement and co-creation.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** EU and city government climate ambitions.
- **Economic:** Lower energy costs, local investment, EU funding.
- **Social:** Quality of life, resident participation.
- **Environmental:** Emission reductions, renewable integration.
- **Technological:** Maturity of smart grids, sensors, and digital platforms.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**  
Based on the Positive Energy District (PED) model, emphasizing energy self-sufficiency, digitalization, and resident co-creation.

**Physical/Spatial Design:**  
- Deep retrofit of existing buildings (insulation, efficient windows)
- Addition of rooftop solar PV
- Smart meters and environmental sensors throughout the block
- Public spaces and mobility infrastructure upgrades (EV charging)

**Scope and Phasing:**  
- Initial energy audits and resident engagement
- Building retrofits and system installations
- Commissioning of digital management platforms
- Ongoing monitoring and optimization

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Advanced building management systems**
- **Solar photovoltaic arrays**
- **Electric vehicle charging stations**
- **Real-time energy and environmental sensors**
- **Integrated digital platforms for data collection and decision support**[^3][^4]

**Innovation Type:**  
Technological (smart systems, renewables), social (resident engagement), and process (integrated planning and management).

**Integration Approach:**  
Systems designed for interoperability, with data flowing from building sensors to a central platform for optimization at the block level.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Competitive tenders for technology and construction partners, in line with EU and city procurement rules.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Workshops, surveys, and co-design sessions with residents and local businesses.
- **Participatory Elements:** Feedback loops for residents to report issues and suggest improvements; co-creation in sustainability actions.
- **Project Management:** Led by Vienna’s Smart City Agency, with regular coordination meetings, milestones, and performance monitoring.

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Not fully disaggregated for Vienna alone, but the mySMARTLife project’s total budget was approximately €18 million across demonstration cities.
- **Funding Mix:** EU grant, city government contribution, private sector co-financing.
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Reduced energy bills for residents, potential grid services.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Focus on low operating costs post-retrofit and potential for local energy trading in future market models.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Ensuring interoperability between legacy systems and new smart technologies; integrating diverse data streams.
- **Financial:** Upfront investment needs; ensuring cost-effectiveness over lifecycle.
- **Social and Political:** Achieving broad resident buy-in, managing expectations.
- **Administrative:** Navigating EU and local regulations; coordinating multiple partners.
- **Environmental:** Managing construction impacts, ensuring system resilience.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Iterative testing of systems, user-friendly interfaces for residents, ongoing technical support.
- **Design Modifications:** Adjustments to retrofit depth or technology choices based on early feedback and data.
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:** Need for continuous engagement, simplification of user interfaces, and robust data privacy protections[^3].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** Deeply retrofitted residential buildings, new solar installations, EV charging infrastructure, smart sensors and meters.
- **Service Delivery:** Real-time energy management, improved comfort, support for e-mobility.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** 
  - Net-positive annual energy balance for the block
  - Significant reduction in district heating and electricity demand
  - High resident participation rates

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Reduced energy bills; improved indoor comfort; enhanced awareness of sustainability.
- **Equity Outcomes:** Focus on affordability and inclusion, though ongoing monitoring is needed to track distributional impacts.
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** No significant displacement reported; measures taken to maintain affordability.
- **Social Capital:** Strengthened community networks through engagement activities.
- **Cultural Impacts:** Supports Vienna’s identity as a progressive, sustainable city.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Construction and tech sector jobs during retrofit and installation.
- **Economic Activity:** Local businesses benefit from improved infrastructure.
- **Property Values:** Potential uplift due to modernization and sustainability branding.
- **Cost Savings:** Lower utility bills for residents and city, reduced maintenance costs.
- **Economic Inclusion:** Project designed to maintain access for lower-income residents.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Major reduction in CO2 emissions from buildings; supports Vienna’s climate neutrality goal.
- **Resource Management:** Improved energy and water efficiency; on-site renewable generation.
- **Environmental Quality:** Better air quality, reduced noise, more green space.
- **Waste and Pollution:** Lower construction waste via retrofit (vs. demolition/new build); improved recycling practices.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Performance tracked against EU PED standards and city climate plans.

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Enhanced city capability for managing smart city projects.
- **Policy Influence:** Informs Vienna’s broader PED and smart city policies.
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Use of open data platforms and public reporting.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** Block achieved net-positive energy status; high resident satisfaction; technical milestones met.
- **Unintended Consequences:** Complexity of technology adoption among less tech-savvy residents; need for ongoing support.
- **Long-term Viability:** Designed for durability, with city commitments to maintain and expand the approach.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:** Vienna’s strong governance, supportive policy, and high-quality urban infrastructure were key; some elements (e.g., deep retrofits, citizen engagement) are transferable.
- **Critical Success Factors:** Political leadership, integrated planning, robust funding, active resident participation.
- **Scalability:** PED/PEB models can be applied to other city districts, with size and technology mix adjusted to context.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:** Parallels with PED projects in Groningen, Oulu, Alkmaar, and others under the EU PED/Smart City programs[^1][^2].
- **Best Practices:** Integrated data collection, participatory design, staged implementation.
- **Cautionary Lessons:** Importance of clear communication, managing technical complexity, and ongoing support for residents.

- **Contextual Similarities:** Urban density, mixed-use blocks, aging building stock.
- **Methodological Parallels:** Emphasis on data-driven energy management and resident engagement.
- **Technological Overlaps:** Use of smart sensors, PV, and EV infrastructure.
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Multi-actor partnerships; city-led with strong resident participation.
- **Transferable Strategies:** Structured co-creation, staged retrofits, open data.
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Local policy and regulatory adjustments may be needed for energy trading and data sharing.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Demonstrated that **net-positive energy status** is achievable in existing urban fabric.
- Robust **integration of technology and social innovation**.
- Strong **focus on resident engagement** and quality of life.
- Provided a **scalable, replicable model** for other European cities.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Complexity** of digital systems posed adaptation challenges for some residents.
- Upfront **costs** were significant, though long-term savings are expected.
- **Data privacy and interoperability** issues required substantial effort to address.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Maintained affordability and **prevented displacement**.
- Ensured **inclusive participation**, though ongoing monitoring is needed to sustain equity outcomes.

#### **Future Outlook**

- Potential for **citywide and regional scaling** of PED approaches.
- Ongoing **monitoring and optimization** for performance and resident satisfaction.
- Opportunities to integrate with **emerging energy markets** and further digital services.
