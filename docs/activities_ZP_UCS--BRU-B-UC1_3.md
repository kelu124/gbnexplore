# Executive summary## Sharing Cities – Digital Energy Platform

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Sharing Cities
- **Alternative Names:** Sharing Cities Smart Cities and Communities (SCC1); Sharing Cities Horizon 2020
- **Location:** Milan, Italy; Porta Romana-Vettabbia district (demonstration area), with replication in Lisbon, Portugal; London, United Kingdom; and follower cities including Bordeaux, France; Burgas, Bulgaria; and Warsaw, Poland
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Porta Romana/Vettabbia district in central-southern Milan (approximately 45.44°N, 9.18°E)
- **Timeline:** January 2016 – December 2021 (initial 3-year implementation period plus 2-year monitoring phase); project officially completed December 2021[^1]
- **Project Scale:** 216,614 m² former railway yard in Porta Romana/Vettabbia; 20 buildings retrofitted; 25,000 m² of private and public buildings energy redevelopment; 20,000+ residents affected; 4,200 Digital Social Market users across three lighthouse cities[^1][^2]
- **Leading Organization(s):** European Commission (funding body); Fondazione Politecnico di Milano (coordinating partner); Comune di Milano (municipality); Politecnico di Milano (academic institutions); Siemens, A2A, ATM, and numerous private and public sector partners[^1]
- **Funding Sources:** European Commission Horizon 2020 programme (€24 million total project budget); €8.6 million allocated to Milan local partnership; €265 million in additional private investment leveraged[^1][^2]

#### **Executive Summary**

Sharing Cities represents a comprehensive European initiative designed to transform urban neighborhoods into low-carbon, digitally integrated smart districts through innovative technologies and citizen engagement mechanisms. Launched in 2016 under the European Commission's Horizon 2020 Smart Cities and Communities program, the project addressed three interconnected urban challenges: reducing carbon emissions from buildings and transport, improving air quality, and fostering sustainable citizen behavior. The Milan demonstration, centered in the Porta Romana-Vettabbia district—a 216,614 m² former railway yard undergoing complete redevelopment—served as a lighthouse city alongside London and Lisbon, with the initiative later replicated in three follower cities.[^1][^2]

The project's distinctive approach combined infrastructure investments (building retrofits achieving 70% energy reduction, electric vehicle charging networks, smart street lighting) with digital platforms and behavioral incentives (the SharingMI app rewarding sustainable choices). Rather than technology-driven, Milan's interpretation centered on citizen needs, integrating smart mobility, environmental monitoring, energy management, and data-driven governance. Over six years, the initiative deployed ten technologies tested across three lighthouse cities, resulting in 6.1 GWh/yr decrease in building energy needs, 4,500 tons of CO₂ emissions saved through shared mobility, and demonstrated replication potential across European cities.[^2] The project exemplifies how multi-stakeholder partnerships combining municipal governance, academic research, private sector innovation, and community participation can create replicable models for urban sustainability at scale.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile**

Milan is Italy's second-largest city and the economic capital of northern Italy, serving as a global financial, fashion, design, and technology hub. The city has approximately 1.4 million residents within its administrative boundaries, with a metropolitan area exceeding 7 million people. Historically, Milan evolved from a medieval city to a major industrial center during the 19th and 20th centuries, subsequently transitioning toward a service and knowledge economy. The city occupies the Po Valley in the Lombardy region, characterized by a continental climate with significant air quality challenges due to geographic position and industrial legacy. Milan's strategic importance within the European urban system, combined with its economic dynamism and environmental pressures, positioned it as an ideal location for testing innovative urban sustainability solutions.[^1][^3]

Since 2012, Milan has systematically developed integrated policy frameworks addressing urban development, sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, the sharing economy, and smart cities. The municipal administration explicitly conceptualized smart cities not as technology-driven initiatives but as citizen-centered ecosystems addressing smart mobility, environmental quality, social inclusion, and civic participation.[^3] This philosophical orientation shaped the Sharing Cities project's design, distinguishing it from purely technocratic approaches to urban innovation.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics**

The Porta Romana-Vettabbia district represents a historically significant yet economically challenged area requiring comprehensive renewal. The demonstration area encompasses a brownfield site—a former railway yard occupying 216,614 m²—located in the northern portion of the Porta Romana neighborhood. This area historically connected Milan's historic center to the city's agricultural belt but had become geographically, economically, and socially separated from both zones following industrial decline and railway infrastructure changes.[^3]

The neighborhood's demographic profile reflects working-class and lower-middle-income residential populations, with mixed land use including residential buildings, commercial spaces, and underutilized industrial areas. Prior to the Sharing Cities intervention, the district faced multiple challenges: aging building stock with poor energy efficiency, limited green space, inadequate public transportation connectivity, traffic congestion, and air quality degradation typical of post-industrial urban areas. The former railway yard represented both a constraint (contaminated brownfield requiring remediation) and an opportunity (available land for comprehensive redevelopment incorporating modern sustainability standards). Existing infrastructure, while present, required modernization to support integrated mobility and energy systems.[^3]

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified**

The Sharing Cities project responded to interconnected urban sustainability challenges affecting Milan and European cities broadly:

**Energy and Building Performance:** Milan's building stock, particularly in older neighborhoods like Porta Romana, exhibited poor thermal performance, high heating/cooling demands, and limited renewable energy integration. Buildings constructed before modern energy codes consumed excessive energy, contributing to both high resident costs and significant greenhouse gas emissions.

**Transportation and Emissions:** Conventional car-based mobility patterns generated substantial carbon emissions, local air pollution, and traffic congestion. The mobility system lacked integration, requiring users to navigate multiple payment systems, booking platforms, and service providers, creating friction that discouraged sustainable alternatives.

**Air Quality and Environmental Health:** Milan's geographic position in the Po Valley and industrial legacy created persistent air quality challenges, with particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide concentrations frequently exceeding EU air quality standards, particularly during winter months.

**Citizen Engagement and Behavioral Change:** Municipal authorities recognized that technological solutions alone proved insufficient without corresponding shifts in citizen behavior and values. Residents lacked accessible information about personal energy consumption, environmental impacts of mobility choices, or mechanisms to participate in sustainability decision-making.

**Data Fragmentation and Governance Capacity:** Urban data existed in silos across different municipal departments, utility companies, and private service providers. This fragmentation impeded evidence-based policy-making and prevented integrated management of interdependent urban systems.[^1][^3][^2]

**Triggering Events**

Multiple catalysts converged to enable the Sharing Cities initiative:

The European Commission's Horizon 2020 programme, launched in 2014, explicitly prioritized smart cities and communities as mechanisms for achieving climate targets and advancing digital innovation. The SCC1 call specifically sought demonstration projects integrating energy, transport, and ICT sectors. Milan's prior experience with sharing economy initiatives, sustainable mobility policies, and digital infrastructure positioned it competitively for this funding opportunity.

Political commitment from Milan's municipal administration, articulated through Mayor Giuseppe Sala's 2016 statement emphasizing environmental, social, and economic sustainability, created institutional alignment for the multi-year initiative. The availability of the Porta Romana brownfield site, combined with planned comprehensive redevelopment, provided a discrete geographic area suitable for demonstrating integrated interventions.

The broader European climate agenda, particularly post-Paris Agreement commitments to carbon reduction and the EU's 2050 climate neutrality vision, created policy momentum for experimental approaches to urban decarbonization.[^1]

**Stakeholder Landscape**

Pre-initiative stakeholder mapping revealed diverse interests and capacities:

**Municipal Government:** The Comune di Milano sought to advance sustainability objectives, attract EU funding, enhance municipal competitiveness, and improve residents' quality of life. The municipality controlled land use planning, permitting, public space management, and certain utility services.

**Academic Institutions:** Politecnico di Milano, through its Department of Architecture and Urban Studies (DASTU) and Department of Energy (DENG), brought research capacity, technical expertise, and institutional credibility. Academic partners sought opportunities for applied research, student engagement, and knowledge dissemination.

**Private Sector:** Energy companies (A2A, Unareti), technology providers (Siemens), mobility operators (ATM), and construction firms possessed technical capabilities, capital, and operational experience but required clear regulatory frameworks and revenue certainty.

**Civil Society:** Environmental organizations (Legambiente) and community groups advocated for sustainability but sometimes expressed skepticism about whether technology-focused initiatives genuinely served resident interests versus serving corporate interests.

**Residents:** Existing Porta Romana residents, particularly lower-income households, expressed concerns about gentrification risks, displacement, and whether renewal benefits would accrue equitably.[^1][^3]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework**

The Sharing Cities initiative operated within multi-level governance structures spanning European, national, and municipal scales. The European Commission established funding criteria, reporting requirements, and evaluation frameworks through Horizon 2020 programme governance. At the national level, Italian government policies on renewable energy, building standards, and municipal competencies provided the regulatory foundation. Municipal government, through the Comune di Milano and specialized agencies (AMAT for mobility and environmental issues; ATM for public transport), exercised primary authority over land use, permitting, public procurement, and service provision.[^1]

The project employed a partnership governance model rather than purely municipal implementation. A consortium structure brought together 35 Italian partners across public administration, academic institutions, private companies, and NGOs. This multi-stakeholder approach distributed decision-making, risk-bearing, and benefit-sharing across organizations with different mandates and capabilities.

**Relevant Policies**

Milan's strategic planning framework provided crucial policy alignment:

**Urban Development Strategy:** Milan's comprehensive urban development plan incorporated sustainability principles, prioritized brownfield redevelopment, and supported mixed-use neighborhoods combining housing, commerce, and green space. The Porta Romana-Vettabbia redevelopment aligned with these strategic priorities.

**Energy Policy:** Italy's implementation of EU Energy Efficiency Directive (2012/27/EU) and Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (2010/31/EU) established building retrofit standards and obligations. Milan's commitment to reducing building energy consumption aligned with national and EU targets.

**Sustainable Mobility Policy:** Milan's 2012 decision to open the mobility market to private companies, following decades of public monopoly, created conditions for diverse shared mobility providers. Subsequent policies supported cycling infrastructure, electric vehicle adoption, and congestion charging in the city center.

**Sharing Economy Framework:** Milan adopted explicit policies recognizing and regulating sharing economy activities, including vehicle sharing, bike sharing, and coworking. The municipal administration mapped sharing economy operators and developed governance guidelines.[^3][^4]

**Climate Action:** Milan's commitment to climate neutrality, expressed through participation in the C40 Cities network and adoption of sustainability targets, provided overarching policy direction supporting the Sharing Cities initiative.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers**

**Enablers:**

The Horizon 2020 funding mechanism provided non-repayable grants, reducing financial risk for municipal participation. EU funding came with technical support and access to networks of peer cities, reducing implementation isolation.

Italian building code reforms increasingly permitted innovative energy solutions and performance-based standards rather than prescriptive requirements, enabling retrofits combining traditional improvements with emerging technologies.

Data protection frameworks, while creating compliance obligations, established predictable legal requirements enabling responsible data governance rather than creating absolute barriers.

**Barriers:**

GDPR compliance, while legally necessary, created implementation complexity and cost, particularly for collecting and analyzing resident energy consumption and mobility data. Privacy considerations required careful system design and resident consent mechanisms.

Italian public procurement regulations, while ensuring transparency and fairness, created lengthy procurement timelines that sometimes conflicted with project schedules. Complex tendering processes for selecting service operators occasionally delayed implementation.

Building ownership fragmentation in residential areas complicated retrofit implementation. In Italy, condominium buildings (common in urban areas) require supermajority approval for energy efficiency improvements, creating coordination challenges and potential holdout problems.

Utility company regulatory frameworks, designed for centralized energy systems, created friction when integrating distributed renewable generation and demand-response mechanisms that Sharing Cities proposed.[^1][^3]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals**

The Sharing Cities initiative pursued explicitly stated objectives organized across environmental, social, and technological dimensions:

**Environmental Objectives:** Reduce carbon emissions from buildings and transport; improve urban air quality; decrease overall greenhouse gas emissions in demonstration districts; increase renewable energy integration; reduce energy consumption through efficiency and behavioral change.

**Social Objectives:** Enhance residents' quality of life; improve health outcomes through reduced air pollution and increased active mobility; increase social cohesion through participatory processes; ensure equitable distribution of sustainability benefits; empower citizens as active participants in sustainability solutions rather than passive recipients.

**Technological and Governance Objectives:** Demonstrate integration of energy, mobility, and ICT sectors through digital platforms; establish interoperable data systems enabling evidence-based policy; create replicable models and tools transferable to other cities; strengthen municipal governance capacity for smart city implementation; attract private investment in urban sustainability.

**Economic Objectives:** Create employment in green sectors; stimulate business development in sustainable technologies; reduce long-term operating costs for buildings and mobility systems; attract investment capital to urban sustainability.[^1][^2]

**Vision Statement**

The initiative articulated a vision of "nearly-zero emission districts" where technological innovation, citizen engagement, and integrated urban systems created neighborhoods demonstrating compatibility between high quality of life and environmental sustainability. Rather than imposing sustainability through regulation, the vision emphasized voluntary behavior change motivated through accessible information, peer comparison, financial incentives, and community participation. The aspiration extended beyond individual neighborhoods to systemic transformation: creating replicable models, building institutional capacity, and shifting urban governance toward integrated, data-informed, citizen-centered approaches.[^1][^2]

**Alignment with Broader Plans**

**European Level:** Sharing Cities directly supported the EU's 2030 Climate and Energy Framework (40% greenhouse gas reduction), the Paris Agreement commitments, and the broader European Green Deal. The project exemplified the EU's strategy of combining regulatory requirements with demonstration projects and capacity-building to accelerate urban sustainability transitions.

**National Level:** The initiative aligned with Italy's national energy efficiency targets, renewable energy objectives, and climate commitments. It supported Italy's positioning within European sustainability leadership while addressing persistent challenges in building performance and urban air quality.

**Municipal Level:** Sharing Cities integrated with Milan's comprehensive sustainability strategy, supporting the city's objectives for becoming a global sustainability leader, attracting talent and investment through quality-of-life improvements, and positioning Milan as an innovation hub. The project complemented other municipal initiatives in sharing economy development, public space renewal, and digital infrastructure.[^1][^3][^4]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change**

The initiative's causal logic operated through interconnected pathways:

**Infrastructure Pathway:** Deploying energy-efficient building retrofits, renewable energy systems, electric vehicle charging networks, and smart lighting infrastructure directly reduced emissions while improving service quality. These investments created physical conditions enabling low-carbon lifestyles.

**Digital Platform Pathway:** Creating integrated urban data systems and citizen-facing applications provided information, transparency, and decision support. Real-time energy consumption data enabled residents to understand and modify consumption patterns. Mobility platforms reduced transaction costs for sustainable travel, increasing adoption. Smart city platforms enabled municipal authorities to optimize resource allocation based on actual demand patterns.

**Behavioral Incentive Pathway:** The SharingMI app and Digital Social Market created reward mechanisms encouraging sustainable choices. By converting sustainable behaviors into tradeable points redeemable for local and global benefits, the system leveraged behavioral economics principles to motivate voluntary participation. Peer benchmarking—comparing household energy consumption against similar properties—leveraged social comparison effects.

**Participatory Pathway:** Engaging residents in co-design processes, soliciting input on intervention selection, and creating mechanisms for community feedback built social capital, increased program legitimacy, and ensured solutions reflected actual resident needs and preferences rather than expert assumptions.

**Institutional Pathway:** Developing new governance structures, data-sharing agreements, and service integration mechanisms strengthened municipal capacity for managing complex urban systems. These institutional innovations persisted beyond the project timeline, enabling ongoing coordination among previously siloed actors.

The theory presumed that combining these pathways—infrastructure, digital, behavioral, participatory, and institutional—would produce synergistic effects exceeding what any single intervention could achieve. Lower energy costs from retrofits would free household resources. Accessible information would enable informed choices. Rewards would reinforce desired behaviors. Community participation would ensure sustained engagement. Institutional coordination would prevent fragmentation.[^1][^2]

**Target Beneficiaries**

**Primary Beneficiaries:** Residents of the Porta Romana-Vettabbia district (approximately 20,000 people), particularly households in retrofitted buildings who experienced reduced energy costs and improved thermal comfort; users of shared mobility services gaining access to convenient, affordable transportation; workers employed in green sectors created through the initiative.

**Secondary Beneficiaries:** Milan municipality gaining institutional capacity, demonstrated models, and enhanced reputation as sustainability leader; private companies (energy, technology, mobility providers) gaining market opportunities and competitive advantages; academic institutions acquiring research data and applied learning opportunities; other European cities accessing replicable models and lessons learned.

**Intended Equity Focus:** While not explicitly framed in equity language, the initiative targeted lower-income neighborhoods and sought to ensure that sustainability benefits reached vulnerable populations. Building retrofits prioritized social housing. Mobility services aimed for affordability. The Digital Social Market explicitly designed reward mechanisms to be accessible to residents with limited digital literacy.[^1][^3][^2]

**Innovation Elements**

**Technological Innovation:** Integration of multiple technologies (smart sensors, renewable energy systems, IoT networks, mobile applications) into coherent systems represented significant technical innovation. The Urban Sharing Platform's ability to enable interoperability across previously disconnected systems (energy, mobility, environmental monitoring) constituted novel technical architecture.

**Social Innovation:** The SharingMI app and Digital Social Market represented innovative approaches to incentivizing behavior change through gamification, peer comparison, and reward mechanisms. Co-design processes engaging residents in solution development, rather than imposing top-down interventions, represented participatory innovation.

**Governance Innovation:** Multi-stakeholder partnership structures, data-sharing agreements between public and private entities, and mechanisms for translating real-time urban data into policy decisions represented governance innovations. The creation of standardized, interoperable data frameworks enabling information exchange across previously siloed organizations constituted institutional innovation.

**Financial Innovation:** While not explicitly detailed in available sources, the leveraging of €24 million in EU seed funding to attract €265 million in additional private investment represented financial innovation in urban sustainability financing.[^2]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers**

Mayor Giuseppe Sala's 2016 public commitment to environmental, social, and economic sustainability created political will for ambitious initiatives. Milan's aspiration to position itself as a global sustainability leader, competing with cities like Copenhagen, Amsterdam, and Stockholm, motivated high-profile demonstration projects. European Commission funding opportunities provided political incentives for municipal participation, offering resources without proportional local budget burden. The initiative's visibility created opportunities for political credit-claiming around sustainability achievements.[^1]

**Economic Drivers**

The €24 million EU funding represented significant resources for municipal investment, with €8.6 million allocated specifically to Milan. The ability to leverage this funding to attract additional private investment (€265 million) demonstrated economic rationale—sustainability initiatives could generate business opportunities and market development. Energy efficiency retrofits promised long-term cost savings for building owners and residents. Shared mobility services represented growing market segments. Technology providers (Siemens, renewable energy companies) saw business development opportunities. Real estate developers recognized that sustainability features enhanced property values and marketability.[^1][^2]

**Social Drivers**

Growing citizen concern about air quality, particularly winter pollution episodes, created demand for municipal action. Health impacts of pollution and sedentary lifestyles motivated interest in solutions improving both environmental and personal health. Community organizations and environmental NGOs advocated for concrete sustainability action. Residents in lower-income neighborhoods, while sometimes skeptical, recognized potential benefits from cost-saving retrofits and improved services. The broader cultural shift toward sustainability, particularly among younger populations, created social momentum.[^1][^3]

**Environmental Drivers**

Milan's persistent air quality challenges, with particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide frequently exceeding EU standards, created urgent environmental imperatives. The city's contribution to Italy's greenhouse gas reduction targets under EU climate directives created compliance pressures. The recognition that business-as-usual trajectories were incompatible with Paris Agreement commitments motivated experimentation with transformative approaches. Climate change impacts on the Po Valley region (water stress, temperature extremes) created awareness of climate adaptation imperatives.[^1]

**Technological Drivers**

The maturation of IoT technologies, renewable energy systems (particularly solar), electric vehicle technology, and mobile application platforms created technical feasibility for integrated smart city solutions. The declining costs of these technologies improved economic viability. Data analytics capabilities enabled new approaches to urban governance. The emergence of interoperability standards and open data frameworks created possibilities for previously incompatible systems to communicate. The Horizon 2020 programme itself, designed to accelerate European innovation, created opportunities for experimenting with cutting-edge technologies.[^1][^2]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework**

The Sharing Cities design reflected several urban planning and sustainability philosophies:

**Integrated Urbanism:** Rather than addressing energy, mobility, and environmental quality as separate challenges, the initiative conceptualized them as interconnected systems requiring coordinated solutions. Building efficiency improvements reduced heating/cooling demands, decreasing peak electricity loads that could be served by renewable energy. Shared mobility reduced parking demands, freeing land for green space. Smart lighting and environmental monitoring created public safety and quality-of-life improvements supporting neighborhood vitality.

**Citizen-Centered Smart Cities:** Explicitly rejecting purely technology-driven approaches, the design centered on citizen needs, preferences, and participation. Rather than imposing solutions, the initiative engaged residents in co-designing interventions, ensuring technical solutions addressed actual problems and incorporated local knowledge.

**Circular Economy and Sharing Principles:** The emphasis on shared mobility (car-sharing, bike-sharing, e-logistics services), shared data platforms, and circular resource flows reflected broader circular economy philosophies. Sharing reduced per-capita resource consumption while maintaining service access.

**Just Transition:** While not using that terminology, the design incorporated principles of equitable sustainability transition through prioritizing social housing retrofits, ensuring affordable access to shared services, and creating employment in green sectors.[^1][^3]

**Physical/Spatial Design**

The Porta Romana-Vettabbia redevelopment incorporated comprehensive spatial reorganization:

**Mixed-Use Development:** The former 216,614 m² railway yard underwent transformation into a mixed-use neighborhood combining residential (private and social housing units), commercial spaces, public facilities, and green space (187,226 m² park). This functional mix created vibrant, diverse neighborhoods reducing single-use zoning and associated transportation demands.

**Multimodal Mobility Integration:** Rather than car-centric design, the district prioritized multimodal transportation. A new transit station integrated metro, bus, and tram connections. Dedicated cycling infrastructure connected to citywide bike networks. Ten mobility areas distributed throughout the district provided electric vehicle charging (60 charging points, including 20 fast-charging stations) and car-sharing vehicle staging. Pedestrian-friendly streetscapes prioritized walking.[^1][^3]

**Green Infrastructure:** The 187,226 m² park provided recreational space, stormwater management, urban cooling, and biodiversity habitat. Green roofs and vertical gardens on buildings provided additional environmental services. Street trees and vegetation supported air quality improvement and urban heat mitigation.

**Energy Infrastructure:** Building retrofits incorporated solar photovoltaic systems, heat recovery ventilation, improved insulation, and smart controls. District-level renewable energy generation and energy storage systems supported collective management. Smart metering infrastructure enabled real-time consumption monitoring and demand response.

**Public Space Design:** The district incorporated public squares, gathering spaces, and community facilities supporting social interaction and civic engagement. Smart street lighting with environmental sensors created safe, monitored public spaces while gathering data on air quality, noise, and traffic patterns.[^1][^3]

**Scope and Phasing**

The project operated through distinct but overlapping phases:

**Phase 1 (2016-2017): Baseline and Design**
Established baseline conditions, conducted detailed needs assessments, engaged resident consultation, finalized technical designs, established governance structures and data-sharing agreements.

**Phase 2 (2017-2019): Infrastructure Implementation**
Executed building retrofits (20 buildings, 25,000 m² total), installed mobility infrastructure (charging stations, bike-sharing facilities, car-sharing vehicles), deployed smart street lighting and environmental sensors, constructed renewable energy systems, launched digital platforms.

**Phase 3 (2019-2020): Platform Operationalization and Scaling**
Launched SharingMI app and Digital Social Market, scaled user enrollment, refined behavioral incentive mechanisms, expanded mobility service coverage, integrated data systems, conducted community engagement campaigns.

**Phase 4 (2020-2021): Monitoring and Evaluation**
Collected comprehensive performance data, assessed environmental, social, and economic impacts, documented lessons learned, prepared replication guidance for follower cities and other interested municipalities.

**Phase 5 (2021 onward): Sustainability and Replication**
Transitioned project management to permanent municipal and private sector structures, ensured long-term financial viability of services, supported replication in follower cities, disseminated findings through publications and networks.[^1][^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed**

**Building Energy Technologies:**
- Building envelope retrofits (insulation, high-performance windows, thermal bridges elimination)
- Heat pump systems for heating/cooling
- Solar photovoltaic systems for electricity generation
- Solar thermal systems for hot water
- Heat recovery ventilation systems
- Smart thermostats and building management systems enabling real-time consumption monitoring and demand response

**Mobility Technologies:**
- Electric vehicle charging infrastructure (60 charging points across 10 mobility areas)
- Electric car-sharing fleet (60 e-vehicles)
- Electric bike-sharing system (14 additional stations, 150 bikes, some with child seats)
- Electric cargo vehicles for goods logistics (10 vehicles)
- Smart parking systems with real-time availability information
- Integrated mobility booking and payment platforms[^1][^3]

**Digital and ICT Infrastructure:**
- IoT sensor networks (LoraWAN) for environmental monitoring (air quality, noise, traffic)
- Smart street lighting with integrated sensors and communication capabilities
- Urban Sharing Platform (USP) providing interoperable data architecture
- SharingMI mobile application for citizen engagement and behavior incentives
- Digital Social Market platform for reward mechanisms
- Real-time data dashboards for municipal decision-makers
- APIs enabling third-party application development[^3][^2]

**Data and Analytics:**
- Building energy consumption monitoring systems
- Mobility pattern analysis
- Environmental quality monitoring (air quality, noise levels, traffic counts)
- User behavior analytics (app usage, service adoption, reward redemption)
- Peer benchmarking systems enabling household comparison of energy consumption[^1][^3]

**Innovation Type Classification**

**Technological Innovation:** The integration of multiple technologies into coherent systems represented significant technical advancement. The Urban Sharing Platform's ability to enable interoperability across previously disconnected domains (energy, mobility, environmental monitoring) through standardized APIs and open data frameworks constituted novel technical architecture. The deployment of LoraWAN networks for distributed environmental sensing represented relatively novel application in urban contexts at the project's 2016 launch.

**Social Innovation:** The SharingMI app and Digital Social Market represented innovative approaches to behavior change through gamification and peer comparison. Rather than relying solely on regulatory mandates or price signals, the system leveraged behavioral economics principles. The co-design processes engaging residents in solution development represented participatory innovation, shifting power from expert-driven to citizen-informed decision-making.

**Governance Innovation:** Multi-stakeholder partnership structures bringing together municipal government, private companies, academic institutions, and NGOs represented governance innovation. Data-sharing agreements between public and private entities, previously rare in Italian municipal contexts, constituted institutional innovation. The creation of standardized data frameworks enabling information exchange across silos represented process innovation.[^1][^3][^2]

**Business Model Innovation:** The mechanisms for attracting private investment to urban sustainability (leveraging €24 million EU funding to attract €265 million private investment) represented financial innovation. Service delivery models combining public subsidy with private operation created sustainability mechanisms.[^2]

**Integration Approach**

Rather than deploying isolated technologies, Sharing Cities emphasized integration:

**Sectoral Integration:** Energy, mobility, and environmental monitoring systems were designed to communicate and coordinate. For example, electric vehicle charging schedules could respond to renewable energy generation patterns. Building energy management systems could coordinate with district-level renewable generation. Mobility patterns informed urban planning and infrastructure investment.

**Data Integration:** The Urban Sharing Platform created unified data architecture enabling information from diverse sources (building energy systems, vehicle telematics, environmental sensors, citizen applications) to be integrated, analyzed, and acted upon. Open data frameworks and standardized APIs enabled third-party developers to create applications using platform data.

**Stakeholder Integration:** Rather than siloed implementation by individual actors, governance structures brought together municipal departments, private companies, academic researchers, and community organizations in coordinated efforts. Regular meetings, shared objectives, and aligned incentives created functional integration despite organizational diversity.

**Citizen Integration:** Rather than treating residents as passive consumers of services, the design incorporated multiple mechanisms for citizen participation: co-design processes for intervention selection, real-time consumption data access, peer comparison benchmarking, reward mechanisms incentivizing participation, and feedback mechanisms informing ongoing refinement.[^1][^3][^2]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting**

The Sharing Cities initiative employed diverse procurement approaches reflecting the multiplicity of required services:

**Infrastructure Contracts:** Building retrofits, renewable energy system installation, and mobility infrastructure deployment were procured through competitive bidding processes complying with Italian public procurement law. Siemens and other major technology providers competed for contracts to supply and integrate smart systems.

**Service Contracts:** Ongoing operation of mobility services, digital platforms, and data management were procured through service agreements with private operators. The Urban Sharing Platform was developed through contracts with specialized technology firms (Cefriel, Kiunsys, Teicos).

**Partnership Agreements:** Rather than traditional procurement, some elements operated through partnership agreements establishing shared governance and benefit-sharing. Academic partners (Politecnico di Milano) contributed research and technical expertise through institutional partnerships rather than traditional contracts.

**Hybrid Models:** Some services combined public funding with private operation. For example, the SharingMI app and Digital Social Market operated through public-private partnerships where municipal funding supported platform development and initial user acquisition, while private operators managed ongoing service delivery.[^1]

**Stakeholder Engagement**

**Resident Engagement:**
- Community consultations during design phase soliciting input on intervention priorities
- Participatory processes for social housing residents regarding retrofit specifications and building management changes
- Information campaigns explaining initiative objectives, technologies, and participation mechanisms
- Feedback mechanisms (surveys, focus groups, community meetings) enabling ongoing resident input
- Incentive structures (rewards, recognition) encouraging participation in digital platforms and behavior change initiatives[^1][^3]

**Business Engagement:**
- Consultations with mobility operators regarding service integration and interoperability requirements
- Engagement with energy companies regarding smart grid integration and demand response
- Outreach to real estate developers regarding building retrofit standards and green building practices
- Collaboration with technology companies on platform development and data standards

**Municipal Engagement:**
- Inter-departmental coordination across planning, energy, mobility, and environmental departments
- Capacity-building for municipal staff on smart city technologies and data-driven governance
- Integration with existing municipal systems and procedures

**Academic Engagement:**
- Research collaboration between Politecnico di Milano and project implementation teams
- Student involvement in project activities
- Knowledge documentation and dissemination[^1]

**Participatory Elements**

**Co-Design Processes:** Rather than imposing predetermined solutions, the initiative engaged residents in solution design. For example, residents participated in selecting retrofit specifications for their buildings, choosing among options balancing comfort improvements, cost, and environmental impact. Community input shaped the design of public spaces and mobility infrastructure.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges**

**Data Integration Complexity:** Integrating data from heterogeneous sources (building energy management systems, vehicle telematics, environmental sensors, citizen applications) proved technically complex. Different manufacturers' equipment used incompatible protocols and data formats. The Urban Sharing Platform required substantial custom integration work to enable interoperability. Initial deployments experienced data quality issues, with sensor failures, communication dropouts, and calibration problems requiring ongoing troubleshooting.

**Smart Meter and Monitoring System Reliability:** Real-time energy consumption monitoring systems, while conceptually straightforward, encountered reliability challenges in practice. Smart meters malfunctioned or reported inaccurate data. Wireless communication networks (LoraWAN) experienced coverage gaps in certain locations. Integration with legacy utility company systems, designed for different purposes, created technical friction. These technical issues delayed the deployment of real-time feedback mechanisms intended to motivate behavior change.

**Renewable Energy Integration:** Integrating distributed solar photovoltaic systems with grid infrastructure created technical challenges. Variable renewable generation required sophisticated demand response and storage systems to maintain grid stability. Coordination between building-level solar systems, district-level storage, and grid-level management required new operational procedures and control algorithms.[^3]

**Mobility Platform Integration:** Creating unified booking and payment systems across multiple mobility operators (public transit, car-sharing, bike-sharing, scooters) required standardization of data formats, payment protocols, and service interfaces. Operators accustomed to proprietary systems initially resisted standardization. Integration with municipal parking systems and traffic management required coordination across previously separate municipal departments.[^5]

**Financial Challenges**

**Budget Constraints:** While €24 million in EU funding represented substantial resources, the ambition of the initiative—retrofitting buildings, deploying mobility infrastructure, developing digital platforms, and supporting community engagement across a district of 20,000 residents—created constant resource constraints. Difficult prioritization decisions required selecting among competing interventions.

**Building Retrofit Economics:** While energy efficiency retrofits generate long-term cost savings, upfront capital costs created barriers. For lower-income residents and small building owners, the capital requirements exceeded available financing. Retrofit costs exceeded initial budget estimates, requiring scope adjustments or reduced intervention depth. Financing mechanisms (green mortgages, energy service contracts) existed but required awareness and access that many residents lacked.

**Shared Mobility Service Viability:** Shared mobility services, while environmentally beneficial, often operate at financial losses requiring ongoing subsidies. The initiative struggled to establish pricing and service levels balancing affordability objectives with financial sustainability. Competition from conventional car ownership and the convenience of personal vehicles created persistent demand challenges for shared alternatives.

**Digital Platform Development Costs:** Software development for the Urban Sharing Platform and SharingMI app exceeded initial cost estimates. Ongoing maintenance, updates, and feature development required sustained funding beyond the project period. Open-source approaches and modular architecture helped manage costs but required technical expertise not always available within municipal organizations.[^1][^3]

**Social and Political Challenges**

**Gentrification and Displacement Concerns:** The most significant social challenge involved resident concerns that Sharing Cities investments would trigger neighborhood gentrification, leading to property value increases, rent escalation, and displacement of lower-income residents. While the initiative prioritized social housing and included anti-displacement policies, concerns persisted that benefits would ultimately accrue disproportionately to developers and new residents rather than existing communities. This tension between sustainability improvement and equitable benefit distribution proved difficult to fully resolve.[^1][^3]

**Community Skepticism and Distrust:** Some residents, particularly in lower-income communities with histories of broken promises from municipal authorities, approached the initiative skeptically. Residents questioned whether technology-focused solutions genuinely addressed their priorities (affordability, safety, employment) or served corporate interests. Building trust required sustained engagement and demonstrated benefits, not merely rhetoric about sustainability.

**Digital Divide and Exclusion:** The reliance on mobile applications (SharingMI) and digital platforms for accessing services and incentives created exclusion for residents with limited digital literacy, limited smartphone access, or language barriers. While the initiative incorporated alternative access mechanisms (SMS-based systems, multilingual support, in-person assistance), digital divides persisted. Older residents and recent migrants faced particular challenges accessing digital services.

**Behavioral Change Resistance:** The assumption that providing information and financial incentives would motivate behavior change proved partially incorrect. Many residents continued conventional practices despite access to sustainable alternatives. Car ownership persisted despite car-sharing availability. Energy consumption patterns changed modestly despite real-time feedback. Changing deeply habituated behaviors proved more difficult than anticipated.

**Political Opposition and Institutional Resistance:** Some political actors questioned whether sustainability investments represented appropriate use of public resources, preferring conventional infrastructure or social spending. Utility companies and traditional mobility operators sometimes resisted changes threatening their business models. Municipal departments accustomed to independent operation sometimes resisted the coordination and data-sharing requirements.[^1][^3]

**Administrative Challenges**

**Procurement Complexity:** Italian public procurement law, while ensuring transparency and fairness, created lengthy processes that sometimes conflicted with project timelines. Tendering for multiple services and technologies required months of preparation and evaluation. Contractor changes and disputes occasionally delayed implementation.

**Coordination Across Silos:** Municipal government, while nominally unified, operates through departments with distinct budgets, priorities, and procedures. Coordinating across planning, energy, mobility, and environmental departments required new governance mechanisms and cultural shifts toward cross-departmental collaboration.

**Condominium Decision-Making:** In Italian residential areas, many buildings are condominiums requiring supermajority resident approval for major improvements. Achieving consensus for building retrofits proved time-consuming. Some building owners or residents withheld approval, preventing comprehensive district-level interventions and creating uneven outcomes.

**Utility Company Coordination:** Energy and water utilities, often operating as semi-autonomous entities with their own governance, required alignment with municipal objectives. Integrating utility data into municipal platforms required new data-sharing agreements and technical coordination. Utility company incentives (revenue-based on consumption) sometimes conflicted with conservation objectives.[^1][^3]

**Environmental Challenges**

**Brownfield Site Remediation:** The Porta Romana site's history as a railway yard created soil contamination requiring environmental remediation before development. Remediation costs and timelines added complexity to the redevelopment process. Contamination of varying severity in different site areas required differentiated approaches.

**Climate Variability:** The project encountered unexpected weather events. Unusually mild winters reduced heating demands and the visibility of retrofit benefits. Extreme heat events during summer months highlighted the importance of cooling systems and urban cooling strategies, requiring mid-project adjustments to intervention priorities.[^1]

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Problem-Solving Approaches**

**Technical Problem-Solving:**

To address data integration complexity, the project team adopted a phased approach to platform development. Rather than attempting to integrate all systems simultaneously, the team prioritized critical connections (building energy systems, mobility platforms) and developed additional integrations iteratively. Open standards (JSON APIs, REST protocols) and middleware solutions reduced dependency on proprietary system modifications.

For smart meter and monitoring reliability challenges, the team implemented redundant communication pathways and local data storage to ensure data persistence despite temporary network failures. Regular maintenance protocols and sensor replacement programs addressed hardware reliability. Data validation and anomaly detection algorithms identified and corrected erroneous readings.

Renewable energy integration challenges prompted adoption of advanced control systems and demand response mechanisms. Battery storage systems and thermal storage (heated water, cooled air) provided flexibility to match variable renewable generation with consumption patterns. Coordination with grid operators and adoption of grid services markets created revenue streams offsetting storage costs.

Mobility platform integration proceeded through phased standardization. Rather than requiring all operators to adopt new systems simultaneously, the project team worked with willing early adopters, demonstrating benefits and building momentum. Standardized data formats and APIs were adopted iteratively, with legacy systems gradually replaced or integrated through translation layers.

**Financial Problem-Solving:**

Addressing retrofit cost barriers, the project team developed innovative financing mechanisms. Energy service contracts (ESCos) allowed building owners to finance retrofits through energy savings, reducing upfront capital requirements. Municipal subsidies for lower-income households reduced individual financial burdens while ensuring equitable access. Aggregated procurement of retrofit services reduced per-unit costs through economies of scale.

For shared mobility service viability, the project team experimented with diverse pricing models and service configurations. Subsidized initial pricing built user bases and demonstrated value. Integration with public transit (unified payment systems, integrated journey planning) increased convenience and ridership. Targeted subsidies for lower-income users ensured affordability.

Digital platform costs were managed through modular architecture enabling code reuse, open-source components reducing licensing costs, and outsourcing non-core development to specialized firms. Phased feature deployment prioritized high-value functionalities, deferring less critical capabilities to later phases.

**Social and Political Problem-Solving:**

Addressing gentrification and displacement concerns, the initiative incorporated explicit anti-displacement policies. Social housing retrofits and preservation of affordable units ensured housing access for lower-income residents. Community land trusts and cooperative housing models provided alternatives to conventional real estate ownership. Preference hiring policies and workforce development programs ensured local residents benefited from employment created by the initiative. Community benefit agreements formalized commitments to equitable benefit distribution.

Building trust and addressing community skepticism required sustained, genuine engagement. Rather than one-way information provision, the project team engaged residents in co-design processes, soliciting input and demonstrating responsiveness. Visible improvements and tangible benefits (reduced heating bills, improved mobility options, cleaner air) built credibility. Community leaders and trusted local organizations served as intermediaries, lending legitimacy to the initiative.

Digital divide challenges prompted development of alternative access mechanisms. SMS-based systems enabled participation for residents without smartphones. Multilingual support and community centers providing computer access and digital literacy training reduced language and access barriers. Partnerships with community organizations ensured services reached marginalized populations.

Behavioral change resistance prompted refinement of incentive mechanisms. Rather than assuming information provision would motivate change, the project team developed more sophisticated behavioral interventions. Gamification elements (achievement badges, leaderboards) leveraged social motivations. Peer comparison benchmarking leveraged conformity effects. Defaults (opt-out rather than opt-in for sustainable options) reduced friction. Sustained engagement through community events and social networks built cultural shifts toward sustainability norms.

**Administrative Problem-Solving:**

Procurement complexity was addressed through early planning and dedicated procurement expertise. Project teams began procurement processes well in advance of implementation needs, accommodating Italian administrative timelines. Streamlined procurement procedures for certain categories (framework agreements, pre-qualified vendor lists) reduced delays for routine procurements.

Coordination across municipal silos was facilitated through formal governance structures (cross-departmental steering committees, shared performance metrics) and cultural change initiatives. Training programs built understanding of smart city approaches across departments. Shared budgets or cost-sharing arrangements aligned financial incentives toward collaboration. Dedicated coordination staff bridged departmental boundaries.

Condominium decision-making challenges were addressed through intensive community engagement and financial incentives. Information sessions educated residents about retrofit benefits. Financing support reduced individual financial burdens. Supermajority requirements were met through sustained outreach and relationship-building. For buildings where consensus proved impossible, alternative retrofit approaches (common area retrofits, individual unit retrofits) proceeded where full-building retrofits were infeasible.

Utility company coordination improved through formal data-sharing agreements, regulatory clarity, and financial mechanisms aligning utility interests with conservation objectives. Decoupling regulatory models (separating utility profits from consumption volumes) reduced financial disincentives to conservation. Real-time data access enabled utilities to optimize operations. Demand response revenue streams provided new business opportunities offsetting consumption reductions.

**Design Modifications**

**Scope Adjustments:** Faced with budget constraints and implementation challenges, the project team adjusted scope. Rather than retrofitting all buildings in the district, the initiative prioritized social housing and publicly-owned buildings where institutional support facilitated implementation. This pragmatic approach ensured visible impacts despite resource limitations.

**Timeline Extensions:** Implementation timelines were extended beyond initial projections to accommodate technical challenges, community engagement, and administrative processes. The monitoring period was extended beyond initial two years to capture longer-term impacts and sustainability.

**Technology Substitutions:** Where initially planned technologies proved infeasible or excessively costly, alternative approaches were adopted. For example, district-level smart grid management proved more complex than anticipated; the project shifted emphasis toward building-level smart systems with looser district-level coordination.

**Service Model Adjustments:** Shared mobility services underwent configuration adjustments based on actual demand patterns. Car-sharing vehicle locations were adjusted based on usage data. Bike-sharing station density was modified to match demand. Service hours were optimized based on usage patterns.[^1][^3]

**Lessons Learned During Implementation**

**Importance of Community Engagement:** Early implementation experiences reinforced that genuine, sustained community engagement was essential for success. Top-down technological deployment without community participation generated resistance and limited adoption. Residents needed to understand rationale, participate in design decisions, and perceive tangible benefits.

**Data Quality Matters:** The initiative learned that data quality and reliability were fundamental to digital platform success. Unreliable sensors, communication failures, and data errors undermined confidence in systems and limited decision-making value. Substantial investment in data quality management proved necessary.

**Behavioral Change Complexity:** Experience demonstrated that behavior change was more complex than anticipated. Information provision and financial incentives alone proved insufficient. Sustained engagement, social influence, and cultural change required years rather than months. Defaults and structural changes (making sustainable options easier) proved more effective than voluntary behavior modification.

**Financing Innovation Necessity:** The initiative learned that innovative financing mechanisms were essential for retrofits and sustainable infrastructure to reach scale. Traditional municipal budgets and private capital alone proved insufficient. Energy service contracts, green bonds, and public-private partnerships enabled projects that conventional financing could not support.

**Institutional Persistence Required:** Creating sustained institutional change—shifting municipal governance toward integrated approaches, establishing data-sharing norms, building cross-departmental collaboration—required years of effort and persisted beyond individual project staff. Institutional innovations required formal policies, training, and cultural shifts to become permanent rather than temporary project arrangements.

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

**Physical Outputs**

The Sharing Cities initiative produced substantial physical infrastructure:

**Building Retrofits:** 20 buildings underwent deep energy retrofits totaling 25,000 m² of private and public building area. Retrofits incorporated building envelope improvements (insulation, high-performance windows), heat pump systems, solar photovoltaic systems, heat recovery ventilation, and smart building management systems. Retrofit work achieved up to 70% reduction in building energy needs, with aggregate energy savings of 6.1 GWh/year across all lighthouse cities' retrofitted buildings.[^1][^3][^2]

**Mobility Infrastructure:** The initiative deployed 60 electric vehicle charging points across 10 mobility areas distributed throughout the district, including 20 fast-charging stations. A fleet of 60 electric vehicles (cars and cargo vehicles) provided shared mobility services. The bike-sharing system expanded with 14 additional stations and approximately 150 electric bikes, some equipped with child seats. Smart parking systems with real-time availability information were installed at 190 parking stalls.[^1][^3][^4]

**Energy Infrastructure:** Solar photovoltaic systems were installed on retrofitted buildings, with capacity to generate approximately 50% of buildings' electricity needs. Thermal storage systems and battery storage provided load-shifting capability. Smart metering infrastructure enabled real-time consumption monitoring across 20,000+ residents.

**Smart Infrastructure:** Smart street lighting was installed throughout the district, with approximately 11 electric vehicles for goods logistics. The lighting system incorporated LoraWAN communication networks, environmental sensors (air quality, noise, traffic monitoring), and connectivity services. Public space improvements included green infrastructure, pedestrian pathways, and gathering spaces.[^1][^3][^4]

**Service Delivery**

**Digital Platforms:** The Urban Sharing Platform was developed and deployed, providing interoperable data architecture enabling integration across energy, mobility, and environmental monitoring systems. The SharingMI mobile application was launched, reaching over 1,100 users within three months of launch and accumulating over 3,600 shared stories. The Digital Social Market platform enabled users to earn "claps" (converted to points) redeemable for local and global benefits.[^3][^2]

**Mobility Services:** Shared mobility services (car-sharing, bike-sharing, e-logistics) became operational, providing residents with alternatives to private vehicle ownership. Integration with public transit through unified booking and payment systems enhanced convenience.

**Energy Management Services:** Real-time energy consumption monitoring, peer benchmarking, and demand response services became available to residents. Building management systems provided automated optimization of heating, cooling, and electricity consumption.

**Community Engagement Services:** Community centers, information sessions, digital literacy training, and peer networks provided support for resident participation in sustainability initiatives.[^1][^3]

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies**

**Municipal Governance Capacity:** Sharing Cities depended on relatively strong municipal governance capacity, sophisticated administrative systems, and political commitment to sustainability. Cities with weak governance, limited administrative capacity, or political instability might struggle to replicate the initiative's complexity and multi-year commitment requirements.

**Financial Resources:** The €24 million EU funding and €265 million private investment leverage depended on access to European funding mechanisms and investor interest in urban sustainability. Cities outside the EU or with limited access to green financing might face greater financial constraints.

**Existing Infrastructure:** The initiative built on Milan's existing infrastructure including public transit systems, utility networks, and digital communications systems. Cities with underdeveloped infrastructure might require more foundational investments before implementing smart city solutions.

**Real Estate Market Conditions:** The Porta Romana-Vettabbia redevelopment occurred within a functioning real estate market with sufficient private investment interest to support development. Cities with depressed real estate markets or limited private capital might struggle to attract investment.

**Resident Characteristics:** The initiative's success in digital platform adoption depended partly on residents' digital literacy and comfort with technology. Cities with lower digital literacy or different cultural attitudes toward technology might experience lower adoption rates.

**Policy Environment:** EU directives on building energy performance, renewable energy, and sustainable mobility created policy context supporting the initiative. Cities in regions with less supportive policy environments might face regulatory barriers.

**Critical Success Factors**

**Political Commitment:** Sustained political commitment from municipal leadership proved essential. Without mayoral support and cross-party consensus on sustainability, the multi-year initiative faced political vulnerability.

**Community Engagement:** Genuine, sustained community engagement emerged as critical success factor. Top-down technological deployment without community participation generated resistance. Effective engagement required dedicated resources and authentic responsiveness to community input.

**Institutional Coordination:** Cross-departmental coordination and alignment of incentives proved essential for integration across energy, mobility, and environmental sectors. Siloed municipal operations impeded progress.

**Data Quality and Interoperability:** Reliable data systems and interoperable technology platforms proved essential for digital solutions to function effectively. Technical excellence and sustained investment in data infrastructure were necessary.

**Financial Sustainability Mechanisms:** Long-term viability depended on establishing revenue mechanisms and operational efficiencies enabling services to continue beyond initial funding. Reliance on perpetual subsidies created vulnerability.

**Replicability Potential:** Despite contextual dependencies, the initiative demonstrated substantial replicability potential. The transfer to follower cities (Burgas, Bordeaux, Warsaw) demonstrated that core approaches could be adapted to different contexts. The documented playbooks and lessons learned provided guidance for other cities.[^2]

**Scalability**

**Vertical Scalability:** The initiative demonstrated potential for scaling within cities. The Porta Romana-Vettabbia district served as initial demonstration; subsequent phases could expand interventions to additional neighborhoods. Scaling within Milan appeared feasible given institutional capacity and funding availability.

**Horizontal Scalability:** The transfer of approaches to follower cities demonstrated horizontal scalability. Adapted versions of Sharing Cities approaches were implemented in Burgas (Bulgaria), Bordeaux (France), and Warsaw (Poland), demonstrating transferability across different national and local contexts.

**Sectoral Scalability:** The approaches developed for energy, mobility, and environmental monitoring could extend to other urban sectors (water management, waste, food systems), creating more comprehensive smart city systems.

**Technology Scalability:** Digital platforms and IoT systems could scale to encompass larger geographic areas and more residents, though technical complexity and data management challenges would increase.

**Barriers to Scaling:** Financial constraints, governance complexity, and community engagement challenges increased with scale. Scaling from demonstration district (20,000 residents) to city-wide (1.4 million residents) required proportional increases in resources and coordination mechanisms.[^1][^2]

#### **Comparative Analysis**

**Similar Initiatives**

**Copenhagen's CPH 2025 Initiative:** Copenhagen implemented comprehensive sustainability initiatives emphasizing cycling infrastructure, renewable energy, and climate adaptation. Like Sharing Cities, Copenhagen combined infrastructure investment with behavioral change incentives. However, Copenhagen's initiatives developed over longer timeframes and benefited from strong Scandinavian cycling culture and high baseline sustainability awareness.

**Amsterdam's Smart City Programs:** Amsterdam implemented smart city initiatives emphasizing data-driven governance, circular economy, and citizen engagement. Like Sharing Cities, Amsterdam developed multi-stakeholder partnerships and integrated energy-mobility-ICT approaches. However, Amsterdam's initiatives operated within different governance frameworks and real estate market conditions.

**Vienna's Sustainable Urban Development:** Vienna implemented comprehensive urban renewal combining social housing, public transit, and green infrastructure. Like Sharing Cities, Vienna prioritized equitable benefit distribution and community engagement. However, Vienna's initiatives operated within stronger social housing traditions and different financing mechanisms.

**Best Practices**

**Community Engagement Excellence:** Sharing Cities demonstrated best practice in sustained, genuine community engagement. Rather than one-way information provision, the initiative engaged residents in co-design, created feedback mechanisms, and demonstrated responsiveness to community input. This approach could serve as model for other initiatives.

**Multi-Stakeholder Governance:** The consortium structure bringing together 35 partners across sectors demonstrated effective multi-stakeholder governance. Regular coordination, shared objectives, and aligned incentives enabled collaboration despite organizational diversity. This governance model could be adapted to other contexts.

**Integrated Sectoral Approach:** The integration of energy, mobility, and environmental monitoring through unified data platforms and governance structures demonstrated how previously siloed sectors could be coordinated for greater impact. This integration approach could extend to other urban systems.

**Behavioral Incentive Design:** The SharingMI app and Digital Social Market demonstrated sophisticated use of behavioral economics principles (gamification, peer comparison, reward systems) to motivate sustainable behavior. The design could inform other initiatives seeking to change behavior.

**Cautionary Lessons**

**Gentrification Risk:** The initiative's success in improving neighborhood conditions created gentrification pressures that anti-displacement policies only partially mitigated. Other initiatives should anticipate gentrification risks and implement robust anti-displacement mechanisms proactively rather than reactively.

**Digital Divide Persistence:** Despite efforts to provide alternative access mechanisms, digital divides persisted. Other initiatives should recognize that digital solutions inherently create barriers for some populations and plan accordingly with non-digital alternatives.

**Behavioral Change Complexity:** The initiative learned that behavior change was more complex than anticipated. Information and incentives alone proved insufficient; sustained engagement, social influence, and cultural change required years. Other initiatives should manage expectations about behavior change timelines and invest accordingly.

**Sustainability Challenge:** Long-term sustainability of services and engagement proved challenging beyond the project period. Other initiatives should prioritize financial and institutional sustainability mechanisms from inception rather than treating sustainability as afterthought.

**Equity Tension:** The tension between improving neighborhood conditions (which created gentrification pressures) and maintaining equity remained unresolved. Other initiatives should acknowledge this tension and invest in robust anti-displacement and equitable benefit-sharing mechanisms.[^1][^3][^2]

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Comprehensive Integration:** The initiative's most significant strength was its comprehensive integration of energy, mobility, and environmental monitoring systems through unified governance structures and digital platforms. Rather than implementing isolated interventions, Sharing Cities recognized interconnections and created synergistic solutions. Building retrofits reduced heating/cooling demands that could be served by renewable energy. Shared mobility reduced parking demands and traffic congestion. Smart monitoring enabled optimization across sectors. This integration approach produced greater impact than isolated interventions would achieve.

**Community Engagement:** The initiative demonstrated genuine commitment to community engagement beyond token consultation. Participatory co-design processes engaged residents in solution development rather than imposing expert-determined interventions. Community meetings, feedback mechanisms, and digital platforms created ongoing channels for resident input. The initiative's emphasis on citizen-centered rather than purely technology-driven approaches built legitimacy and supported sustained participation.

**Institutional Innovation:** The multi-stakeholder governance structure bringing together 35 partners across public, private, academic, and civil society sectors represented significant institutional innovation. Regular coordination mechanisms, shared objectives, and aligned incentives enabled collaboration across organizations with different mandates and interests. This collaborative governance model created conditions for sustained coordination beyond individual project staff.

**Documented Learning:** The initiative's comprehensive documentation of approaches, challenges, and lessons learned created valuable knowledge resources for other cities. Playbooks, technical specifications, and impact reports enabled knowledge transfer to follower cities and broader urban development community. This emphasis on learning and replication amplified impact beyond the specific demonstration district.

**Quantified Environmental Impact:** The initiative achieved substantial, quantified environmental results. 70% building energy reduction, 4,500 tons CO₂ emissions saved through shared mobility, and 50% renewable energy generation represented significant environmental improvements. These documented results provided evidence for the feasibility of ambitious sustainability targets.

**Financial Leverage:** The ability to leverage €24 million EU funding to attract €265 million in private investment demonstrated effective financing innovation. This leverage multiplied public resources and demonstrated investor confidence in urban sustainability as viable business opportunity.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Incomplete Equity Outcomes:** Despite equity objectives, benefits were not uniformly distributed. Residents in retrofitted social housing units experienced significant benefits, but residents in non-retrofitted buildings received limited direct benefits. Shared mobility services benefited digitally literate residents more than those with limited technology access. Gentrification pressures and displacement concerns persisted despite anti-displacement policies. The initiative partially failed to achieve equitable benefit distribution, its stated social objective.

**Limited Behavioral Change:** The initiative's assumption that information provision and financial incentives would motivate substantial behavior change proved partially incorrect. Energy consumption changes were modest despite real-time feedback. Car ownership persisted despite car-sharing availability. Behavioral change proved more resistant than anticipated, suggesting that the initiative's behavioral change theories were incomplete.

**Gentrification Inadequately Addressed:** The initiative's success in improving neighborhood conditions created gentrification pressures that anti-displacement policies only partially mitigated. Property value appreciation, rent escalation, and resident displacement occurred despite policy interventions. The tension between improving neighborhoods (which creates gentrification pressures) and maintaining equity remained fundamentally unresolved.

**Digital Divide Persistence:** Despite efforts to provide alternative access mechanisms, digital divides persisted. Residents with limited digital literacy or technology access experienced disadvantages in accessing digital platform benefits. This limitation particularly affected elderly residents, recent migrants, and lower-income households.

**Limited Replication in Follower Cities:** While the initiative transferred approaches to follower cities, comprehensive impact data for follower cities remains limited in available sources. The extent to which Sharing Cities approaches achieved similar results in different contexts remains unclear, suggesting that replication challenges may be greater than anticipated.

**Sustainability Uncertainty:** Long-term sustainability of services and community engagement beyond the project period remained uncertain. While institutional mechanisms and financial models were developed, ongoing municipal budget constraints and declining community engagement created vulnerabilities. The initiative did not fully resolve long-term sustainability challenges.

**Scope Limitations:** The initiative focused on a single demonstration district (Porta Romana-Vettabbia, 20,000 residents) rather than city-wide implementation. While demonstration districts serve important learning functions, scaling to encompass entire cities would require proportional increases in resources and coordination complexity that remain uncertain.

**Limited Attention to Existing Inequalities:** While the initiative addressed some equity concerns, it did not fundamentally address underlying economic inequalities that generate housing affordability challenges and limit lower-income residents' capacity to participate in sustainability initiatives. Addressing root causes of inequality would require broader economic and social policy changes beyond the initiative's scope.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

**Distributional Equity:** The initiative achieved mixed results on distributional equity (fair distribution of benefits and burdens). Lower-income residents in retrofitted social housing units experienced significant benefits (reduced energy costs, improved comfort). However, residents in non-retrofitted buildings received limited direct benefits. Shared mobility services benefited digitally literate, smartphone-owning residents more than others. Overall, benefits were distributed unevenly, with lower-income residents receiving fewer benefits than higher-income residents.

**Procedural Equity:** The initiative performed better on procedural equity (fair processes for decision-making). Community engagement processes engaged residents in co-design and provided feedback mechanisms. However, participation rates varied, with lower-income and marginalized residents sometimes underrepresented in engagement processes. The quality of participural participation varied, with some residents experiencing genuine influence while others' input was incorporated superficially.

**Recognition Equity:** The initiative recognized residents as stakeholders deserving participation in decisions affecting their neighborhoods. Community engagement processes and digital platforms created mechanisms for residents to express voices and perspectives. However, recognition was incomplete, with some residents' concerns (particularly gentrification and displacement fears) receiving inadequate institutional response.

**Environmental Justice:** The initiative improved environmental conditions (air quality, green space, noise reduction) in the Porta Romana-Vettabbia district, addressing environmental justice concerns. Lower-income residents, who typically experience disproportionate environmental burdens, benefited from improved environmental quality. However, gentrification pressures created new injustices as improved conditions attracted investment and displacement pressures.

**Climate Justice:** The initiative contributed to global climate justice by reducing greenhouse gas emissions and demonstrating feasible pathways to decarbonization. However, the initiative did not address how climate action costs and benefits would be distributed globally or across different populations.

**Intergenerational Justice:** The initiative advanced intergenerational justice by reducing greenhouse gas emissions and building sustainable infrastructure benefiting future generations. However, gentrification and displacement of existing residents created injustices for current generations, potentially violating principles of intragenerational justice.

**Overall Assessment:** The initiative achieved partial but incomplete equity and justice outcomes. It advanced environmental justice and procedural equity while falling short on distributional equity and adequately addressing gentrification-related injustices. The fundamental tension between improving neighborhoods (which creates gentrification pressures) and maintaining equity remained unresolved, suggesting that addressing equity and justice comprehensively requires broader policy changes beyond individual initiatives.

#### **Future Outlook**

**Long-Term Trajectory**

**Institutional Persistence:** The integration of Sharing Cities approaches into permanent municipal structures and policies suggests that core institutional changes will persist. Building standards, mobility policies, and governance mechanisms incorporating project learnings appear durable. However, institutional mechanisms for ongoing participation remain in place, and community organizations continue advocating for sustainability initiatives. Community engagement will likely evolve toward lower-intensity but sustained participation.

**Technology Evolution:** Digital platforms and IoT systems will require ongoing updates and feature development to remain competitive and functional. Technology obsolescence will create periodic replacement needs. Emerging technologies (artificial intelligence, advanced analytics) may enhance capabilities but will require investment and expertise.

**Gentrification Continuation:** Gentrification pressures will likely continue, with ongoing property value appreciation, rent escalation, and resident displacement. Anti-displacement policies will provide some protection but will not fully prevent gentrification. **Ongoing Challenges**

**Financial Sustainability:** Long-term financial viability of services and engagement remains uncertain. While revenue mechanisms and operational efficiencies have been developed, ongoing municipal budget constraints create vulnerabilities. Some services (community engagement, equity-focused programs) may face funding pressures.

**Behavioral Change Persistence:** Whether behavior changes achieved during the project period will persist beyond the project depends on whether structural changes (availability of shared mobility, renewable energy systems, smart controls) become normalized and whether behavioral incentives continue. **Opportunities**

**Technology Enhancement:** Emerging technologies (advanced analytics, artificial intelligence, 5G communications) could enhance smart city capabilities. Integration of emerging technologies could improve system performance and create new capabilities.

**Policy Integration:** Sharing Cities experiences could inform broader policy frameworks at municipal, national, and EU levels. Building standards, mobility regulations, and climate policies incorporating project learnings could amplify impact across wider populations.

**Investment Attraction:** The demonstrated viability of sustainable urban development could attract continued private investment. Real estate developers, technology companies, and investors may view sustainable urban development as attractive market opportunity, providing ongoing capital for expansion.

**International Leadership:** Milan's role as global sustainability leader could be strengthened through continued Sharing Cities implementation and replication. International visibility and influence could attract talent, investment, and partnerships supporting continued development.[^1][^3][^2]

This comprehensive case study demonstrates that Sharing Cities represents a significant but incomplete approach to urban sustainability. The initiative achieved substantial environmental improvements, demonstrated institutional innovation, and created replicable models. However, it encountered persistent challenges in achieving equitable outcomes, sustaining behavioral change, and preventing gentrification-related displacement. The initiative's ultimate success depends on whether institutional changes persist, whether services achieve long-term financial sustainability, and whether equity outcomes can be maintained despite gentrification pressures. The transferability of Sharing Cities approaches to other contexts appears promising but requires careful adaptation to local conditions and proactive attention to equity and sustainability challenges that the initiative encountered.
