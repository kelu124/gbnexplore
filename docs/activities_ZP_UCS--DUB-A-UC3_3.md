# Executive summary## Tallaght District Heating Using Data Center Waste Heat

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** Tallaght District Heating Scheme (TDHS)
- **Alternative Names:** Tallaght District Heating Network, HeatWorks Energy Centre
- **Location:** Tallaght, South Dublin, Ireland
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 53.2857° N, 6.3731° W (Tallaght, Dublin)
- **Timeline:**
  - Project initiation: 2018–2019 (planning and design)
  - Construction started: May 2021[^1]
  - Phase 1 operational: December 2022 – early 2023[^2][^3][^4]
  - Full connection of affordable apartments: Early 2025 expected[^3]
- **Project Scale:**
  - Initial coverage: 32,800 m² of public buildings[^3][^4]
  - Planned total: 47,000 m² (including commercial and residential buildings)[^5][^6]
  - Residential impact: 133 affordable apartments (2025), up to 3,500 homes in 3–5 years[^3][^7]
  - Investment value: Not publicly disclosed; funded by multiple sources (see below)
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - South Dublin County Council (SDCC) – project initiator/owner[^3][^4]
  - Codema – Dublin’s energy agency (project partner, technical lead)[^5][^8]
  - Amazon Web Services (AWS) – data center host, heat provider[^3][^6][^4]
  - Fortum – Finnish energy company (design, construction, operation, maintenance)[^3][^9]
- **Funding Sources:**
  - European Union Interreg NWE programme (HeatNet)[^3][^2]
  - Project Ireland 2040 Climate Action Fund[^3]
  - Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland (SEAI)[^3]
  - South Dublin County Council (direct funds)[^3]
  - Additional public-private partnership contributions (AWS, Fortum)

#### **Executive Summary**

The Tallaght District Heating Scheme (TDHS) in Dublin, Ireland is the country’s first large-scale urban heating network to utilize **waste heat from a data center**—specifically, Amazon Web Services (AWS)—as its principal thermal source. Managed by South Dublin County Council in partnership with Codema and Fortum, TDHS captures excess heat generated by the AWS data center’s IT operations, which would otherwise be vented into the atmosphere, and redistributes it through highly-insulated underground pipes to a variety of public, commercial, and residential buildings in Tallaght. This innovative approach delivers substantial **carbon savings**, with an estimated annual reduction of 1,400–1,500 tonnes of CO₂, representing up to 60% less emissions compared to traditional fossil-fuel-based heating[^5][^3][^4]. 

The system is designed for scalability, with Phase 1 serving municipal buildings and Technological University Dublin (TU Dublin) and future phases set to connect affordable housing, additional homes, and potentially hospitals. HeatWorks, Ireland’s first publicly-owned, not-for-profit energy utility, ensures the system operates for community benefit, offering **lower heating costs** and improved energy resilience[^4]. The initiative demonstrates how **public-private collaboration** and advanced energy systems can transform urban sustainability, aligning with Ireland’s Climate Action Plan targets and offering a replicable model for other cities facing similar challenges. The project’s success highlights the potential of **district heating networks** to deliver environmental, economic, and social benefits, while leveraging the growing waste heat resource from Ireland’s expanding data center sector[^5][^3][^6][^4].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Dublin is Ireland’s capital, with a metropolitan population of over 1.2 million. The economy is diverse (finance, technology, education), and Dublin hosts a large share of Ireland’s data centers—about 70 in total, consuming up to 14% of all metered electricity[^1].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
  - Tallaght is a populous, mixed-use suburb in South Dublin, marked by residential estates, public facilities (hospitals, university), and commercial centers[^5].
  - Historically, Tallaght transitioned from a rural village to a major urban center, now home to a broad socioeconomic mix, including some of Dublin’s lowest-income communities[^5][^1].
  - Land use: Residential (apartments, estates), public buildings (council offices, university), commercial sites.
  - Demographics: Diverse; includes students, working-class families, and a multicultural population.
  - Infrastructure: Aging building stock in parts, with varying energy efficiency; many buildings previously relied on individual gas or oil boilers[^5][^1].
  - Environmental conditions: Cold, wet climate; heating demand is high, especially in winter.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High carbon emissions (a third of Ireland’s emissions from heating)[^10][^1].
  - Energy poverty and high heating costs in low-income districts[^5][^1].
  - Waste heat from data centers vented unused, despite high local heating demand[^5][^10][^1].
  - Technical barriers to retrofitting old buildings for energy efficiency[^1].
- **Triggering Events:**
  - Growing awareness of data centers’ energy footprint and waste heat potential.
  - EU and national climate targets, including Project Ireland 2040 and Climate Action Plan 2023[^3].
  - HeatNet EU funding opportunity for demonstration projects[^2][^3].
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - SDCC (municipal leadership, climate action mandate)
  - Codema (technical expertise, energy mapping)
  - AWS (corporate sustainability commitment)
  - Fortum (energy system operator)
  - Local residents (beneficiaries, energy users)
  - TU Dublin (institutional partner, early adopter)

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**
  - Local government (SDCC) as project lead and utility owner[^3][^4].
  - Collaboration with national agencies (SEAI, Climate Action Fund).
- **Relevant Policies:**
  - Project Ireland 2040 (national development plan)[^3]
  - Climate Action Plan 2023 (targets for district heating and carbon reduction)[^3][^7]
  - EU Interreg NWE HeatNet (support for low-carbon heating networks)[^2][^3]
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
  - Supportive regulatory environment for district heating (new Heat Bill enabling expansion)[^7].
  - Institutional complexity in public-private partnerships and utility management.
  - Need for coordination with building owners, planning authorities, and energy regulators.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Reduce urban carbon emissions by replacing fossil-fuel heating with low-carbon district heating sourced from waste heat[^5][^3][^4].
  - Improve energy efficiency and resilience for Tallaght’s buildings.
  - Demonstrate the viability of district heating in Ireland and catalyze broader adoption.
- **Vision Statement:**
  - “To provide affordable, low-carbon heat to Tallaght’s public, commercial, and residential buildings by harnessing the untapped potential of data center waste heat, setting a new standard for sustainable urban energy systems in Ireland.”[^3][^4]
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**
  - Supports Ireland’s Climate Action Plan and EU climate targets[^3][^7].
  - Advances Project Ireland 2040 objectives for sustainable infrastructure[^3].
  - Model for future urban development strategies in Dublin and other Irish cities[^5][^11].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**
  - Data centers generate large quantities of low-grade heat as a byproduct.
  - District heating networks can capture and redistribute this heat, directly replacing more carbon-intensive heating methods.
  - Smart controls and digital energy management optimize efficiency and reliability, benefiting users and reducing emissions[^5][^10][^2].
- **Target Beneficiaries:**
  - Municipal buildings (SDCC offices, libraries, etc.)
  - Educational institutions (TU Dublin)
  - Residents in affordable housing and apartments
  - Local businesses and future private developments
- **Innovation Elements:**
  - First Irish district heating network using data center waste heat[^3][^4].
  - Integration of smart controls, digital heat-demand mapping, and automated system operation[^5][^8].
  - Publicly-owned, not-for-profit energy utility (HeatWorks)[^4].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political Drivers:** Support for climate action, leadership by SDCC, alignment with national policy[^3][^7].
- **Economic Drivers:** Lower heating costs, local investment attraction, reduced need for expensive retrofitting[^1][^6].
- **Social Drivers:** Addressing energy poverty, community resilience, social equity[^5][^1].
- **Environmental Drivers:** Major CO₂ reductions, protection against energy market volatility, climate adaptation[^5][^3].
- **Technological Drivers:** Availability of data center waste heat, advances in district heating and heat pump technology, smart energy management systems[^5][^10][^2].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Urban sustainability, circular economy, low-carbon infrastructure, and inclusive urban development.
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**
  - Construction of insulated underground pipeline network connecting heat source to buildings[^4].
  - Central energy center (HeatWorks) integrating heat pumps and backup electric boilers[^4].
  - Initial connections: SDCC offices, TU Dublin campus, library, innovation center, affordable apartments[^3][^4].
- **Scope and Phasing:**
  - Phase 1: Public buildings and TU Dublin (2022–2023)[^3].
  - Phase 2: Affordable apartments (2025), further residential/commercial expansion (planned)[^3][^7].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Heat recovery coils** in AWS data center air handling units[^5][^4].
  - Water-based heat transfer: Cold water absorbs server heat, pumped to energy center, condensed via heat pumps to 70–85°C[^5][^10].
  - **Heat pumps** for temperature elevation and efficiency[^5][^10].
  - **Insulated steel-bonded pipework** with leak detection[^4].
  - **Smart digital controls** for monitoring, demand mapping, and system optimization[^5][^8].
  - **Backup 3MW electric boiler** for resilience[^4].
- **Innovation Type:** Technological (heat recovery, smart controls), governance (public utility), process (digital heat mapping, stakeholder coordination).
- **Integration Approach:** Plug-and-play design—existing buildings can connect without major retrofits[^5]; automated operation minimizes labor needs[^8].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Fortum selected for design, construction, operation, and maintenance based on European expertise[^3][^9].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Multi-actor partnership (SDCC, Codema, AWS, residents, businesses, university)[^3][^8].
- **Participatory Elements:** Initial focus on institutional buildings; future expansion to residential sectors with engagement of local communities and developers.
- **Project Management:** SDCC as owner/operator; Codema as technical lead; HeatWorks as energy utility; Fortum as contracted operator[^3][^4].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Not fully disclosed; significant EU and national funding leveraged.
- **Funding Mix:**
  - EU Interreg HeatNet programme
  - Project Ireland 2040 Climate Action Fund
  - SEAI funding
  - SDCC direct investment[^3][^2]
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Heat supplied at competitive rates; cost savings for users; AWS provides waste heat free of charge[^5][^6][^4].
- **Financial Sustainability:** Not-for-profit utility (HeatWorks); backup systems and scalable expansion planned to ensure long-term viability[^4].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical Challenges:**
  - Adjusting low-grade waste heat to usable temperatures (heat pumps required)[^5][^1].
  - Ensuring heat demand density sufficient for system efficiency (>120 TJ/km²)[^5].
  - Infrastructure integration with existing building stock.
- **Financial Challenges:**
  - High upfront investment for pipe network and energy center.
  - Uncertainty in scaling beyond initial phases; long-term funding and revenue streams[^1].
- **Social and Political Challenges:**
  - Need for sustained buy-in from residents, businesses, and institutional partners.
  - Addressing skepticism about data center energy use and environmental impact[^1].
- **Administrative Challenges:**
  - Coordination among multiple actors (local government, private sector, technical agencies).
  - Regulatory compliance and securing planning permissions.
- **Environmental Challenges:**
  - Potential inefficiencies in heat transport (air vs. water as medium)[^1].
  - Ensuring backup systems and fossil-free solutions for resilience[^4].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**
  - Use of digital heat-demand mapping to identify optimal locations for network deployment[^5].
  - Plug-and-play integration to minimize building retrofits[^5].
  - Automated system operation for reliability and efficiency[^8].
- **Design Modifications:**
  - Backup electric boilers added for redundancy[^4].
  - Expansion plans adjusted to accommodate additional buildings as demand grows[^3][^7].
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**
  - Importance of high heat demand density for efficiency.
  - Public ownership and not-for-profit structures can facilitate rapid adoption and community trust[^4].
  - Data center waste heat is a viable renewable source, but technical upgrades (heat pumps, insulation) are essential for performance[^5][^1].

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Major CO₂ reductions** and alignment with national/EU climate targets[^5][^3][^4].
- **Lower heating costs** and improved energy resilience for communities[^5][^1].
- **Public ownership and not-for-profit model** ensure accountability and equity[^4].
- **Scalable design**, with plug-and-play integration enabling cost-effective expansion[^5].
- **Strong multi-actor collaboration** and advanced technical systems (heat pumps, smart controls)[^5][^8].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Technical inefficiencies** in upgrading low-grade waste heat; heat pumps require energy input[^1].
- **Infrastructure investment** is high; scaling beyond initial phases requires sustained funding.
- **Heat demand density** is essential for efficiency—may limit applicability to certain areas[^5].
- **Potential skepticism** about reliance on data centers, given their own high energy consumption.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Focus on affordable housing** and public institutions supports urban equity[^5][^1][^4].
- **No reported negative displacement** or gentrification impacts.
- **Ownership structure** (public utility) prioritizes community benefit.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Trajectory:** Ongoing expansion to more homes and buildings; aims to serve 3,500 homes in 3–5 years[^7].
- **Challenges:** Maintaining financial sustainability, technical upgrades, stakeholder engagement.
- **Next Phases:** Planned integration of other waste heat sources (e.g., Poolbeg incinerator), further scaling of district heating networks across Dublin and Ireland[^7].
