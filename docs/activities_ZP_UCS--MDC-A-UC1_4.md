# Executive summary## Staying Green: Madrid’s Urban Green Space Monitoring

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Staying Green: Madrid’s Urban Green Space Monitoring
- **Alternative Names:** Madrid Urban Green Space Satellite Monitoring; Madrid NDVI/FVC Monitoring Project
- **Location:** Madrid, Community of Madrid, Spain
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 40.4168° N, 3.7038° W (city center)
- **Timeline:**  
  - Project launch: 2009  
  - Key milestone: Deployment of WorldView-2 satellite data  
  - Ongoing: Continual monitoring and updates as of at least 2023
- **Project Scale:**  
  - Area: Entire municipal territory of Madrid (~605 km²)  
  - Population affected: ~3.4 million residents[^1]
- **Leading Organization(s):**  
  - City Council of Madrid  
  - Indra Sistemas S.A. (technology/services provider)  
  - Maxar Technologies (satellite imagery provider, through WorldView Global Alliance)
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - Primarily public funding (City Council of Madrid); in-kind/technical contributions from private partners

#### **Executive Summary**

Madrid’s “Staying Green” initiative represents a pioneering use of high-resolution satellite imagery to monitor and manage urban green spaces. Launched in 2009, the project leverages the advanced multispectral capabilities of the WorldView-2 satellite to generate detailed maps of vegetation health and coverage using indices such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Fractional Vegetation Cover (FVC)[^2][^3][^4]. This digital, data-driven approach enables city officials to proactively manage and expand green infrastructure, mitigate the urban heat island effect, and inform sustainable water and landscape planning. Key outcomes include the creation of a robust, continually updated base map for municipal planners, improved transparency in environmental governance, and the ability to prioritize investments in green infrastructure. The project’s methodology—combining satellite data, geospatial analysis, and visualization tools—serves as a model for cost-effective, scalable urban sustainability strategies. Despite challenges such as ensuring high temporal and spatial resolution and integrating new data streams into existing municipal workflows, the initiative has set a benchmark for smart, resilient urban planning in major European cities[^2][^3][^4].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Madrid is Spain’s capital and largest city, with a 2025 population estimated at 3.4 million. It is a major economic hub, with strengths in finance, government, tourism, and culture. The city’s dense urban core is surrounded by diverse neighborhoods and extensive green infrastructure, including parks and tree-lined streets[^1].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  The project covers the entire city, including historic districts with compact development, mixed-use modern neighborhoods, and peri-urban fringes.  
  - *Historical development:* Madrid’s growth has been characterized by both organic expansion and planned green interventions (e.g., Retiro Park, Casa de Campo).
  - *Land use:* High-density residential, commercial, and institutional zones interspersed with significant green areas.
  - *Demographics:* Socio-economically mixed, with a broad age and cultural profile.
  - *Infrastructure:* Well-developed, but facing pressures from urbanization, climate change, and resource constraints.
  - *Environmental conditions:* Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers; notable urban heat island (UHI) effect; periodic droughts increase stress on vegetation.
  
#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Accelerating urban heat due to UHI effect  
  - Insufficient, unevenly distributed green spaces  
  - Lack of up-to-date, comprehensive data on vegetation health and coverage  
  - Need for improved water management and climate resilience[^2][^4]
- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Growing environmental awareness and policy focus on climate adaptation  
  - Technological advances in remote sensing  
  - Increasing pressure on local government to deliver sustainability outcomes
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - City Council (urban planning, environment, public works)  
  - Technology partners (Indra, Maxar)  
  - Residents and environmental advocacy groups

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Led by municipal government, coordinated through urban planning and environmental departments
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - City’s climate adaptation and sustainability strategies  
  - European Union directives on environmental quality and urban resilience (e.g., Strategic Environmental Assessment - SEA)[^4]
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Supportive EU and national frameworks for environmental data and urban sustainability  
  - Need to integrate new data flows into existing bureaucratic and legal structures

  Building a data-driven, resilient, and livable city through innovative environmental monitoring and management.

  - Integral to Madrid’s sustainability and climate adaptation strategies

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  All city residents, particularly those in areas vulnerable to heat and lacking green space; municipal planners and policymakers
- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Use of *high-resolution, multispectral satellite imagery* (WorldView-2)  
  - Application of NDVI/FVC methodologies for urban monitoring  
  - Integration with municipal planning and water management systems

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

  - Public demand for livability, climate action, and transparent governance  
  - Leadership commitment to innovation
- **Economic Drivers:**  
  - Cost savings through targeted investments and reduced resource waste

- **Social Drivers:**  
  - Equity in green space access

- **Environmental Drivers:**

- **Technological Drivers:**  
  - Advances in satellite imaging and geospatial analysis

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  *Smart city* and *resilient city* paradigms; evidence-based, adaptive urban planning
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - No direct construction, but digital mapping of all green areas and vegetation  
  - Creation of high-resolution geospatial layers for municipal use
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Initial deployment and calibration (2009–2010)  
  - Ongoing data acquisition, map updating, and integration with planning workflows

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - **WorldView-2** satellite: 8-band multispectral, 0.41–2m resolution[^5][^6]  
  - **NDVI/FVC analysis:** Quantitative vegetation indices for health and coverage  
  - **Geospatial mapping and visualization:** GIS platforms for data integration and policymaker access
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (remote sensing, digital mapping)  
  - Governance/process (institutionalizing data-driven management)
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Satellite imagery processed by Indra and Maxar  
  - Output integrated into municipal GIS and planning systems[^2][^3]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Indra selected as lead technology partner; Maxar as imagery provider
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Primary engagement with municipal departments; indirect benefits to public
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Limited direct public participation; transparency via open data and communication
- **Project Management:**  
  - Coordinated by City Council’s planning and environmental divisions with technical partners

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Specific figures not published; cost-effective relative to traditional field surveys[^2][^4]
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Public sector budget (city), supplemented by in-kind/technical contributions from private partners
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - None directly; value derived from improved planning and cost savings
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Ongoing costs for imagery acquisition and analysis; justified by operational efficiencies

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical Challenges:**  
  - Ensuring sufficiently high spatial and temporal resolution for urban applications  
  - Processing and interpreting large volumes of satellite data  
  - Integrating new data streams into existing municipal workflows[^2][^3][^4]
- **Financial Challenges:**  
  - Ongoing costs of high-resolution imagery and technical services
- **Social and Political Challenges:**  
  - Limited stakeholder engagement in technical design
- **Administrative Challenges:**  
  - Need for staff training and capacity building  
  - Coordination between technical partners and city departments
- **Environmental Challenges:**  
  - Cloud cover or atmospheric conditions affecting image quality

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Selection of WorldView-2 for its 8-band, high-resolution, and frequent revisit capabilities[^5][^6]
  - Ongoing methodological refinements for data processing and analysis
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Adjustments to imagery acquisition schedules  
  - Iterative integration with city planning systems
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
  - Importance of high-quality, timely data  
  - Need for cross-departmental collaboration  
  - Value of partnering with specialized technology providers
