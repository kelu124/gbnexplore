# Executive summary

## Living Labs Brussels Retrofit Project

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** Living Labs Brussels Retrofit Project  
- **Alternative Names:** FEDER ‘Living Labs Brussels Retrofit’  
- **Location:** Brussels, Belgium (multiple districts, including Molenbeek, VUB campus, and others)  
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 50.8503° N, 4.3517° E (Brussels city center)  
- **Timeline:** Initiated 2016, main activities 2017–2021; ongoing dissemination and scaling  
- **Project Scale:** Five living labs; over 20 organizations involved; direct impact on multi-family housing stock; EUR 5.44 million total investment  
- **Leading Organization(s):** Retrofit Platform (coordination structure), with participation from industrial, research, and non-profit organizations; notable actors include Bruwatt, Reno-Lab-C, Modul’Air, Prio Climat, and WVDM  
- **Funding Sources:**  
  - European Regional Development Fund (ERDF): EUR 1,826,412  
  - Brussels Capital Region Operational Programme (2014–2020)  
  - Additional public and private partners[^1][^2].

#### **Executive Summary**

The Living Labs Brussels Retrofit Project was launched to spearhead the transformation of the city’s aging multi-family housing stock toward greater energy efficiency and sustainability, in line with Brussels’ pressing climate and social goals. By establishing five “living labs”—real-life testbeds for innovation—across strategically selected neighborhoods, the initiative set out to develop, test, and scale advanced retrofit solutions that blend technological, social, and business model innovation. These labs piloted digital energy monitoring, prefabricated insulated facades, hybrid ventilation systems, and new forms of resident engagement, aiming to accelerate deep renovation rates from a regional average of 0.5% to 3% annually by 2030[^1][^2]. The project’s participatory and multidisciplinary approach mobilized over 20 organizations, including industry, academia, government, and residents, fostering knowledge exchange and co-design. Key outcomes include measurable reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, new business and financing models for collective retrofits, and the creation of green jobs in a city facing high unemployment. The initiative stands as a model for integrated urban retrofit strategies, offering scalable solutions and policy lessons for European cities grappling with decarbonization, energy poverty, and inclusive urban renewal[^1][^2].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Brussels, the capital of Belgium and the European Union, has a population of approximately 1.2 million. The city features a dense urban fabric and is characterized by socio-economic contrasts between central and peripheral districts. Its economy is service-oriented with a significant public sector, but unemployment, especially among youth, remains high (17.6% overall and 31.4% for under-25s as of 2016)[^1]. Geographically, Brussels is a compact region with limited space for new development, making building renovation essential for urban sustainability.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
The living labs were distributed across diverse areas:
- **Molenbeek:** Historically working-class, now ethnically diverse, with significant social housing and lower-income residents.
- **VUB campus (Etterbeek):** Site of post-war student housing, notable for its architectural heritage.
- **Other districts:** Included neighborhoods with large apartment blocks, social housing, and mixed-use urban environments.

Common features include:
- Aging building stock, much of it from the post-war period or earlier.
- High energy consumption and poor insulation standards.
- Residents with limited resources, often facing energy poverty.
- Environmental stressors such as air pollution and limited green space.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**
- High building-related greenhouse gas emissions (nearly 69% of Brussels' total)[^1].
- Low renovation rates (0.5% per year), insufficient to meet climate targets.
- High unemployment and lack of skilled jobs in the construction sector.
- Energy poverty and poor living conditions for vulnerable groups.
- Fragmented market for retrofits, with little innovation or scale.
- Loss of architectural heritage through ad hoc demolitions or poor-quality upgrades.

**Triggering Events:**
- Adoption of Brussels’ regional climate and energy targets.
- Recognition of the need for large-scale, deep energy renovations.
- Availability of European funding to pilot new models.

**Stakeholder Landscape:**
- Regional and municipal governments (policy and funding).
- Construction industry (implementation).
- Research institutions (innovation, monitoring).
- Non-profits and resident groups (co-design, advocacy).
- Social housing companies (building owners, users)[^1][^2].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**
- Brussels Capital Region: overall coordination and policy leadership.
- Retrofit Platform: cross-sectoral coordination structure overseeing the labs.

**Relevant Policies:**
- Brussels’ climate and energy strategies, emphasizing retrofitting as a key lever for emissions reduction.
- EU directives on energy efficiency in buildings.
- Urban planning and heritage protection guidelines.

**Legal/Institutional Enablers and Barriers:**
- Supportive policies for energy retrofits and social housing upgrades.
- Regulatory complexity regarding renovations in heritage buildings.
- Procurement and financing limitations in the fragmented construction sector[^1][^2].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:**
- Dramatically increase the rate and quality of energy-efficient retrofits in Brussels’ housing sector.
- Develop scalable, market-ready solutions and business models.
- Improve living conditions for residents, especially the vulnerable.
- Foster green jobs and skills development.

**Vision Statement:**
To make Brussels a European leader in sustainable urban retrofitting, bridging social, environmental, and economic objectives through collaborative innovation.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:**
- Directly supports city, regional, and EU climate goals.
- Aligns with inclusive urban regeneration and anti-poverty strategies.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:**
If diverse stakeholders co-create, test, and refine innovative renovation solutions in real-life settings, then barriers to large-scale retrofitting (technical, financial, social) can be overcome, resulting in measurable energy savings, job creation, and improved urban equity.

**Target Beneficiaries:**
- Residents of retrofitted multi-family and social housing.
- Local construction workers and SMEs.
- The city at large, through emissions reductions and economic revitalization[^1][^2].

**Innovation Elements:**
- Use of “living labs” for iterative, user-centered innovation.
- Integration of digital monitoring and prefabrication.
- Collective, rather than individual, retrofit business models.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Ambitious regional climate targets; desire for flagship innovation projects.
- **Economic:** Need to stimulate the green construction sector and address high unemployment.
- **Social:** Addressing energy poverty and improving housing quality.
- **Environmental:** Reducing building emissions, conserving resources.
- **Technological:** Advances in digital monitoring, prefabrication, and ventilation[^1][^2].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**
- User-centered innovation (“living lab” methodology).
- Focus on both social and technical aspects of renovation.
- Emphasis on scalability and market creation.

**Physical/Spatial Design:**
- Retrofitting of existing apartment blocks, student housing, and social housing.
- Use of prefabricated facades, hybrid ventilation, and modular interiors.

**Scope and Phasing:**
- Initial selection of five pilot labs, each with a distinct theme (e.g., business models, technical innovation, heritage).
- Four-year implementation, with ongoing monitoring and dissemination[^1][^2].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**
- **Digital monitoring:** Real-time sensors for energy use.
- **Prefabricated insulated facades:** Fast, low-disruption envelope upgrades.
- **Hybrid ventilation systems:** Improved indoor air quality and energy savings.
- **Digital dashboards:** Data-driven maintenance and anomaly detection.

**Innovation Type:**
- Technological (sensors, prefabrication).
- Social (co-design, collective action).
- Financial (new business models for third-party and collective retrofits).
- Process (living lab methodology)[^1][^2].

**Integration Approach:**
Technologies piloted in active residential buildings, emphasizing minimal disruption and compatibility with existing systems.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:**
- Competitive calls for living lab proposals (Test-it instrument).
- Consortium approach: research, industry, and non-profit partners.

**Stakeholder Engagement:**
- Residents involved from the outset (user-centered co-design).
- Workshops, site visits, and feedback loops.

**Participatory Elements:**
- Residents help shape business models and technical solutions.
- Social housing companies as key partners.

**Project Management:**
- Retrofit Platform divided into program, scientific, and industrial impact offices for coordination, monitoring, and dissemination[^1][^2].

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:**
- Total: EUR 5,440,017.
- ERDF: EUR 1,826,412.
- Remainder from regional, public, and private partners.

**Funding Mix:**
- ~34% EU funding; balance from local and sectoral sources.

**Revenue Mechanisms:**
- Emphasis on business models for third-party investment (Bruwatt lab).
- Collective financing strategies for social housing (Reno-Lab-C)[^1].

**Financial Sustainability:**
- Focus on developing replicable financing solutions to unlock future private investment.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Integrating new systems into old buildings; ensuring quality and durability of prefabricated facades; compatibility with heritage requirements.
- **Financial:** High upfront costs of deep retrofits; aligning diverse funding streams.
- **Social and Political:** Engaging residents, especially in vulnerable communities; managing disruption during construction.
- **Administrative:** Coordinating multiple stakeholders; ensuring quality and timely delivery.
- **Environmental:** Addressing unforeseen site-specific issues (e.g., asbestos, moisture)[^1][^2].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- Iterative prototyping and adaptation via living lab methodology.
- Creation of business models to spread upfront costs over time.
- Transparent communication and participatory co-design to build trust with residents.
- Modular construction to minimize disruption and allow for future adaptability.
- Flexibility in technical design to accommodate heritage and site constraints[^1][^2].

---

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- Retrofitted multi-family and social housing units using advanced methods.
- Deployment of real-time energy monitoring and digital dashboards.
- Creation of accessible demonstration sites for the construction sector.

**Quantitative Metrics:**  
- Higher renovation rates in pilot areas (specific numbers vary by lab).
- Measurable reductions in energy use and emissions (building-level data available from scientific office).
- Over 20 organizations mobilized; 5 labs operational[^1][^2].

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Improved comfort, air quality, and affordability for residents.
- **Equity Outcomes:** Targeted inclusion of low-income and socially disadvantaged groups.
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** No evidence of displacement; focus on minimizing negative social externalities.
- **Social Capital:** Strengthened collaboration among residents, housing providers, and sector actors.
- **Cultural Impacts:** Preservation and adaptive reuse of architectural heritage (e.g., VUB student housing)[^3][^4].

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Boost to green construction sector; direct employment in retrofits.
- **Economic Activity:** New business and financing models piloted.
- **Cost Savings:** Lower energy bills for residents; reduced long-term maintenance costs.
- **Economic Inclusion:** Focus on social housing and marginalized communities[^1].

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Significant reduction in building-related emissions in pilot projects.
- **Resource Management:** Improved energy efficiency; use of circular construction methods (especially in VUB labs).
- **Environmental Quality:** Enhanced indoor environment; preservation of green space by avoiding demolition.
- **Waste and Pollution:** Reduction through prefabrication and circular design.
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Alignment with EU low-carbon economy priorities; demonstration of high-performance retrofits[^1][^3][^4].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- Strengthened cross-sectoral partnerships and institutional capacity.
- Inspiration for further policy development and regional scaling.
- Increased transparency and accountability via monitoring and dissemination structures.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- Renovation rates and energy savings in pilot sites exceeded conventional approaches.
- Business models and technical solutions demonstrated scalability.
- Ongoing dissemination and replication in Brussels and beyond.
- Some challenges in scaling finance and overcoming regulatory complexity remain[^1][^2].

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:** Brussels’ policy environment and funding access facilitated innovation; strong social housing sector was an asset.
- **Critical Success Factors:** Multidisciplinary collaboration, user-centered design, public funding, and iterative experimentation.
- **Scalability:** Approaches are being promoted for citywide and regional replication; business models adaptable to other European contexts[^1][^2].

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:** Comparable to Energiesprong (Netherlands), RetrofitWorks (UK), and other EU Horizon 2020 pilots.
- **Best Practices:** Living lab methodology as a best-practice for complex urban innovation.
- **Cautionary Lessons:** Need for early and sustained resident engagement; challenges of aligning regulatory and funding structures.

#### **Parallels to [Your Specific Use Case]**

- **Contextual Similarities:** Urban areas with aging housing stock, energy poverty, and climate targets.
- **Methodological Parallels:** Digital monitoring, resident engagement, and collective business models.
- **Technological Overlaps:** Use of sensors, prefabrication, and data analytics for building management.
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Multisectoral partnerships and co-design.
- **Transferable Strategies:** Iterative piloting, business model innovation, and policy engagement.
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Adjust for local regulatory, market, and social contexts.

---

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Integrated technological, social, and financial innovation.
- Strong participatory and co-design elements.
- Measurable energy, social, and economic impacts.
- Demonstrated scalability and policy alignment.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Complexity and cost of deep retrofits remain barriers at scale.
- Regulatory hurdles, especially in heritage and multi-owner buildings.
- Need for ongoing financial innovation and market development.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Focus on social housing and vulnerable groups promoted urban equity.
- Participatory methods helped avoid displacement and exclusion.
- Strong attention to minimizing negative externalities.

#### **Future Outlook**

- Continued scaling of business models and technical solutions.
- Ongoing monitoring and dissemination to inform policy and practice.
- Next phases may include deeper integration with circular economy and digital twin technologies, and broader regional/national replication.
- Challenges remain in mainstreaming retrofits across diverse building types and ownership structures[^1][^2][^3].
