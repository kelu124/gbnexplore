# Executive summary## Living Lab BIPV - Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Living Lab for Building-Integrated Photovoltaics (BIPV)
- **Alternative Names:** PVcomB Living Laboratory, HZB Testinghalle BIPV Demonstration
- **Location:** Berlin-Adlershof, Southwest Berlin, Germany (at the WISTA science site on Ernst-Ruska-Ufer)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approximately 52.43°N, 13.65°E (Berlin-Adlershof district)
- **Timeline:** Phase 1 operational since 2016 (silver-grey aluminum façade); Phase 2 (BIPV demonstration) commissioned June 2021; ongoing research and monitoring
- **Project Scale:** Two-story research building with floor area of 62 × 16 m (992 m²) and total height of approximately 13 m; solar façade area of approximately 380 m²
- **Leading Organization(s):** Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin für Materialien und Energie (HZB), a research institution under the Helmholtz Association
- **Funding Sources:** German public research funding (Helmholtz Association); specific budget details not publicly disclosed in available sources

#### **Executive Summary**

The Living Lab BIPV represents a comprehensive research and demonstration facility operated by the Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin that showcases the technical, aesthetic, and performance integration of photovoltaic systems into building façades.[^1] Completed in 2021 as the second phase of the HZB's "Testinghalle" research building, the facility features 360 frameless, colored thin-film solar modules (Avancis Skala 7003) installed across the south, west, and north-facing facades, with a total installed capacity of 48.6 kWp and annual energy yield of approximately 30 MWh.[^1] The initiative addresses a critical gap in the built environment sector: the lack of empirical, long-term performance data for building-integrated photovoltaics under real-world conditions. Rather than functioning as a conventional demonstration project, the Living Lab serves dual purposes as both an operational research facility and a practical testing ground for architectural integration of solar technology. The facility incorporates 120 measuring points and sensors throughout the PV façade system, monitoring solar irradiation, electrical output, temperature variations, rear-ventilation dynamics, and comprehensive building physics parameters.[^1] This unprecedented level of instrumentation enables advanced research into how façade-integrated solar systems perform across different seasons and weather conditions. The initiative is significant for the urban sustainability landscape because it addresses a fundamental barrier to BIPV adoption: the disconnect between architectural aspirations for aesthetically integrated renewable energy and the technical and performance uncertainties that constrain widespread implementation. By generating rigorous, publishable scientific data on real-system performance, the Living Lab aims to accelerate the transition from BIPV as a niche experimental technology to a mainstream building design strategy for urban energy transitions.

The city has been undergoing significant transformation since reunification in 1990, with major investments in infrastructure, research institutions, and sustainable urban development. Berlin has established itself as a leader in sustainability and clean energy transitions, with ambitious climate neutrality targets.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The BIPV Living Lab is located in Berlin-Adlershof, a science and technology park in the Köpenick district of southeastern Berlin. Adlershof has undergone dramatic transformation from its industrial past to become one of Europe's leading research and innovation clusters. The district hosts numerous research institutions, technology companies, and educational facilities, creating a knowledge-intensive economy. The WISTA (Wissenschafts- und Wirtschaftsstandort Adlershof) science park where the HZB is located represents planned urban renewal focused on high-value knowledge work rather than residential development. The area combines modern research facilities with remnants of industrial heritage. Infrastructure is well-developed, with good transportation connections. The district has a relatively low residential population density compared to central Berlin, with most activity concentrated in institutional and commercial functions. Environmental conditions include proximity to the Köpenick forests and water bodies, providing recreational amenities. The location at a busy four-lane road (Ernst-Ruska-Ufer) means the facility experiences typical urban traffic and associated noise/air quality conditions.

**Pre-Initiative Conditions:** Before the BIPV Living Lab was established, several contextual factors were relevant. The building sector was experiencing growing pressure to decarbonize and integrate renewable energy, but building-integrated photovoltaics remained largely experimental and niche despite decades of research. Architects and building designers lacked reliable performance data on how BIPV systems would actually perform in practice, creating uncertainty about energy yields, maintenance requirements, and long-term durability. The aesthetic integration of solar technology into building façades remained challenging, with many early PV installations appearing as afterthoughts rather than integral design elements. The research community recognized that BIPV adoption was constrained not primarily by technological feasibility but by lack of empirical validation and design guidance. Additionally, Germany's Energiewende (energy transition) policy created strong political and economic drivers for renewable energy integration, including building-integrated systems. The Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin, as a leading materials science and energy research institution, had both the technical capacity and institutional mission to address these gaps.

**Governance Framework:** The initiative operates within Germany's federal research governance structure, with HZB functioning as a public research institution under the Helmholtz Association, which is funded by the German federal government and state governments. Research priorities and funding are determined through competitive processes and strategic planning by the Helmholtz Association. The facility operates within Berlin's building codes, planning regulations, and sustainability policies.

**Relevant Policies:** Germany's Energiewende (energy transition) policy framework established ambitious renewable energy targets, creating favorable conditions for BIPV research and development. EU directives on building performance and renewable energy integration also shape the regulatory environment. Building regulations and safety standards required careful attention to façade integration, electrical systems, and structural safety, but did not present insurmountable barriers. The primary enabler was the recognition within the German research community and policy environment that BIPV required dedicated research infrastructure to overcome adoption barriers.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:** The Living Lab BIPV was established with several interconnected objectives:[^1][^2]

- To generate comprehensive, long-term empirical performance data on building-integrated photovoltaic systems under real-world conditions across varying seasons and weather patterns
- To validate technical approaches for façade-integrated solar systems, including optimal rear-ventilation configurations and insulation interactions
- To demonstrate the feasibility of aesthetically integrated BIPV that seamlessly merges solar modules with conventional façade elements
- To provide a research platform for investigating the influence of specific design parameters (rear-ventilation area, insulation distance, module orientation) on system performance
- To generate scientific publications and research outputs that inform architects, engineers, and building professionals about BIPV design and performance
- To serve as a visible demonstration facility that raises awareness and reduces psychological barriers to BIPV adoption among building industry stakeholders

**Vision Statement:** The Living Lab embodies a vision of buildings as active energy generators integrated seamlessly into urban environments, where solar technology is not an added component but an integral part of architectural design. This vision emphasizes that the transition to renewable energy in the built environment requires not only technological solutions but also aesthetic integration and empirical validation that builds confidence among practitioners.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:** The initiative aligns with Germany's Energiewende strategy, which targets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and climate neutrality by 2045. It supports the EU's broader building decarbonization agenda, which recognizes that buildings account for approximately 40% of EU energy consumption and 36% of CO₂ emissions. The facility contributes to research priorities within the Helmholtz Association's Energy research program, which focuses on sustainable energy systems. It also supports Berlin's positioning as a leading research and innovation hub for sustainable urban development.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:** The initiative operates on the following causal logic: BIPV adoption has been constrained by several interconnected barriers—lack of empirical performance data, aesthetic integration challenges, and uncertainty about long-term durability and maintenance. By creating a dedicated research facility that generates rigorous scientific data on real-system performance across multiple parameters and seasons, this uncertainty is reduced. When architects and building professionals have access to validated performance data and design guidance, they gain confidence to specify BIPV in new projects. As BIPV projects increase, supply chains improve, costs decline, and market adoption accelerates. Additionally, by demonstrating that BIPV can be aesthetically integrated into high-quality architectural design, the facility challenges perceptions that solar technology is incompatible with architectural excellence. This combination of scientific validation and aesthetic demonstration is intended to shift BIPV from niche experimental status to mainstream building practice.

**Target Beneficiaries:** The primary intended beneficiaries are building professionals (architects, engineers, building developers) who gain access to performance data and design guidance. Secondary beneficiaries include building owners and operators who can make more informed decisions about renewable energy integration. Broader beneficiaries include society generally, through accelerated renewable energy adoption in the building sector, reduced carbon emissions, and progress toward climate targets. The research community benefits from access to unique long-term performance datasets.

**Innovation Elements:** The Living Lab's distinctive innovation lies not in the solar modules themselves (which use commercial Avancis thin-film technology) but in the comprehensive instrumentation and systematic research approach.[^1] The installation of 120 measuring points throughout the façade system, including 10 irradiance sensors at different positions, 72 temperature sensors on module backs, 4 flow sensors for rear-ventilation measurement, and a full weather station, represents an unprecedented level of monitoring for a building-integrated PV system.[^1] This instrumentation density enables investigation of phenomena that cannot be studied through conventional performance monitoring. The systematic variation of rear-ventilation areas and the intentional inclusion of a partially covered north façade for studying diffuse irradiation effects represent deliberate experimental design within an operational building. The seamless architectural integration of solar modules with aluminum façade elements, achieved through concealed suspension systems and careful color matching, represents innovation in façade integration methodology.[^1] The commitment to long-term monitoring and the institutional research framework represent innovation in knowledge generation approaches.

Climate policy priorities at federal and state levels created favorable conditions for renewable energy research. ### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:** The Living Lab design reflects several urban sustainability and research principles. First, it embodies a "living laboratory" concept where research occurs within operational buildings rather than laboratory settings, generating data under real-world conditions. This approach recognizes that building performance in controlled environments may not reflect actual performance. Second, the design reflects integrated systems thinking, recognizing that building performance depends on interactions between solar modules, ventilation systems, insulation, structural elements, and external environmental conditions. Third, the design prioritizes architectural integration, reflecting the principle that sustainable technologies must be aesthetically compatible with high-quality urban design. The facility exemplifies the "research building as instrument" concept, where the building itself functions as a sophisticated measurement apparatus.

**Physical/Spatial Design:** The Living Lab occupies the second phase of the HZB's Testinghalle research building, a two-story structure with dimensions of 62 × 16 m and total height of approximately 13 m.[^1] The building features a distinctive architectural design where the second phase (with BIPV) is visually distinguished from the first phase (completed 2016) through color and material differentiation. The first phase has a silver-grey aluminum façade, while the second phase incorporates a blue-hued façade that represents the Helmholtz Association's corporate color. The blue façade is pulled out approximately 50 mm compared to the silver element, creating visual depth and emphasizing the distinction between phases.[^1]

The 360 thin-film solar modules (Avancis Skala 7003, each 135 W) are distributed across three façade orientations:[^1]
- **South façade:** Primary solar collection area, receiving direct solar radiation throughout the day
- **West façade:** Secondary collection area, receiving afternoon/evening sun
- **North façade:** Partial coverage (58 modules) combined with aluminum façade elements, specifically designed to study diffuse and indirect irradiation effects

The modules are mounted as a ventilated curtain wall system, with a concealed suspension system that enables frameless modules to integrate seamlessly with metal curtain wall elements.[^1] The rear-ventilation area (distance between insulation and solar module) is systematically varied across portions of the south façade to enable investigation of how ventilation affects module temperature and performance.[^1]

**Scope and Phasing:** The project was implemented in two distinct phases. Phase 1 (2016) established the basic research building with conventional aluminum façade, creating the structural and institutional foundation. Phase 2 (2021) added the BIPV demonstration, transforming half the building into a showcase for integrated solar technology. This phasing approach allowed learning from Phase 1 to inform Phase 2 design decisions. The project was conceived with long-term research horizons, with ongoing monitoring and data collection extending years beyond the initial commissioning.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:** The Living Lab integrates several categories of technology:[^1]

- **Photovoltaic Modules:** 360 Avancis Skala 7003 thin-film CIGS (copper indium gallium diselenide) modules, each rated at 135 W, providing 48.6 kWp total installed capacity
- **Façade System:** Ventilated curtain wall mounting system with concealed suspension hardware enabling seamless module integration
- **Electrical Infrastructure:** 38 individual string monitoring circuits enabling granular electrical performance tracking
- **Sensor Network:** Comprehensive instrumentation including:
  - 10 irradiance sensors measuring solar radiation at different façade positions
  - 72 temperature sensors mounted on module backs to track thermal performance
  - 4 flow sensors measuring rear-ventilation air velocity
  - Full weather station measuring ambient temperature, humidity, wind speed/direction, and direct/indirect irradiance
- **Data Acquisition:** Real-time monitoring system collecting data from 120+ measurement points
- **Grid Integration:** Connection to the research campus electrical grid, with energy fed directly into campus consumption

**Innovation Type:** The technological innovations are primarily in the integration and instrumentation rather than in individual components. The innovations span multiple categories:
- **Technological innovation:** Comprehensive sensor network enabling unprecedented monitoring density
- **Design innovation:** Seamless architectural integration of solar modules with conventional façade elements
- **Methodological innovation:** Living laboratory approach to building performance research
- **Governance innovation:** Integration of research infrastructure with operational building management

**Integration Approach:** Technologies are integrated through a systems approach where the building itself functions as a research instrument. The electrical monitoring system captures performance data at multiple levels (individual strings, total system output). The sensor network captures environmental conditions and building physics parameters simultaneously, enabling investigation of causal relationships. Data flows continuously from sensors through acquisition systems to research databases, where it is processed and analyzed. The integration is designed to minimize interference with building operations while maximizing research data quality.

**Project Management:** The project was managed by HZB's research teams, with coordination across architectural, engineering, and research functions. The integration of research requirements with building operations required careful project management to ensure that instrumentation and monitoring systems did not compromise building functionality. Long-term data management and quality assurance processes were established to maintain data integrity over extended monitoring periods.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges:** Several technical challenges emerged during design and implementation:

- **Façade Integration Complexity:** Seamlessly merging frameless PV modules with conventional aluminum façade elements presented significant technical challenges. The concealed suspension system required careful engineering to support module weight while maintaining aesthetic appearance. The color matching between blue PV modules and blue aluminum elements required precision manufacturing and quality control.

- **Sensor Calibration and Reliability:** Installing and maintaining 120+ sensors across a building façade exposed to weather presented calibration and drift challenges. Irradiance sensors required periodic calibration to maintain accuracy. Temperature sensors needed careful mounting to ensure they measured module surface conditions accurately without thermal artifacts from mounting hardware.

- **Data Volume Management:** The comprehensive monitoring system generates substantial continuous data streams from 120+ measurement points. Managing, storing, and processing these data volumes required robust data infrastructure and quality assurance procedures. Data gaps from sensor failures or maintenance required protocols for handling incomplete datasets.

- **Rear-Ventilation Optimization:** Systematically varying rear-ventilation areas and measuring their effects required careful experimental design to isolate ventilation effects from other variables. Temperature variations, wind conditions, and solar angles all influence module performance, requiring sophisticated data analysis to extract ventilation effects.

- **North Façade Diffuse Irradiation:** Studying diffuse and indirect irradiation through partial north façade coverage required careful measurement and analysis, as diffuse irradiation patterns are more complex and variable than direct solar radiation.

**Financial Challenges:** No significant financial challenges are documented in available sources. The public research funding model provided stable financing without budget pressure that might have forced compromises to research objectives.

**Social and Political Challenges:** Limited documentation exists regarding social or political challenges during implementation. The location within a research campus rather than a residential area likely minimized community opposition. The project's alignment with German energy policy and climate objectives suggests strong political support.

**Administrative Challenges:** Coordinating across multiple specialized contractors (architects, façade installers, solar installers, module suppliers) while integrating research requirements into building operations required sophisticated project management. Balancing research instrumentation needs with building functionality and safety requirements presented coordination challenges.

**Environmental Challenges:** The building's exposure to Berlin's continental climate required systems designed to withstand temperature extremes, precipitation, wind loads, and varying solar conditions across seasons. Winter snow loads and summer heat presented different challenges for both the PV system and the monitoring equipment.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:** While specific adaptive responses are not extensively documented, the project's design reflects anticipatory problem-solving:

- The systematic variation of rear-ventilation areas in the south façade design was an experimental approach to understanding and optimizing this parameter
- The intentional partial coverage of the north façade with comparison aluminum elements reflected deliberate experimental design to isolate diffuse irradiation effects
- The comprehensive sensor network was designed to capture data at multiple scales, enabling investigation of phenomena that might not be apparent from system-level monitoring alone

**Design Modifications:** The available sources do not document major design modifications during implementation. The project appears to have proceeded largely according to original plans, suggesting either effective initial planning or successful resolution of challenges without major redesign.

**Lessons Learned During Implementation:** Published research outputs from the project document several important findings that emerged during implementation and monitoring:

- The influence of rear-ventilation configuration on module temperature and performance has been systematically characterized, providing design guidance for future BIPV projects
- The integration of PV modules with conventional façade elements through concealed suspension systems has been validated as technically feasible and aesthetically successful
- Long-term performance data on thin-film CIGS modules in façade applications has been generated, addressing a significant knowledge gap
- The feasibility of comprehensive building-integrated PV monitoring has been demonstrated, establishing methodologies for future research facilities

- **Public Utilities Building, Aachen (Germany, 1991):** An early BIPV demonstration featuring 37 m² of semi-transparent crystalline silicon panels embedded in insulation glass. This facility pioneered façade integration but lacks the sophisticated monitoring of contemporary facilities.

The Berlin Living Lab distinguishes itself through the combination of comprehensive instrumentation, systematic experimental design (varying rear-ventilation parameters), long-term monitoring commitment, and integration of aesthetic and technical research.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

The Living Lab BIPV demonstrates several significant strengths:

**Scientific Rigor:** The comprehensive instrumentation with 120+ measurement points represents an unprecedented level of monitoring for a building-integrated PV system. This enables investigation of phenomena that cannot be studied through conventional performance monitoring, generating unique scientific value. The systematic experimental design—varying rear-ventilation parameters, including a partially covered north façade—demonstrates sophisticated research methodology.

**Long-term Commitment:** The institutional commitment to multi-decade monitoring, secured through public research funding, enables investigation of long-term performance trends, seasonal patterns, and system durability that shorter-term projects cannot address. This long-term perspective generates knowledge of substantial practical value.

**Aesthetic Integration:** The facility demonstrates that solar technology can be seamlessly integrated into contemporary high-quality architecture, with frameless colored modules and concealed suspension systems achieving visual coherence. This addresses a critical barrier to BIPV adoption—the perception that solar technology compromises architectural quality.

**Knowledge Dissemination:** Publication of research findings in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at professional conferences ensures that knowledge generated by the facility reaches relevant professional communities. This amplifies the facility's impact beyond the research institution.

**Operational Integration:** The facility successfully integrates research instrumentation with building operations, demonstrating that sophisticated monitoring need not compromise building functionality. This provides a model for other research facilities.

**Institutional Support:** The facility benefits from stable institutional funding through the Helmholtz Association, enabling research priorities to guide decisions rather than commercial or short-term financial pressures.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

Despite its strengths, the Living Lab has several limitations:

**Limited Scope:** The facility focuses specifically on thin-film CIGS module technology in a particular climate context (Berlin continental climate). Findings may not directly transfer to other module technologies (crystalline silicon, perovskite) or different climates (Mediterranean, tropical, arctic). The facility's single-building focus limits understanding of how BIPV performs across diverse building types and contexts.

**Research-Focused Mission:** While the facility serves demonstration functions, its primary mission as a research facility means it operates under conditions that may not represent typical building practice. Optimal research conditions (comprehensive monitoring, careful experimental design) may not reflect how BIPV performs in standard commercial or residential buildings with conventional monitoring.

**Limited Community Engagement:** The facility's location within a research campus and its research-focused mission limit community engagement compared to GBNs that involve residential populations and local stakeholders. The facility provides knowledge to professional communities but limited direct benefits to broader urban populations.

**Aesthetic Limitations:** While the facility demonstrates successful aesthetic integration, the specific design approach (blue-colored modules, research campus context) may not be universally preferred or appropriate. Different architectural contexts and aesthetic preferences might require different design solutions.

**Economic Barriers Not Addressed:** The facility generates knowledge about technical and aesthetic feasibility but does not directly address economic barriers to BIPV adoption. The cost of comprehensive monitoring and high-quality architectural integration remains substantial, potentially limiting adoption even among professionals with technical knowledge.

**Limited Investigation of Social Factors:** While the facility addresses technical and aesthetic dimensions of BIPV, it generates limited insight into social and psychological factors that influence adoption decisions among building professionals and owners. Understanding these factors is essential for accelerating BIPV adoption.

**Data Accessibility:** While research findings are published, access to raw monitoring data appears limited to the research team and collaborators. Broader data accessibility could accelerate research by external groups and generate additional insights.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

The Living Lab raises several equity and justice considerations:

**Knowledge Access:** The facility generates knowledge primarily for professional communities with technical capacity to understand and apply sophisticated research findings. Building professionals in well-resourced organizations can access and benefit from this knowledge, while practitioners in under-resourced contexts may lack capacity to engage with complex technical information. This could reinforce existing inequalities in access to advanced building technologies.

**Technology Access:** BIPV technology remains relatively expensive, and the high-quality aesthetic integration demonstrated by the facility requires substantial investment. This may limit adoption primarily to well-resourced building projects, potentially concentrating renewable energy benefits among affluent populations. Lower-income housing and buildings serving disadvantaged communities may not benefit equally from BIPV technology advancement.

**Research Priorities:** The facility's research priorities reflect interests of research institutions and professional communities, which may not align with priorities of lower-income populations or marginalized communities. Research on BIPV for luxury buildings may be prioritized over research on affordable housing renewable energy integration.

**Environmental Justice:** To the extent that BIPV adoption accelerates due to knowledge from facilities like this, broader environmental benefits (reduced emissions, improved air quality) are distributed across society, including disadvantaged populations. However, concentrated deployment in affluent areas could create unequal distribution of local environmental benefits.

**Procedural Justice:** The facility's location within a research campus and its research-focused mission limit opportunities for affected communities to participate in decisions about research priorities and implementation. Community input on research questions and knowledge generation is minimal.

**Recognition Justice:** Building professionals and researchers receive recognition for BIPV advancement, while broader populations who benefit from resulting emission reductions receive no recognition. This asymmetry reflects broader patterns in how technical expertise and innovation are valued relative to community participation.

To advance equity, future BIPV research facilities might prioritize: research on affordable housing applications, mechanisms to reduce costs and improve accessibility, community engagement in research priority-setting, and explicit attention to distributional equity of benefits.

#### **Future Outlook**

**Long-term Trajectory:** The Living Lab BIPV appears positioned for sustained operation and continued research contributions. The institutional commitment through the Helmholtz Association provides financial stability. As the facility accumulates longer monitoring records, research value increases, enabling investigation of multi-year and decadal patterns. The facility is likely to remain operational for decades, generating increasingly valuable long-term datasets.

**Ongoing Challenges:** Several challenges will likely persist:

- **Technology Evolution:** As solar module technologies evolve (perovskite cells, advanced crystalline silicon), the facility's focus on thin-film CIGS modules may become dated. Decisions about whether to upgrade module technology or maintain current systems for long-term comparison will require careful consideration.

- **Data Management:** As monitoring periods extend and data volumes accumulate, data management and quality assurance become increasingly challenging. Ensuring data integrity and accessibility over decades requires sustained technical infrastructure and expertise.

- **Relevance Maintenance:** As building practice evolves and new technologies emerge, maintaining the facility's relevance to contemporary practice requires periodic updating of research questions and monitoring approaches.

**Opportunities and Next Phases:**

- **Network Development:** Expanding the Living Lab concept to multiple facilities across different climates and building types would enable comparative analysis and broader knowledge generation. A network of BIPV research facilities could address regional variations in climate, architecture, and building practice.

- **Technology Integration:** Investigating integration of BIPV with other building technologies (thermal storage, heat pumps, battery systems) would address emerging interests in integrated energy systems.

- **Retrofit Applications:** Extending research to façade retrofits of existing buildings would address the substantial opportunity in building stock renovation.

- **Economic Analysis:** Complementing technical research with economic analysis of BIPV lifecycle costs, financing mechanisms, and market development would address adoption barriers beyond technical feasibility.

- **Social Research:** Integrating social science research on adoption barriers, professional decision-making, and community acceptance would provide more complete understanding of factors influencing BIPV deployment.

- **Open Data Initiatives:** Increasing public access to monitoring data and research outputs would accelerate external research and innovation.

- **Professional Training:** Expanding the facility's role in professional education and training would increase its impact on building practice.

The Living Lab BIPV represents a significant contribution to advancing building-integrated photovoltaics from experimental technology toward mainstream practice. Its comprehensive instrumentation, long-term commitment, successful aesthetic integration, and active knowledge dissemination position it as a leading facility for BIPV research globally. While limitations exist regarding scope, community engagement, and equity considerations, the facility's strengths in scientific rigor and knowledge generation make it a valuable resource for the building sector's energy transition. Future evolution toward networked facilities, integrated technology investigation, and broader stakeholder engagement could amplify the facility's impact on urban sustainability.
