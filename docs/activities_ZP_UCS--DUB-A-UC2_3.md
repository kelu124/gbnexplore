# Executive summary## Mapping Green Dublin

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Mapping Green Dublin: Strategic Pathways to Community-Led Greening
- **Alternative Names:** Mapping Green Dublin (MGD)
- **Location:** Dublin 8 (South-West Inner City), Dublin City Council area, Ireland
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Centered on Dublin 8 neighborhood; exact boundaries defined by census boundaries and geographic remit of project partners
- **Timeline:** 2019–2021 (project duration); ongoing community engagement through D8 Green Bridge Forum
- **Project Scale:** Neighborhood-scale intervention covering Dublin 8; initial citywide tree survey of entire Dublin City Council area
- **Leading Organization(s):** University College Dublin's School of Geography (lead); Common Ground (arts organization); artist Seoidín O'Sullivan; Connect the Dots (social enterprise event facilitators); Dublin City Council; local community organizations
- **Funding Sources:** Irish Environmental Protection Agency (primary funder); subsequent funding from businesses in the area for D8 Green Bridge Forum; Irish Research Council (IRC) funding for follow-up Urban Grit initiative

#### **Executive Summary**

Mapping Green Dublin is a collaborative action research project that pioneered a community-led approach to urban greening strategy development in an inner-city Dublin neighborhood. Conducted between 2019 and 2021 with funding from the Irish Environmental Protection Agency, the project combined rigorous scientific mapping of trees and green infrastructure with intensive community engagement to co-create a neighborhood greening strategy. The initiative addressed a critical gap in urban environmental equity: the uneven distribution of tree canopy and green space across Dublin, with particular deficiencies in the densely populated, historically disadvantaged Dublin 8 area. Rather than imposing top-down greening solutions, the project employed a transdisciplinary methodology integrating geography, art, and community development to ensure that interventions responded to local needs, possessed high social and cultural value, and maximized opportunities for community wellbeing. The project generated a comprehensive urban tree inventory, identified specific greening intervention sites, established the permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum for ongoing community advocacy, and produced a replicable community-led greening strategy framework that has influenced Dublin City Council's broader green infrastructure policies. Its significance lies not merely in the physical greening outcomes but in demonstrating how evidence-based planning can be democratized through genuine community participation, empowering residents to become active participants in designing their own urban environments rather than passive recipients of planning decisions.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile**

Dublin, Ireland's capital and largest city, is experiencing rapid urban transformation driven by technology sector growth, significant housing demand, and regeneration pressures. The city functions as the national economic hub with a population of approximately 1.2 million in the metropolitan area. Dublin's development is characterized by competing demands for limited urban space, with available plots increasingly converted into high-rise apartment blocks that often feature limited private open space. The city's inner-city areas, in particular, face intense development pressure as the technology sector attracts investment and population growth.[^2]

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics: Dublin 8**

Dublin 8 is located in the south-west inner city and represents a microcosm of Dublin's broader urban challenges and opportunities. The area has undergone significant demographic and built-form transitions. Historically, Dublin 8 was characterized by working-class communities with strong social networks and cultural identity. The neighborhood exhibits several defining characteristics:

- **Historical Development:** The area contains significant cultural and historical heritage, including de-consecrated churches, archaeological sites, and historic streetscapes. It has experienced cycles of disinvestment and recent regeneration pressure.
- **Current Land Use:** Mixed residential, commercial, and institutional uses. The area includes local authority housing complexes (St. Teresa's Gardens, Oliver Bond, Dolphin House, St. Michael's on Emmet Road), the National Children's Hospital construction site (12 acres), the Players Wills redevelopment site, and scattered vacant plots resulting from recent demolitions.
- **Demographic Profile:** The area experiences persistent inter-generational social problems, with visible concerns about the neighborhood's appearance and a sense of being left behind in terms of social and environmental infrastructure.[^5] The population includes vulnerable groups including residents of local authority housing, families with limited economic resources, and long-term residents concerned about gentrification pressures.
- **Environmental Conditions:** Dublin 8 exhibits significant green space deficiencies, with uneven distribution of trees and limited accessible green infrastructure. The area experiences inadequate provision of public realm quality and green space compared to other Dublin neighborhoods.[^6]

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified**

The Mapping Green Dublin project was designed to address several interconnected urban challenges:

1. **Environmental Inequity:** A citywide survey revealed highly variable green cover across Dublin, with significant disparities in tree canopy distribution and green space access. Inner-city neighborhoods like Dublin 8 experienced substantially lower provision of green infrastructure compared to more affluent areas.[^2]

2. **Green Infrastructure Deficiency:** Dublin 8 suffered from inadequate green space provision and public realm quality. The dense urban fabric, combined with competing development pressures and limited available land, created significant challenges for green infrastructure enhancement.[^2][^6]

3. **Disconnection Between Planning and Community Needs:** Traditional top-down planning approaches often failed to align greening interventions with actual community priorities, resulting in underutilized or culturally inappropriate green spaces. Communities lacked meaningful participation in decisions affecting their neighborhoods.[^2][^3]

4. **Loss of Accessible Green Spaces:** Demolition of buildings to make way for redevelopment had left vacant sites; while some temporary uses (allotments) emerged through grassroots movements, there was no systematic strategy for converting these into permanent, high-quality green infrastructure.[^6]

5. **Broader Urban Resilience Concerns:** As a dense inner-city area experiencing development pressure and demographic change, Dublin 8 faced challenges related to climate resilience, public health, biodiversity, and quality of life that green infrastructure could help address.[^1]

**Triggering Events**

The initiative emerged from converging factors: growing awareness of climate change and urban resilience needs, recognition of environmental health disparities in inner-city areas, grassroots community campaigns for parks and green spaces (exemplified by successful temporary allotment projects), and Dublin City Council's policy commitment to enhance green infrastructure. A 2019 Department of Housing, Planning and Local Government report highlighted local concerns about the area's visible appearance, lack of greenspace, and sense of being left behind, providing additional impetus for action.[^5]

**Stakeholder Landscape**

Before the initiative, the stakeholder landscape included: Dublin City Council (local government authority responsible for planning and green infrastructure); local residents and community organizations with deep knowledge of neighborhood needs; local sports clubs and community development organizations; arts organizations interested in creative place-making; academic researchers at UCD; and developers and private interests focused on site redevelopment. Community stakeholders had demonstrated capacity for grassroots action (temporary allotments) but lacked formal mechanisms to influence city planning decisions at scale.[^4]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework**

Dublin operates under Ireland's planning and local government system, with Dublin City Council holding primary authority over planning decisions, parks and landscape services, and local development plans. The project operated within Dublin City Council's Parks and Landscape Services Division, working in collaboration with the South Central Area Office.[^6] The governance structure involved multiple layers: citywide policy frameworks, area-based strategies, and neighborhood-level implementation.

**Relevant Policies**

- **Dublin City Development Plan (2011-2017 and subsequent iterations):** Provided the overarching planning framework, though the project identified divergences between greening strategy objectives and the Local Area Plan for Dublin 8 (particularly regarding proposed park sites).[^6]
- **Dublin City Council Green Infrastructure Policy:** The council had committed to enhancing green infrastructure citywide, but implementation required evidence-based prioritization and community support.[^2]
- **Liberties Local Area Plan:** Specific area planning document for the Dublin 8 study area that required statutory amendment to align with community-led greening proposals.[^6]
- **Environmental Protection Agency Research Funding:** The EPA's research funding program enabled collaborative, community-engaged environmental research, providing the financial and institutional support necessary for the project.[^1]

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers**

Enablers included: Dublin City Council's stated policy commitment to green infrastructure enhancement; the EPA's willingness to fund innovative, community-engaged research approaches; academic institutions' capacity to conduct rigorous research while maintaining community partnerships; and community organizations' existing relationships and credibility. Barriers included: the complexity of decision-making when multiple stakeholders held interests in particular sites; the challenge of securing land for green space development when property ownership was fragmented or sites were earmarked for commercial development; the need to amend formal planning documents to align with community recommendations; and limited funding for implementation of identified greening interventions.[^2][^6]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals**

Mapping Green Dublin pursued multiple interconnected objectives:

1. **Scientific Documentation:** To conduct a comprehensive inventory of Dublin's urban trees and green spaces, identifying their location, quality, environmental value, and distribution patterns across the city.[^1][^2]

2. **Equity Assessment:** To identify places with significant environmental deficiencies by comparing tree cover maps with population and traffic information, thereby revealing environmental inequities.[^2]

3. **Community-Led Strategy Development:** To develop a neighborhood greening strategy for Dublin 8 that was grounded in community knowledge, priorities, and aspirations rather than expert-driven planning alone.[^1][^3]

4. **Empowerment and Capacity Building:** To empower residents and community organizations with evidence-based knowledge and new technical skills (GIS mapping, data interpretation) so they could participate on more equal terms with policymakers and planning professionals.[^4]

5. **Policy Influence:** To generate evidence and demonstrate processes that could inform Dublin City Council's broader green infrastructure policies and contribute to the city's next development plan.[^2]

**Vision Statement**

The project articulated a vision of inclusive, socially and environmentally just urban greening grounded in community collaboration. As stated in the project documentation, the team aimed to "empower local communities to action greening projects in their own neighbourhoods, inform more socially just urban greening policy and practice, and demonstrate how community-based strategy-making can make a significant contribution to addressing climate action, liveability and wellbeing objectives."[^5]

**Alignment with Broader Plans**

The project aligned with several broader strategic frameworks: Dublin City Council's commitment to green infrastructure enhancement; Ireland's climate action objectives and commitment to addressing environmental equity; the European Union's urban sustainability and green cities agenda; and the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals related to sustainable cities, climate action, and reduced inequality.[^1][^2]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change**

The project's logic model operated as follows: Scientific mapping would reveal patterns of environmental inequality and green space deficiency → Community engagement processes would surface local knowledge about how residents actually used and valued green spaces and what their specific needs were → Collaborative workshops and co-creation activities would translate this combined evidence base (scientific + community) into concrete, locally-grounded greening proposals → Empowered community members and organizations would advocate for implementation of these proposals → Dublin City Council would incorporate community-endorsed proposals into formal planning documents and development plans → Implemented greening interventions would deliver environmental, health, social, and wellbeing benefits while maintaining community ownership and cultural appropriateness.

The theory recognized that sustainable greening outcomes required alignment between scientific evidence, community priorities, and formal planning authority—none of these alone would be sufficient.[^3][^5]

**Target Beneficiaries**

Primary beneficiaries were Dublin 8 residents, particularly those in local authority housing and economically disadvantaged households who experienced the greatest green space deficiency. Secondary beneficiaries included: community organizations and local development agencies gaining new partnerships and influence; Dublin City Council gaining community-endorsed, evidence-based greening priorities; and the broader Dublin population who would benefit from improved urban environmental quality and replicable models for community-engaged planning.[^4]

**Innovation Elements**

The project's innovation lay not in individual components (community engagement, GIS mapping, or environmental assessment are established practices) but in their transdisciplinary integration and genuine co-creation approach:

- **Methodological Innovation:** Combining rigorous scientific mapping with artistic practice and community knowledge systems to create new forms of environmental understanding[^1]
- **Governance Innovation:** Establishing genuine power-sharing between academic researchers, community members, and planning authorities rather than token consultation[^3][^4]
- **Process Innovation:** Using interactive mapping, web-based discussions, and urban prototyping workshops to make technical data accessible and creatively engage diverse community members[^2]
- **Institutional Innovation:** Creating the permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum as an ongoing mechanism for community-led advocacy, embedded within the formal governance structure[^4]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers**

Dublin City Council's stated policy commitment to green infrastructure enhancement created political space for the initiative. The project provided an opportunity for the council to demonstrate commitment to environmental sustainability and community engagement—priorities increasingly important to urban voters and aligned with national climate policy objectives.[^2]

**Economic Drivers**

While not primarily economically motivated, the project recognized that green infrastructure investments could attract inward investment in high-quality work space, learning facilities, and evening economy and tourist accommodation—economic benefits that might support the neighborhood's regeneration while maintaining community character.[^6] The project also demonstrated that community-engaged planning could reduce costly conflicts and delays by building early consensus around greening priorities.

**Social Drivers**

Grassroots community campaigns for parks and green spaces (exemplified by the successful temporary allotments projects) demonstrated strong local demand for green infrastructure. The project responded to community advocacy while also creating more formal, structured mechanisms for community participation in planning decisions. Concerns about displacement and gentrification pressures motivated a commitment to ensuring that greening benefited existing residents rather than pricing them out through property value increases.[^4][^5]

**Environmental Drivers**

Recognition of climate change, urban heat island effects, air quality problems, and biodiversity loss in dense urban areas created environmental imperatives for greening. The project positioned green infrastructure as essential to urban resilience, public health, and environmental justice.[^2][^3] The EPA's research funding reflected national environmental priorities.

**Technological Drivers**

Advances in GIS mapping technology, digital visualization tools, and web-based platforms enabled new forms of environmental data collection and community engagement. The project leveraged these tools to make technical data accessible and to facilitate participatory planning processes.[^2][^3]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework**

The project drew on several planning and development philosophies:

- **Environmental Justice:** Recognition that environmental benefits and burdens are inequitably distributed and that planning must explicitly address these disparities[^5]
- **Community-Based Participatory Research:** Academic-community partnerships where research questions, methods, and interpretation are shaped collaboratively[^3]
- **Co-creation and Co-design:** Moving beyond consultation to genuine shared power in decision-making[^1][^3]
- **Place-Based Approaches:** Recognition that effective development strategies must be grounded in specific local contexts, histories, and community aspirations rather than generic templates[^5]
- **Transdisciplinarity:** Integration of geography, art, community development, and planning to create richer understanding than any single discipline could provide[^1]
- **Urban Resilience:** Green infrastructure as essential to climate adaptation, public health, and community wellbeing in dense urban areas[^2]

**Physical/Spatial Design**

The project did not impose a predetermined physical design but rather developed specific design recommendations through collaborative processes. However, the final strategy identified several types of spatial interventions:

- **New Urban Parks:** Proposed creation of neighborhood parks on vacant sites (Weaver Park, Bridgefoot Street Park) to serve local residents[^6]
- **Enhanced Existing Green Spaces:** Improvements to six existing green spaces (Pimlico, Oisín Kelly Park, Poole Street, Vicar Street, Oscar Square, Park Terrace) including tree planting, accessibility improvements, and community stewardship opportunities[^6]
- **Green Streets:** Introduction of tree pits along streets, replacement of hard standing with soft landscape, development of rain gardens for stormwater management[^6]
- **Accessible Heritage Green Spaces:** Making churchyards and archaeological sites accessible as urban green spaces (St. James Church, St. Luke's)[^6]
- **Productive Green Spaces:** Community gardens and allotments for food production (Thomas Abbey site)[^6]
- **Green Mobility Infrastructure:** Off-road cycle tracks linking the Liberties to proposed Grand Canal Greenway, integration with Dublin Bikes expansion[^6]
- **Intergenerational Spaces:** Design of green spaces accommodating diverse age groups and community uses[^5]

**Scope and Phasing**

The project was structured in three distinct phases:

- **Phase 1 (2019-early 2020): Data Mapping and Assessment** – Scientific inventory of trees and green spaces across Dublin City, with detailed focus on Dublin 8; identification of green space deficits and environmental inequities[^1][^3]
- **Phase 2 (March-August 2020): Community Mapping and Co-Creation** – Series of curated events, focus groups, community launch events, urban prototyping workshops, and web-based discussions to surface community knowledge and co-develop greening proposals[^1][^3][^5]
- **Phase 3 (2020-2021): Action and Strategy Development** – Synthesis of scientific and community data; identification of specific greening intervention sites; development of community greening strategy; engagement with policy and practitioner stakeholders; formulation of action points for implementation[^5]

The phasing reflected an intentional progression from evidence gathering to community dialogue to actionable strategy.[^1][^3]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed**

- **Geographic Information Systems (GIS) Mapping:** Comprehensive spatial analysis of tree canopy, green space distribution, and environmental quality across Dublin; creation of maps comparing tree cover with population and traffic data to identify environmental deficiencies[^2][^3]
- **Community Surveys and Focus Groups:** Structured data collection on how residents used and valued green spaces, their concerns, and their aspirations[^1][^3]
- **Interactive Mapping Workshops:** Community members engaged with scientific data through participatory mapping exercises, making technical information accessible and enabling residents to annotate maps with local knowledge[^2][^5]
- **Web-Based Discussion Platforms:** Digital tools enabling asynchronous community participation, particularly important during COVID-19 pandemic restrictions[^3][^5]
- **Urban Prototyping:** Physical interventions and temporary installations in public space to help communities envision proposed greening changes and provide feedback[^5]
- **Digital Visualization Tools:** Three-dimensional renderings and visual simulations helping community members understand proposed changes[^2]
- **Artistic Practice and Creative Facilitation:** Collaboration with artist Seoidín O'Sullivan to reconfigure perspectives on greening, ecology, and local microgeographies; use of creative methods to engage diverse community members beyond traditional planning consultation formats[^1][^4]

**Innovation Type**

These technologies represent a combination of innovation types:

- **Technological Innovation:** Application of GIS and digital visualization to make environmental data more accessible
- **Social Innovation:** Use of arts-based and creative facilitation methods to engage communities in planning
- **Governance Innovation:** Integration of community knowledge systems with scientific expertise in formal planning processes
- **Process Innovation:** Development of structured methodologies for genuine co-creation that moved beyond tokenistic consultation[^3][^4]

**Integration Approach**

Technologies were integrated to create complementary forms of knowledge and engagement. Scientific GIS mapping provided systematic, comparable data across the city; community surveys and focus groups surfaced qualitative understanding of how people actually experienced green space; interactive workshops made technical data accessible while enabling residents to contribute local knowledge; artistic practice created emotional and imaginative engagement with possibilities for change; and digital platforms enabled participation across diverse schedules and circumstances.[^2][^3][^5]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting**

The project operated through institutional partnerships rather than traditional procurement. UCD's School of Geography held the research contract with the EPA. Implementation involved formal partnerships with Common Ground (arts organization), artist Seoidín O'Sullivan, and Connect the Dots (social enterprise), selected for their existing relationships with communities and complementary expertise. Dublin City Council participated as a key stakeholder and policy partner rather than as a contractor. Local community organizations and residents participated as co-researchers and co-creators rather than as hired service providers, reflecting a commitment to genuine partnership.[^1][^4]

**Stakeholder Engagement**

The project employed multiple engagement mechanisms:

- **Focus Groups:** Initial focus groups mapped trees and greenspaces and identified community priorities[^4]
- **Community Launch Event:** Public event introducing the project and inviting participation[^1][^4]
- **Curated Events Series:** March-August 2020 events mapping different themes and concerns around trees and greenspaces[^1][^3]
- **Urban Prototyping Workshop:** Hands-on workshop enabling communities to experiment with and provide feedback on proposed greening interventions[^5]
- **Neighborhood Greening Forum:** Ongoing mechanism for community dialogue and advocacy (later formalized as D8 Green Bridge Forum)[^4]
- **Workshops and Interactive Mapping:** Multiple sessions enabling residents to work with scientific data and contribute local knowledge[^2][^5]
- **Web-Based Discussions:** Digital platforms for participation[^3]

**Participatory Elements**

The project's participatory approach was distinguished by genuine power-sharing:

- **Co-Definition of Problems:** Community members contributed to identifying what constituted "green space deficiency" and what mattered about greening beyond technical environmental metrics
- **Co-Creation of Solutions:** Residents participated in designing specific interventions, not merely responding to expert proposals
- **Shared Data Interpretation:** Community members worked with researchers to interpret scientific findings in light of lived experience
- **Collective Decision-Making:** Community-endorsed priorities shaped the final strategy recommendations
- **Ongoing Governance:** The permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum ensured community voice in implementation and adaptation[^4]

This represented a significant departure from typical consultation models where experts develop proposals and communities provide feedback.[^3][^4]

**Project Management**

The project employed collaborative project management structures involving:

- **Research Team:** UCD researchers (Gerald Mills, Niamh Moore-Cherry, Alma Clavin) providing scientific expertise and research coordination
- **Arts Organization Partnership:** Common Ground and artist Seoidín O'Sullivan providing creative facilitation and community engagement
- **Social Enterprise Coordination:** Connect the Dots facilitating events and community participation
- **Community Leadership:** Local residents and community organizations as active decision-makers, not merely participants
- **Institutional Liaison:** Dublin City Council participation ensuring policy relevance and potential for implementation

Regular coordination meetings, transparent communication, and documented decision-making processes maintained coherence across the diverse partnership.[^1][^4]

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs**

Specific total project budget figures are not detailed in available sources, but the project was funded through a research grant from the Irish Environmental Protection Agency. The budget covered research team salaries, community engagement events, technology and mapping resources, artistic collaboration, and publication of findings.

**Funding Mix**

- **Primary Funder:** Irish Environmental Protection Agency (research grant)
- **In-Kind Contributions:** UCD institutional resources; Common Ground and Connect the Dots organizational capacity; volunteer community participation
- **Follow-On Funding:** Business contributions to D8 Green Bridge Forum for ongoing community advocacy; Irish Research Council funding for Urban Grit follow-up project[^4]

**Revenue Mechanisms**

The project did not generate revenue but rather operated on a grant-funded research model. However, the D8 Green Bridge Forum (emerging from the project) secured ongoing funding through local business contributions, demonstrating a model for sustaining community-led initiatives through local economic engagement.[^4]

**Financial Sustainability**

The project's financial sustainability operated at multiple levels:

- **Research Phase:** Funded through EPA grant with defined timeline (2019-2021)
- **Community Forum:** Sustained through business contributions and demonstrated community value
- **Implementation:** Dependent on Dublin City Council's capital budget for parks and green infrastructure, with the project's evidence base and community endorsement strengthening the case for investment
- **Replication:** The project developed resources and methodologies that other neighborhoods and cities could adapt without requiring equivalent financial investment[^4][^5]

The model demonstrated that community-engaged planning could be financially sustainable through combinations of public research funding, local institutional resources, and demonstrated value to government and business stakeholders.[^4]

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges**

- **Data Accessibility:** Making complex GIS data and environmental science comprehensible to diverse community members without technical training required significant methodological innovation. The team addressed this through interactive mapping, visual simulations, and collaborative interpretation sessions where residents and researchers worked together to understand what the data meant for their neighborhood.[^2][^3]

- **Pandemic Disruption:** The project encountered significant disruption when COVID-19 lockdowns occurred during Phase 2 (March-August 2020), precisely when in-person community engagement was planned. The team adapted by developing web-based discussion platforms and shifting to digital engagement tools, though this necessarily altered the nature of community participation and excluded those without reliable internet access or digital literacy.[^3][^5]

- **Site Complexity:** Many proposed greening sites involved complex ownership arrangements, existing uses, or competing claims. For example, some vacant sites were owned by private developers, local authority housing departments, or had uncertain tenure, complicating the path from community recommendation to implementation.[^6]

**Financial Challenges**

- **Implementation Funding Gap:** While the project successfully identified priority greening interventions, securing funding for actual implementation proved challenging. The research funding supported strategy development but not capital investment in parks, tree planting, or green infrastructure.[^2][^4]

- **Long-Term Maintenance Costs:** Proposed green spaces would require ongoing maintenance, and questions about responsibility and funding for long-term stewardship needed resolution.[^6]

**Social and Political Challenges**

- **Engaging Diverse Communities:** Dublin 8 contains diverse populations (long-term residents, recent arrivals, local authority tenants, private residents, business owners, institutions) with potentially different priorities. The project worked to ensure that engagement processes reached beyond the "usual suspects" and included marginalized voices, though this remained an ongoing challenge.[^3][^4][^5]

- **Gentrification Concerns:** Community members expressed anxiety that improved green spaces and neighborhood amenities could attract investment and increase property values, potentially pricing out existing residents. The project acknowledged these concerns and incorporated equity considerations into strategy development, but could not resolve the fundamental tension between neighborhood improvement and displacement risk.[^4][^5]

- **Political Alignment:** The project needed to maintain alignment with Dublin City Council's priorities while maintaining independence and community credibility. In some cases, proposed greening interventions diverged from the adopted Local Area Plan (particularly regarding Weaver Park and Bridgefoot Street Park sites), requiring Dublin City Council to initiate statutory planning amendments.[^6] This process was lengthy and created uncertainty about implementation.

- **Community Skepticism:** Some residents initially approached the project with skepticism, having experienced previous planning processes that failed to deliver promised improvements. Building trust required sustained engagement and demonstrated commitment.[^4]

**Administrative Challenges**

- **Coordination Complexity:** Coordinating across multiple organizations (UCD, Common Ground, Connect the Dots, Dublin City Council, community groups), each with different cultures, timelines, and priorities, required significant effort.[^1][^4]

- **Bureaucratic Timelines:** The statutory processes required to amend planning documents and secure approval for site development operated on longer timelines than the research project, creating misalignment between when recommendations were ready and when they could be formally adopted.[^6]

- **Capacity Constraints:** Community organizations and residents had limited capacity for intensive engagement alongside their other responsibilities. The project supported participation but could not eliminate the underlying resource constraints facing community-based organizations.[^4]

**Environmental Challenges**

- **Site Constraints:** Some proposed greening sites had environmental or practical constraints (contamination, drainage, underground infrastructure) that affected feasibility or design.[^6]

- **Climate Variability:** Uncertainty about how climate change would affect urban tree survival and green space utility required adaptive management approaches.[^2]

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

**Problem-Solving Approaches**

- **Methodological Flexibility:** The team adapted engagement methods in response to COVID-19, shifting to digital platforms while recognizing limitations and seeking hybrid approaches when possible.[^3][^5]

- **Iterative Co-Creation:** Rather than presenting a fixed plan, the team engaged in iterative processes where community feedback shaped evolving proposals. Urban prototyping workshops allowed communities to experience and respond to proposed changes before final recommendations.[^5]

- **Multi-Pathway Approach:** Recognizing that implementation would depend on multiple actors and funding sources, the strategy identified diverse types of interventions (some requiring major capital investment, others achievable through smaller-scale community action) to create multiple pathways to greening.[^6][^5]

- **Data Democratization:** The team invested heavily in making scientific data accessible and comprehensible, recognizing that community power required access to the evidence base. This included not just explaining findings but teaching community members to interpret and use data themselves.[^2][^4]

- **Institutional Embedding:** Rather than relying on the research project's institutional support, the team worked to embed community advocacy structures (D8 Green Bridge Forum) within formal governance, creating ongoing mechanisms for community influence.[^4]

- **Policy Engagement:** The team engaged directly with Dublin City Council and policymakers, translating community recommendations into policy-relevant language and demonstrating how community-led approaches could strengthen planning outcomes.[^2][^4]

**Design Modifications**

- **Inclusive Site Selection:** When initial proposed sites encountered obstacles, the team identified alternative sites and multiple options for each type of intervention, recognizing that flexibility would be necessary for implementation.[^6]

- **Phased Implementation:** The strategy proposed phasing of interventions, recognizing that some projects would be feasible in the short term while others required longer-term planning and funding.[^6][^5]

- **Scalability of Interventions:** The strategy included both large-scale projects (new parks) and smaller-scale interventions (street tree planting, rain gardens, community gardens) recognizing that different scales of action would be necessary and that smaller projects could build momentum and demonstrate value.[^6][^5]

**Lessons Learned During Implementation**

- **Power and Trust Matter:** The project recognized that genuine community participation required not just methods but actual shifts in power and decision-making authority. Technical expertise alone was insufficient; trust-building and institutional change were equally important.[^4]

- **Arts and Creativity Enable Engagement:** Collaboration with artists and use of creative facilitation methods enabled engagement from community members who might not participate in traditional planning forums. This broadened participation and enriched the planning process.[^1][^4]

- **Sustained Engagement is Essential:** One-off consultation events were insufficient; ongoing relationships and repeated engagement opportunities were necessary to build understanding, trust, and collective vision.[^4]

- **Equity is Structural:** Addressing environmental inequity required not just identifying deficiencies but understanding and addressing the structural reasons for those inequities—land ownership patterns, development pressures, historical disinvestment, power imbalances in decision-making.[^5]

- **Implementation Requires Institutional Change:** Community-led planning strategies required changes in how government agencies operated, not just changes in what was planned. Dublin City Council needed to adapt its decision-making processes and timelines to accommodate genuine community participation.[^4]

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

**Physical Outputs**

While the Mapping Green Dublin project itself was research and strategy development rather than construction, it directly enabled specific physical outputs:

- **Comprehensive Urban Tree Inventory:** Complete scientific mapping and assessment of Dublin's urban tree canopy, with detailed analysis of tree species, size, condition, and environmental value[^2]
- **Greening Strategy Document:** Publication of "Strategic Pathways to Community-Led Greening" strategy document identifying specific sites for focused greening interventions and providing a replicable methodology for community-led greening strategy development[^5]
- **Urban Prototyping Installations:** Temporary installations and physical experiments in public space demonstrating proposed greening changes, allowing communities to experience and provide feedback on design concepts[^5]
- **Award-Winning Park Development:** Development of Turvey Park, an award-winning green space project that emerged directly from community advocacy enabled by the Mapping Green Dublin research[^4]

**Service Delivery**

- **Community Engagement Events:** Series of curated events, workshops, focus groups, and web-based discussions that created new mechanisms for community participation in planning processes[^1][^3][^5]
- **Capacity Building:** Training and skill development enabling community members to interpret GIS data, understand environmental assessment methodologies, and engage more effectively in planning discussions[^4]
- **Technical Assistance:** Ongoing support from research team and community organizations helping residents translate their knowledge and priorities into planning-relevant recommendations[^3]

**Quantitative Metrics**

- **Citywide Tree Inventory:** Complete survey of all trees in Dublin City Council area with environmental value assessment[^2]
- **Geographic Coverage:** Detailed mapping of Dublin 8 neighborhood (approximately 2-3 square kilometers)
- **Community Participation:** Multiple engagement events with dozens of community participants; development of permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum with sustained participation[^4]
- **Site Identification:** Specific identification of greening intervention sites with detailed recommendations and feasibility assessments[^6][^5]

#### **Social Impacts**

**Community Benefits**

- **Improved Quality of Life:** Proposed greening interventions directly addressed community concerns about neighborhood appearance and quality of life. Enhanced green spaces provide recreational opportunities, improved aesthetics, and psychological wellbeing benefits.[^6][^5]

- **Health Outcomes:** Green infrastructure improvements are associated with improved air quality, reduced urban heat island effects, increased physical activity opportunities, and improved mental health—benefits documented in the project's evidence base and anticipated from implementation.[^2][^6]

- **Safety and Social Cohesion:** Well-designed, actively used green spaces enhance neighborhood safety and create opportunities for social interaction and community building. The project emphasized multi-functional green spaces serving diverse community needs and age groups.[^6][^5]

**Equity Outcomes**

The project explicitly addressed environmental equity:

- **Targeted Investment in Underserved Areas:** By identifying Dublin 8 as experiencing significant green space deficiency, the project ensured that greening investment would prioritize an economically disadvantaged neighborhood rather than reinforcing existing environmental inequities[^2][^5]

- **Community Voice in Planning:** By centering community participation and knowledge, the project ensured that greening interventions responded to local priorities rather than external assumptions about what communities needed. This is fundamentally an equity issue—who gets to decide what constitutes improvement in one's neighborhood?[^3][^4]

- **Empowerment and Capacity Building:** Providing community members with access to scientific data, technical skills, and mechanisms for influence represented a shift in power dynamics. Residents became participants in planning decisions affecting their neighborhoods rather than passive recipients of expert decisions.[^4]

- **Addressing Displacement Risk:** While the project could not eliminate gentrification pressures, it explicitly incorporated equity considerations into strategy development, working to ensure that greening benefited existing residents and supported community continuity.[^4][^5]

**Displacement and Gentrification**

The project acknowledged but could not fully resolve the tension between neighborhood improvement and displacement risk. Green space improvements and neighborhood amenities can increase property values and attract investment, potentially displacing lower-income residents. The project addressed this through:

- **Community Ownership:** Emphasizing community stewardship and control of green spaces, helping ensure benefits accrued to existing residents
- **Affordable Housing Advocacy:** While beyond the project's direct scope, recommendations included consideration of housing affordability alongside greening
- **Equitable Development Framework:** Articulating principles of social and environmental justice to guide implementation[^5]

However, these measures address symptoms rather than root causes of gentrification pressures. The project's long-term success in preventing displacement would depend on broader housing and development policies beyond its direct control.[^4]

**Social Capital**

- **Community Networks:** The project strengthened existing community networks and created new connections among residents, community organizations, and institutions. The permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum provides ongoing mechanisms for collective action.[^4]

- **Civic Engagement:** By demonstrating that community input could meaningfully shape planning decisions, the project enhanced civic engagement and residents' sense of agency in shaping their neighborhoods.[^4]

- **Institutional Relationships:** The project built new relationships between academic institutions, arts organizations, community groups, and government, creating partnerships that extended beyond the original project into follow-on initiatives like Urban Grit.[^4]

- **Collective Efficacy:** Community members who participated developed increased confidence in their ability to influence planning processes and advocate for neighborhood improvements.[^4]

**Cultural Impacts**

- **Local Identity and Pride:** The project emphasized the importance of green spaces having "high social and cultural value within the community," recognizing that effective greening must connect to local identity and cultural meaning.[^1][^3]

- **Artistic Engagement:** Collaboration with artists and creative facilitation methods brought new perspectives to planning and enabled diverse forms of engagement and expression around greening and neighborhood change.[^1][^4]

- **Heritage Preservation:** The strategy included proposals to make accessible historic and cultural sites (churches, archaeological areas) as green spaces, connecting greening to cultural heritage.[^6]

#### **Economic Impacts**

**Job Creation**

- **Direct Employment:** The research project itself created employment for researchers, community facilitators, and artists[^1]
- **Implementation Employment:** Proposed greening interventions would generate construction and landscaping jobs during implementation, and ongoing maintenance employment[^6]
- **Community Enterprise:** The project supported community organizations and social enterprises (Connect the Dots) in delivering engagement services, strengthening the community economy[^1]

**Economic Activity**

- **Investment Attraction:** The strategy document identified that improved green infrastructure and neighborhood amenities could "attract and support inward investment in high quality work space and learning facilities and investment in evening economy and tourist accommodation," though this benefit must be balanced against displacement risks.[^6]

- **Business Support:** Local businesses contributed to funding the D8 Green Bridge Forum, indicating recognition of neighborhood greening as supporting local economic interests[^4]

- **Community-Based Economic Development:** The project supported community organizations and social enterprises, contributing to community-based rather than extractive economic development models[^4]

**Property Values**

Green infrastructure improvements typically increase nearby property values. While this can benefit existing property owners, it also creates displacement risk for renters and lower-income residents. The project acknowledged this dynamic and sought to structure benefits to support existing community members, though property value increases remain a concern.[^4][^5]

**Cost Savings**

- **Environmental Services:** Green infrastructure provides cost-saving environmental services including stormwater management (reducing flooding and drainage costs), improved air quality, and urban cooling (reducing energy costs), though the project did not quantify these savings.[^6]

- **Health Cost Reductions:** Improved green space access and associated physical activity and mental health benefits could reduce public health costs, though again specific quantification was limited[^2]

**Economic Inclusion**

The project addressed economic inclusion through:

- **Community Participation:** Ensuring that benefits of neighborhood improvement accrued to existing residents rather than only to external investors
- **Community Ownership:** Emphasizing community stewardship and control, supporting community economic agency
- **Equitable Development Framework:** Articulating principles to guide how economic benefits of greening could support rather than displace existing communities[^5]

However, these measures operate within broader economic structures that the project could not fundamentally alter. Long-term economic inclusion would depend on housing policy, development regulation, and wealth-building mechanisms beyond the project's scope.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

**Climate and Emissions**

- **Carbon Sequestration:** Urban trees sequester carbon dioxide, contributing to climate change mitigation. The comprehensive tree inventory and proposed tree planting expansion would increase Dublin's carbon sequestration capacity.[^2]

- **Urban Heat Island Mitigation:** Tree canopy and green space reduce urban temperatures, helping mitigate heat island effects that are exacerbated by climate change and disproportionately affect lower-income neighborhoods with less green cover.[^2][^6]

- **Climate Adaptation:** Green infrastructure supports urban resilience and adaptation to climate change impacts including increased rainfall and flooding events.[^2][^6]

**Resource Management**

- **Water Management:** Proposed rain gardens and tree pits with permeable surfaces manage stormwater runoff, reducing flooding risk and improving water quality. The strategy specifically proposed "diverting surface water run-off from roads and footpaths to large free draining tree pits."[^6]

- **Energy Efficiency:** Tree canopy and green space provide cooling benefits that reduce energy demands for air conditioning in buildings.[^2]

**Environmental Quality**

- **Air Quality:** Urban trees and vegetation improve air quality by filtering pollutants. The strategy proposed "substantial additional tree planting" to improve air quality, particularly important in the dense inner-city environment.[^6]

- **Noise Reduction:** Green infrastructure can reduce noise pollution from traffic and urban activity.[^6]

- **Green Space Access:** The project directly addressed green space deficiency, expanding access to nature for residents of a densely populated inner-city neighborhood. Proposed interventions would create or enhance six existing green spaces and establish new parks.[^6]

**Biodiversity**

- **Habitat Creation:** Green infrastructure improvements "enhance the biodiversity of the area by improving habitats for native flora and fauna."[^6] Diverse green space design supports diverse species.

- **Ecological Connectivity:** The proposed network of linked green spaces and green corridors (including cycle tracks and walkways) would create ecological connectivity, allowing species movement and genetic diversity.[^6]

- **Native Species:** The strategy emphasized best practice in tree planting and green space creation, supporting native species appropriate to Dublin's climate and ecology.[^6]

**Waste and Pollution**

The project did not directly address waste management, though green space design principles (replacing hard standing with soft landscape, managing runoff) reduce some forms of pollution. The strategy did not address pollution remediation for contaminated sites, though this was identified as a consideration for some proposed locations.[^6]

**Sustainability Metrics**

The project did not establish specific quantitative sustainability metrics or targets, though the strategy is grounded in sustainability principles. Implementation of the strategy would be expected to improve environmental sustainability indicators including:

- Tree canopy cover percentage
- Accessible green space per capita
- Stormwater management capacity
- Air quality measures
- Biodiversity indicators

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

**Institutional Capacity**

- **Dublin City Council:** The project strengthened council capacity for community-engaged planning. The evidence base and demonstrated methodology provided models for other neighborhoods and policy areas.[^2][^4]

- **Community Organizations:** The project enhanced community organizations' capacity to engage in technical planning discussions, interpret environmental data, and advocate effectively. Organizations like Common Ground and Connect the Dots demonstrated new models for arts-based and community-engaged facilitation.[^1][^4]

- **Academic-Community Partnership:** The project demonstrated models for genuine partnership between academic institutions and communities, with mutual learning and shared authority.[^4]

**Policy Influence**

- **Green Infrastructure Strategy:** The project directly influenced Dublin City Council's broader Green Infrastructure Strategy. The D8 Green Bridge Forum successfully argued for inclusion of "micro green areas" as part of the city's green infrastructure approach, demonstrating community influence on citywide policy.[^4]

- **Development Plan Amendments:** The project prompted statutory amendments to Dublin City's Local Area Plan and development plan to align with community-endorsed greening proposals, demonstrating that community input could reshape formal planning documents.[^6]

- **Replicable Methodology:** The project developed a methodology for community-led greening strategy development that has influenced approaches in other Dublin neighborhoods (North East Inner City, Stoneybatter) and has potential for broader application.[^4][^7]

**Transparency and Accountability**

- **Data Accessibility:** Publication of tree inventory data and mapping made environmental information publicly available, increasing transparency about Dublin's environmental conditions and inequities.[^2]

- **Decision Documentation:** The project documented its processes and decision-making, providing transparency about how community input shaped recommendations.[^5]

- **Accountability Mechanisms:** The permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum provides ongoing accountability, with community members able to track implementation of recommendations and advocate for follow-through.[^4]

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

**Success Metrics**

Mapping Green Dublin substantially achieved its stated objectives:

1. **Scientific Documentation:** ✓ Completed comprehensive inventory of Dublin's urban trees and green infrastructure[^2]
2. **Equity Assessment:** ✓ Identified significant environmental deficiencies in Dublin 8 and other inner-city areas[^2]
3. **Community-Led Strategy Development:** ✓ Developed detailed greening strategy grounded in community knowledge and priorities[^5]
4. **Empowerment and Capacity Building:** ✓ Demonstrated community members' ability to work with technical data and influence planning decisions[^4]
5. **Policy Influence:** ✓ Generated evidence and demonstrated processes that influenced Dublin City Council's green infrastructure approach[^4]

**Unintended Consequences**

- **Positive Unintended Consequences:** The project generated broader institutional change than originally anticipated. The D8 Green Bridge Forum evolved from a project engagement mechanism into a permanent community advocacy structure. Follow-on projects (Urban Grit, partnerships with other neighborhoods) extended the project's influence beyond the original scope.[^4]

- **Negative Unintended Consequences:** The project's success in raising awareness of neighborhood deficiencies and potential for improvement may have inadvertently accelerated gentrification pressures by attracting external investment and policy attention to the area. While the project sought to manage this through equity frameworks, the underlying tension between neighborhood improvement and displacement risk remained.[^4][^5]

**Long-term Viability**

The project's long-term viability depends on multiple factors:

- **Implementation:** Physical implementation of proposed greening interventions depends on Dublin City Council capital budgets and political will. The project's evidence base and community endorsement strengthen the case for investment, but implementation remains uncertain.[^4]

- **Methodological Adoption:** The community-led greening strategy methodology has potential for long-term adoption across Dublin and potentially other cities, though this requires institutional commitment and resource allocation.[^4]

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies**

Several elements of the Mapping Green Dublin project are specific to Dublin's context and may not transfer directly to other locations:

- **Institutional Structure:** The project depended on specific institutional relationships (UCD's research capacity, Common Ground's community relationships, Dublin City Council's receptiveness to community engagement). Other cities may have different institutional landscapes requiring adapted approaches.[^1][^4]

- **Policy Environment:** Ireland's environmental policy framework, Dublin City Council's commitment to green infrastructure, and the EPA's research funding priorities created enabling conditions. Other jurisdictions may have different policy environments.[^2]

- **Community Characteristics:** Dublin 8's specific demographic composition, historical development, community organization infrastructure, and existing grassroots movements shaped the project. Other neighborhoods would have different community characteristics requiring adapted engagement approaches.[^4][^5]

- **Urban Morphology:** Dublin's specific urban form (Victorian terraced housing, industrial heritage, contemporary development pressures) differs from other cities. The specific spatial interventions identified for Dublin 8 would need adaptation for different urban contexts.[^6]

- **Resource Availability:** The project benefited from EPA research funding and institutional resources. Resource-constrained contexts would need to adapt the approach or find alternative funding mechanisms.[^1]

**Critical Success Factors**

Elements that appear essential to the project's success and should be prioritized in replication:

1. **Genuine Community Power-Sharing:** The project's distinctiveness lay not in individual methods but in actual shifts in decision-making authority. Replication requires commitment to genuine partnership, not token consultation.[^3][^4]

2. **Transdisciplinary Integration:** Combining scientific expertise, artistic practice, community knowledge, and planning authority created richer understanding and more engaging processes than any single discipline could provide. Replication should maintain this integration.[^1][^4]

3. **Sustained Engagement:** One-off consultation events proved insufficient; sustained relationships and repeated engagement opportunities were necessary for building understanding and trust. Replication requires commitment to ongoing rather than episodic engagement.[^4]

4. **Institutional Alignment:** Community-led planning requires alignment between community priorities and institutional capacity for implementation. Replication should ensure that community engagement is coupled with government commitment to consider and implement recommendations.[^4]

5. **Equity Framework:** Explicit attention to environmental justice and equity shaped the project's focus on underserved neighborhoods and commitment to ensuring benefits accrued to existing residents. Replication should maintain this equity orientation.[^5]

**Scalability**

The approach demonstrates scalability in multiple dimensions:

- **Geographic Scaling:** The methodology developed for Dublin 8 has already been adapted for other Dublin neighborhoods (North East Inner City, Stoneybatter), suggesting potential for scaling across Dublin and other cities.[^4][^7]

- **Thematic Scaling:** The community-led strategy-making methodology could extend to other environmental and planning issues beyond greening (housing, transportation, public realm quality), though this would require additional adaptation.[^4][^5]

- **Institutional Scaling:** The approach could be adopted by other local authorities, though this would require institutional change and resource commitment.[^4]

- **Temporal Scaling:** The permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum demonstrates potential for scaling the engagement process from project-based to ongoing governance structures.[^4]

#### **Comparative Analysis**

**Similar Initiatives**

Mapping Green Dublin shares methodological and conceptual similarities with several other initiatives:

- **Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) Projects:** Academic-community partnerships in environmental health, urban planning, and social justice research employ similar collaborative methodologies, though Mapping Green Dublin's integration of artistic practice and formal planning influence is distinctive.[^3]

- **Participatory GIS (PGIS) Initiatives:** Other projects use GIS mapping combined with community participation, though Mapping Green Dublin's emphasis on co-creation rather than data collection alone is distinctive.[^2]

- **Community Gardens and Urban Agriculture Movements:** Grassroots movements for urban greening and community control of green space share similar values and community engagement approaches, though Mapping Green Dublin's integration with formal planning is distinctive.[^6]

- **Transition Towns and Community Resilience Initiatives:** Movements emphasizing community-led sustainability and local resilience employ similar participatory approaches and community empowerment frameworks.[^5]

**Best Practices**

Mapping Green Dublin exemplifies several best practices in urban development and planning:

1. **Evidence-Based Community Engagement:** Combining rigorous scientific evidence with community knowledge creates more robust and legitimate planning decisions than either alone.[^2][^3]

2. **Equity-Focused Planning:** Explicitly targeting environmental improvements in underserved neighborhoods and centering community voice in decision-making represents best practice in equitable urban development.[^5]

3. **Transdisciplinary Collaboration:** Integration of multiple disciplines (geography, art, community development, planning) creates richer understanding and more innovative solutions than disciplinary silos.[^1][^4]

4. **Institutional-Community Partnership:** Genuine partnerships between government institutions and communities, with shared authority and mutual learning, represent best practice in participatory governance.[^4]

5. **Process Documentation and Methodology Development:** Careful documentation of processes and explicit methodology development enables learning and replication, contributing to the field's knowledge base.[^5]

**Cautionary Lessons**

The project also offers cautionary lessons for other initiatives:

1. **Implementation Gap:** Strategy development is necessary but insufficient; translating community recommendations into physical implementation requires sustained attention, funding, and institutional commitment. Communities can become demoralized if recommendations are not implemented.[^4]

2. **Gentrification Risks:** Neighborhood improvements, while beneficial for existing residents, can attract investment and increase property values, potentially displacing lower-income residents. Community-led planning must be coupled with housing and development policies that protect existing residents.[^4][^5]

3. **Pandemic Disruption:** External shocks (like COVID-19) can significantly disrupt engagement processes and alter the nature of community participation. Planning should build in flexibility and contingency while recognizing that some disruptions cannot be fully mitigated.[^3][^5]

4. **Capacity Constraints:** Community organizations and residents have limited capacity for intensive engagement alongside other responsibilities. Initiatives should provide resources and support for participation rather than assuming voluntary engagement.[^4]

5. **Institutional Resistance:** Some government institutions may resist genuine power-sharing with communities, preferring consultation models where final authority remains with officials. Successful initiatives require institutional leadership committed to shared decision-making.[^4]

#### **Parallels to Green Building Neighborhoods (GBN) Framework**

The case study review identifies several parallels between Mapping Green Dublin and green building neighborhoods approaches:

**Digital Tools and Environmental Monitoring**

Both Mapping Green Dublin and GBN approaches employ digital tools (GIS mapping, visualization technologies, monitoring systems) to assess and plan for environmental quality. Mapping Green Dublin's use of GIS mapping and digital visualization parallels GBN's use of digital tools for monitoring and planning environmental performance. However, Mapping Green Dublin emphasizes making these tools accessible to communities rather than reserving them for experts, representing a distinctive democratization of environmental data.[^2][^3]

**Community Involvement in Defining Priorities**

Both approaches recognize that sustainable neighborhood development requires community input in defining priorities and thresholds. Mapping Green Dublin's community-led strategy development exemplifies this principle, with residents participating in identifying what constitutes environmental deficiency and what greening priorities matter most. This represents genuine power-sharing rather than token consultation, which may exceed typical GBN practice.[^3][^4]

**Neighborhood-Scale Focus for Health and Comfort**

Both approaches operate at neighborhood scale, recognizing that improvements in local environmental quality directly affect residents' health and comfort. Mapping Green Dublin's focus on Dublin 8 as a specific neighborhood unit parallels GBN's neighborhood-scale approach, emphasizing that sustainable development must be grounded in specific places and communities rather than abstract principles.[^1][^6]

**Measurable Environmental Outcomes**

Both approaches emphasize measurable environmental outcomes. Mapping Green Dublin's comprehensive tree inventory and identification of specific greening sites with environmental assessments parallels GBN's emphasis on quantifiable environmental performance metrics. However, Mapping Green Dublin also emphasizes qualitative and social outcomes beyond environmental metrics.[^2][^5]

**Integration of Multiple Dimensions**

Both approaches integrate environmental, social, and economic dimensions of sustainability. Mapping Green Dublin's attention to environmental quality, community wellbeing, economic opportunity, and social equity parallels GBN's holistic approach to neighborhood sustainability, though Mapping Green Dublin's explicit equity framework may exceed typical GBN practice.[^1][^2][^3][^6][^5]

**Transferability to GBN Contexts**

Elements of Mapping Green Dublin's approach could enhance GBN implementation:

1. **Community-Led Data Interpretation:** GBN initiatives could adopt Mapping Green Dublin's approach of making environmental data accessible to communities and supporting community interpretation and priority-setting, rather than experts determining priorities unilaterally.

2. **Equity Framework:** Mapping Green Dublin's explicit attention to environmental justice and equity could strengthen GBN approaches that might otherwise risk reinforcing existing inequities or creating new forms of exclusion.

3. **Transdisciplinary Integration:** GBN initiatives could benefit from Mapping Green Dublin's integration of artistic practice and creative facilitation alongside technical expertise, enabling more diverse forms of engagement and innovation.

4. **Institutional-Community Partnership:** GBN initiatives could adopt Mapping Green Dublin's model of genuine partnerships with shared authority, rather than top-down implementation of externally designed sustainability frameworks.

5. **Process Documentation:** GBN initiatives could follow Mapping Green Dublin's example of carefully documenting processes and making methodologies explicit, enabling learning and adaptation across contexts.

**Adaptation Considerations**

To apply Mapping Green Dublin's insights to GBN contexts, practitioners should consider:

1. **Local Context Adaptation:** The specific greening interventions, community engagement methods, and institutional arrangements identified for Dublin 8 would need significant adaptation for different urban contexts. Practitioners should conduct similar contextual analysis and community engagement rather than adopting Dublin's specific recommendations directly.

2. **Institutional Capacity:** GBN initiatives should assess existing institutional capacity for community engagement and plan for capacity building if necessary. Genuine community partnership requires institutional change, not just methodological innovation.

3. **Equity Integration:** GBN initiatives should explicitly incorporate equity frameworks, ensuring that neighborhood improvements benefit existing residents and do not displace vulnerable populations.

4. **Timeline Realism:** Mapping Green Dublin required 2-3 years for community-led strategy development. GBN initiatives should allocate sufficient time for genuine community engagement rather than rushing to implementation.

5. **Implementation Planning:** GBN initiatives should couple community engagement with concrete implementation planning and resource commitment, ensuring that community input translates into physical outcomes rather than remaining as recommendations.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Methodological Innovation**

Mapping Green Dublin's most significant strength lies in its genuine integration of scientific rigor, community knowledge, and artistic practice. Rather than treating these as separate domains, the project created complementary knowledge systems where scientific data informed community dialogue, artistic practice enabled creative engagement, and community knowledge enriched scientific understanding. This transdisciplinary approach produced richer analysis and more innovative solutions than disciplinary silos would have enabled.[^1][^3][^4]

**Equity Orientation**

The project's explicit commitment to environmental justice and equity distinguishes it from many urban development initiatives. By targeting an underserved neighborhood, centering community voice in decision-making, and addressing displacement risks, the project demonstrated commitment to equitable development. This orientation was not peripheral but central to the project's rationale and methodology.[^5]

**Genuine Community Power-Sharing**

Rather than token consultation, Mapping Green Dublin involved real shifts in decision-making authority. Community members participated in defining problems, developing solutions, and shaping recommendations. The permanent D8 Green Bridge Forum institutionalized ongoing community voice in implementation and adaptation. This represents genuine partnership rather than extractive engagement.[^4]

**Evidence Base for Policy Influence**

The comprehensive tree inventory and rigorous analysis provided an evidence base that strengthened the case for policy change. The project demonstrated that community-endorsed recommendations grounded in scientific evidence could influence formal planning documents and citywide policies.[^2][^4]

**Institutional Relationships**

The project built new relationships among academic institutions, arts organizations, community groups, and government. These partnerships extended beyond the original project into follow-on initiatives (Urban Grit, influence on other neighborhoods' greening strategies), demonstrating lasting institutional change.[^4]

**Replicable Methodology**

By carefully documenting processes and making methodologies explicit, the project created resources that other neighborhoods and cities could adapt. The published strategy document and project website provide practical guidance for community-led greening elsewhere.[^5]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Implementation Gap**

While the project successfully developed a community-endorsed strategy, translating recommendations into physical implementation remained uncertain. Capital funding for greening interventions depends on Dublin City Council's budgets and political will, which the project could not guarantee. Communities may become demoralized if recommendations are not implemented, potentially undermining trust in future participatory processes.[^4]

**Gentrification and Displacement Risks**

The project acknowledged but could not fully resolve the tension between neighborhood improvement and displacement risk. Green space improvements and increased neighborhood attention can attract investment, increase property values, and displace lower-income residents. While the project incorporated equity frameworks, these address symptoms rather than root causes of gentrification pressures. Long-term success in preventing displacement depends on broader housing and development policies beyond the project's control.[^4][^5]

**Pandemic Disruption**

COVID-19 lockdowns during Phase 2 (March-August 2020) significantly disrupted in-person community engagement and altered the nature of participation. While the team adapted by developing digital platforms, this necessarily excluded those without reliable internet access or digital literacy. The project's long-term implications of pandemic disruption remain uncertain.[^3][^5]

**Limited Scope**

The project focused on Dublin 8, a specific neighborhood, rather than addressing citywide environmental inequities. While the methodology is replicable, the project did not resolve broader systemic issues affecting Dublin's green infrastructure distribution. Scaling the approach citywide would require substantially more resources and institutional commitment.[^4][^7]

**Capacity Constraints**

Community organizations and residents have limited capacity for intensive engagement alongside other responsibilities. The project provided resources and support, but underlying capacity constraints limit the depth and breadth of participation possible. Relying on community volunteers for ongoing engagement (D8 Green Bridge Forum) may not be sustainable without institutional support and resources.[^4]

**Incomplete Equity Analysis**

While the project explicitly addressed environmental equity, the analysis could have been more thorough regarding other dimensions of justice (racial justice, disability justice, economic justice). The project centered environmental quality and community voice but did not deeply analyze how different community members experience and benefit from greening differently based on race, disability status, immigration status, or other dimensions of identity.[^4][^5]

**Limited Attention to Systemic Barriers**

The project focused on community engagement and strategy development but gave limited attention to the systemic barriers (property ownership patterns, development regulations, funding mechanisms, institutional cultures) that constrain greening in underserved neighborhoods. Addressing these barriers would require engagement with policy and regulatory reform beyond the project's scope.[^5]

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

**Environmental Justice**

Mapping Green Dublin exemplifies environmental justice principles by:

- **Targeting Underserved Communities:** Focusing on Dublin 8, which experienced significant environmental deficiency, ensures that greening investment prioritizes communities most in need rather than reinforcing existing inequities.[^2][^5]

- **Community Voice:** Centering community participation in decision-making ensures that environmental improvements respond to community priorities rather than external assumptions.[^3][^4]

- **Cumulative Benefits:** The project recognized that environmental inequities result from cumulative historical disinvestment and marginalization. Addressing these requires targeted investment and genuine community power-sharing.[^5]

**Social Justice**

The project advances social justice through:

- **Empowerment:** Providing community members with access to data, technical skills, and decision-making mechanisms enhances their capacity to influence conditions affecting their lives.[^4]

- **Dignity and Respect:** Treating community members as partners and co-creators rather than passive recipients of expert decisions respects their dignity and recognizes their knowledge and expertise.[^3][^4]

- **Collective Action:** Supporting community organizing and advocacy strengthens collective power and capacity for ongoing social change.[^4]

**Limitations in Justice Orientation**

Despite its strong equity commitment, the project has limitations:

- **Displacement Risk:** The project cannot fully prevent gentrification pressures that neighborhood improvement may trigger. Long-term justice requires policies protecting existing residents, beyond the project's scope.[^4][^5]

- **Systemic Barriers:** The project focuses on community engagement but gives limited attention to the systemic barriers (property ownership, development regulations, funding mechanisms) constraining change.[^5]

- **Incomplete Representation:** While the project sought to engage diverse community members, some voices (particularly the most marginalized) may remain underrepresented in participation processes.[^4]

#### **Future Outlook**

**Long-Term Trajectory**

Mapping Green Dublin's long-term trajectory depends on multiple factors:

- **Implementation of Recommendations:** Physical implementation of proposed greening interventions would validate the community-led approach and build momentum for future engagement. Lack of implementation would undermine trust and demonstrate that community participation remained advisory rather than genuinely influential.[^4]

- **Institutional Adoption:** Dublin City Council's adoption of the methodology for other neighborhoods and incorporation into broader green infrastructure policy would represent significant institutional change with lasting implications.[^4][^7]

- **Community Sustenance:** The D8 Green Bridge Forum's ability to sustain engagement and maintain influence would indicate whether community power-sharing can be institutionalized beyond individual projects.[^4]

**Ongoing Challenges**

Several challenges will shape the project's long-term success:

- **Funding for Implementation:** Securing capital funding for greening interventions remains uncertain and dependent on Dublin City Council's budgets and political priorities.[^4]

- **Gentrification Management:** Managing gentrification pressures while ensuring benefits accrue to existing residents requires ongoing attention and broader policy engagement.[^4][^5]

- **Sustaining Community Engagement:** Maintaining community participation and preventing volunteer burnout requires institutional support and demonstrated impact.[^4]

- **Equity Maintenance:** Ensuring that ongoing greening efforts maintain focus on equity and environmental justice, rather than drifting toward market-driven or elite-focused approaches.[^5]

**Evolution and Next Phases**

The project has already evolved beyond its original scope:

- **Urban Grit Initiative:** A follow-on project funded by the Irish Research Council extends the community-led approach to address local authority estate regeneration, demonstrating methodology application to housing and built environment issues.[^4]

- **Replication in Other Neighborhoods:** The methodology has been adapted for greening strategies in North East Inner City and Stoneybatter, indicating potential for broader adoption across Dublin.[^4][^7]

- **Policy Integration:** The project's influence on Dublin City Council's Green Infrastructure Strategy and other citywide policies suggests potential for deeper institutional integration.[^4]

- **Potential Expansion:** The methodology could potentially extend to other environmental and planning issues (housing, transportation, public realm) and to other cities, though this would require additional adaptation and resources.[^4][^5]

**Needed Evolution**

To enhance long-term impact, the project could evolve toward:

1. **Implementation Focus:** Shifting from strategy development to active implementation support, ensuring community recommendations translate into physical outcomes.[^4]

2. **Systemic Engagement:** Deeper engagement with systemic barriers and policy reform, not just community engagement at neighborhood scale.[^5]

3. **Equity Deepening:** More thorough analysis and action on multiple dimensions of justice (racial justice, disability justice, economic justice, immigrant justice) beyond environmental equity.[^4][^5]

4. **Institutional Transformation:** Working toward deeper changes in how government institutions operate, not just adding community engagement to existing structures.[^4]

5. **Scale Integration:** Connecting neighborhood-scale community engagement with city-scale policy and systemic change, recognizing that neighborhood improvements require supportive broader conditions.[^5]

---

This comprehensive case study demonstrates that Mapping Green Dublin represents a significant innovation in urban development practice by demonstrating how genuine community partnership, combined with scientific rigor and artistic creativity, can produce more equitable, culturally appropriate, and sustainable outcomes than top-down planning approaches. Its strengths—methodological innovation, equity orientation, genuine community power-sharing, and evidence-based policy influence—offer valuable lessons for urban development practitioners globally. Its limitations—implementation gaps, gentrification risks, and incomplete systemic engagement—indicate areas where future initiatives could build upon and improve this foundation. The project's significance lies not merely in the greening outcomes but in demonstrating that another approach to urban planning is possible: one grounded in community knowledge, committed to equity, and capable of genuinely sharing power with the communities most affected by development decisions.
