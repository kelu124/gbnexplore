# Executive summary

## THERMOS Project: District Heating & Cooling Planning Tools (Horizon 2020)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** THERMOS Project: Thermal Energy Resource Modelling and Optimisation System
- **Alternative Names:** THERMOS (Thermal Energy Resource Modelling and Optimisation System)
- **Location:** Multiple European Cities; pilot and replication sites include London (UK), Granollers (Spain), Jelgava (Latvia), Bristol (UK), Salaspils (Latvia), Żywiec (Poland), Parc de l’Alba (Spain), among others[^1][^2].
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Varies by pilot city; for example, Granollers (41.6080° N, 2.2887° E), Jelgava (56.6527° N, 23.7218° E).
- **Timeline:** Launched in November 2016; three-year duration through 2019, with ongoing dissemination and application[^2][^1].
- **Project Scale:** Consortium of 15 partners from 8 countries; hundreds of thousands of residents and businesses potentially affected; €2.1 million investment for one pilot (Barrio La Pinada); EU grant funding of €121,123 for London participation alone[^2][^1].
- **Leading Organization(s):** Centre for Sustainable Energy (Coordinator, UK); CREARA (Spain); Aalborg University (Denmark); Imperial College London (UK); Greater London Authority; other municipal and technical partners[^2][^1].
- **Funding Sources:** European Union Horizon 2020 programme (grant agreement No 723636); 100% EU grant funding for project partners[^2][^1].

#### **Executive Summary**
The THERMOS Project, funded under the EU Horizon 2020 programme, was designed to accelerate the development and upgrading of **low-carbon district heating and cooling (DHC) systems** across Europe. It addressed the critical need for rapid, sophisticated, and cost-effective thermal energy system planning by developing advanced software tools that leverage real-world data and high-resolution geospatial mapping. The project’s signature outcome—the THERMOS tool—enables cities to design, evaluate, and optimize DHC networks with unprecedented speed and accuracy, integrating considerations like renewable energy sources, climate targets, and operational efficiency[^1][^2].

Pilot cities and replication partners tested the tool in real planning scenarios, ranging from new urban developments to the expansion and decarbonization of existing networks. The project fostered collaboration among local authorities, technical experts, and stakeholders, advancing the European Green Deal’s vision for sustainable, resilient, and inclusive energy infrastructure[^1]. Key impacts include faster feasibility studies, reduced planning costs, improved stakeholder engagement, and more robust integration of renewables in urban energy systems. The initiative stands out for its open-source approach, transferability of methodologies, and alignment with ambitious climate and energy goals at city, regional, and national levels[^2][^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**
- **City Profile:** THERMOS operated in diverse urban environments including major metropolitan areas (London, Bristol), medium-sized cities (Granollers, Jelgava), and industrial/commercial districts. These cities represent varied economic bases (finance, industry, commerce), population scales (from tens of thousands to millions), and geographic characteristics (Northern, Western, and Eastern Europe)[^1][^2].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - Granollers: Industrial EcoCongost complex, characterized by high energy demand, mix of manufacturing and service businesses, and ongoing regeneration.  
  - Jelgava: Residential and commercial zones with existing DHC infrastructure and ambitions for expansion and decarbonization[^1].
  - Bristol: Urban neighborhoods with mixed land use, historic fabric, and new developments[^1].
  - Demographics: Pilot areas generally include a mix of income levels, with some focus on vulnerable populations (elderly, low-income)[^2].
  - Infrastructure: Existing DHC networks in some cities; others are initiating new systems or extending current networks. Accessibility and infrastructure quality vary, with some areas requiring modernization[^1].
  - Environmental Conditions: Urban air pollution, high energy demand, and climate adaptation pressures are common constraints[^1].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**
- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Fragmented, slow, and expensive DHC planning processes  
  - Insufficient integration of renewables and advanced analytics  
  - High carbon emissions from heating/cooling  
  - Need for more inclusive, resilient, and cost-effective urban energy solutions[^2][^1]
- **Triggering Events:**  
  - EU climate and energy targets (European Green Deal, national laws)  
  - Municipal sustainability strategies and decentralised energy targets  
  - Stakeholder advocacy for cleaner, more affordable energy[^2]
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Municipal authorities, technical consultants, energy companies, businesses, residents, EU agencies. Interests ranged from cost reduction and service improvement to climate action and energy security[^1][^2].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**
- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Participating cities operate under national and EU energy/climate policies; municipal agencies coordinate with national ministries and EU bodies[^2].
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - EU Green Deal, Climate Change Mitigation and Energy Strategy (London), local energy masterplans, zoning for energy infrastructure[^2][^1].
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - EU funding and open-source requirements facilitated collaboration  
  - Varied local permitting and data-sharing protocols posed challenges[^2][^1].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**
- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Accelerate deployment and upgrading of low-carbon DHC systems  
  - Enable more rapid, data-driven, and cost-effective planning  
  - Support integration of renewables and climate targets in energy infrastructure[^2][^1]
- **Vision Statement:**  
  - To empower European cities with innovative planning tools, advancing affordable, resilient, and sustainable energy for all[^2][^1].
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Directly supports EU Green Deal, national climate laws, and local energy masterplans (e.g., London Energy Plan)[^2][^1].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**
- **Theory of Change:**  
  - By providing advanced, open-source tools and high-resolution data, cities can overcome planning bottlenecks, lower costs, and accelerate the shift to low-carbon energy[^2][^1].
- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Municipalities, energy planners, businesses, and residents, especially those in areas with high energy burdens or climate vulnerability[^1].
- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - GIS-based network optimization, open-source tool, agile co-development with cities, integration of real-time data and renewable energy planning[^2][^1].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**
- **Political Drivers:**  
  - EU and local climate targets, mayoral priorities, public demand for cleaner energy[^2].
- **Economic Drivers:**  
  - Cost savings, job creation, investment in local energy infrastructure[^2][^1].
- **Social Drivers:**  
  - Energy affordability, inclusion of vulnerable groups[^2].
- **Environmental Drivers:**  
  - Climate mitigation, air quality improvement, resource efficiency[^1][^2].
- **Technological Drivers:**  
  - Advances in GIS, data analytics, and open-source software; smart city initiatives[^1][^2].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**
- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Data-driven, participatory, and open-source planning to democratize DHC development and integrate sustainability at all levels[^2][^1].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Development of address-level energy system maps, network optimization algorithms, and planning scenarios for new and existing DHC networks[^1][^2].
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Multi-phase: initial tool development, pilot city testing, replication in additional cities, ongoing dissemination and training[^1][^2].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**
- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - **GIS-based DHC optimization software (THERMOS tool)**: enables rapid scenario modeling, cost estimation, and renewable integration[^1].
  - **High-resolution energy mapping**: address-level demand/supply analytics[^2].
  - **Open-source platform**: ensures accessibility and adaptability[^2].
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (software, data analytics), process-oriented (agile development, stakeholder co-design)[^2][^1].
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Combined new data layers with existing municipal systems; iterative feedback from pilot cities[^1][^2].

#### **Implementation Methodology**
- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Consortium model; technical partners selected based on expertise and experience[^2].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Continuous feedback loops; pilot cities provided input on tool functionality and usability[^1].
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Co-design phases; training for municipal staff and stakeholders; open workshops[^1].
- **Project Management:**  
  - Work packages (WP): mapping, tool development, pilot testing, dissemination, project coordination[^2].

#### **Financing Model**
- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - EU grant funding covered 100% of partner costs; example investment: €2.14 million for a pilot network[^2][^1].
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Solely EU Horizon 2020 grants for development and implementation[^2].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Not directly applicable for the tool; anticipated downstream cost savings and new energy revenues for cities implementing DHC[^2][^1].
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Open-source model reduces ongoing costs; cities expected to leverage tool for cost-effective expansion and operation[^2][^1].

- **Financial Challenges:**

- **Social and Political Challenges:**  
  - Stakeholder training, managing expectations across cities, ensuring inclusivity in planning[^2][^1].
- **Administrative Challenges:**  
  - Coordination among consortium partners and cities; aligning local practices with EU frameworks[^2][^1].
- **Environmental Challenges:**

#### **Adaptive Strategies**
- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Agile development allowed for iterative improvements; regular feedback from users[^1].
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Tool functionalities adjusted based on pilot city needs; expanded mapping features[^1].
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
  - Importance of local stakeholder training, value of high-resolution data, need for flexible integration with existing systems[^1][^2].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**
- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - Production of energy system maps for pilot and replication cities; deployment of THERMOS tool (free, open-source)[^1][^2].
- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Improved planning services for DHC network expansion, feasibility studies, and stakeholder engagement[^1].
- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - In Barrio La Pinada, 73 buildings connected to DHC network; 6.76 km of pipework; 3.3 MW capacity utilized out of 5 MW available; €2.14 million capital investment[^1].

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- Rapid, accurate, and cost-effective DHC planning tool with open-source accessibility[^1][^2].
- Strong alignment with EU and municipal climate/energy strategies.
- Demonstrated capacity for replication, scalability, and stakeholder engagement.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- Data integration complexities across diverse city contexts.
- Reliance on ongoing training and capacity-building for sustained use.
- Not all localities may have the necessary data or technical infrastructure to fully utilize the tool.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- Inclusive planning process, targeting energy affordability for vulnerable groups[^2].
- No evidence of displacement or negative equity impacts; focus on democratizing access to planning resources.

#### **Future Outlook**
- Ongoing dissemination and adaptation of the tool in new cities and regions.
- Continued evolution of methodologies and capabilities as urban energy systems become more complex.
- Emerging opportunities for integration with other smart city and climate adaptation initiatives.
- Key challenge: ensuring sustained local engagement and technical capacity for long-term impact[^2][^1].
