# Executive summary## HABITA-RES Urban Neighborhood Energy Visualization Tool

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** HABITA-RES Urban Neighborhood Energy Visualization Tool
- **Alternative Names:** HABITA-RES; Proyecto HABITA-RES
- **Location:** Madrid, Spain; focus on peripheral and vulnerable neighborhoods (e.g., Villaverde Bajo, Canillas, Colonia de San Carlos)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approximate city center: 40.4168° N, 3.7038° W; specific neighborhood coordinates not detailed in sources
- **Timeline:** Initiated circa 2018; key milestones include development of the tool, neighborhood selection, in situ monitoring (2018–2021), ongoing analysis and dissemination[^1]
- **Project Scale:** Applied to six neighborhoods; 26 dwellings monitored; city-wide spatial analysis; affects populations in vulnerable districts (thousands of residents)[^1]
- **Leading Organization(s):** Instituto de Ciencias de la Construcción Eduardo Torroja (IETCC-CSIC), Municipal Housing and Land Company of Madrid, Technical University of Madrid, local neighborhood associations[^1]
- **Funding Sources:** Not fully specified; likely a mix of public research funding (CSIC), municipal support, and collaborative academic grants[^1]

#### **Executive Summary**

HABITA-RES is an integrative urban energy analysis initiative designed to empower vulnerable neighborhoods in Madrid by providing data-driven, actionable insights for energy efficiency improvements and retrofit opportunities. The project’s digital tool visualizes neighborhood-scale energy performance by combining spatial cadastre data, resident surveys, environmental sensor readings, and smart meter data. By modeling both current conditions and potential future scenarios, HABITA-RES enables stakeholders—including residents, planners, and policymakers—to target interventions that improve indoor comfort, reduce energy poverty, and support the integration of renewable energy systems.

The tool’s innovative approach lies in its capacity to harmonize large, disparate datasets and render them accessible to non-expert users via a mobile-friendly dashboard. It supports scenario analysis for retrofitting, material selection, and technology integration, including photovoltaic potential studies at the building and neighborhood scales. Residents benefit from transparent access to real-time and historical energy data, fostering engagement and informed decision-making. The project addresses critical challenges such as data harmonization and sustained resident participation, while delivering improved understanding of energy dynamics and targeted refurbishment strategies. Ultimately, HABITA-RES exemplifies the potential of digital urban tools to advance sustainability, equity, and resilience in low-income communities facing energy vulnerability[^1][^2][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Madrid is Spain’s capital and largest city, with a population exceeding 3 million. It is a major economic hub with diverse industries, a Mediterranean climate, and pronounced urban sprawl, especially in peripheral districts. Madrid’s urban hierarchy positions its outlying neighborhoods as sites of historic social housing, often lagging in infrastructure quality and energy performance[^1].

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
- **Historical Development:** Target neighborhoods were built from the late 1930s to the late 1970s, before modern building codes mandated adequate indoor environmental quality. Many are characterized by mass social housing and post-war urbanization patterns[^1].
- **Land Use:** Predominantly residential, with multi-unit housing blocks, limited green space, and aging infrastructure.
- **Demographic Profile:** High proportions of low-income households, elderly residents, and groups facing social disadvantage (high unemployment, low education, ethnic diversity)[^1].
- **Infrastructure Quality:** Buildings exhibit poor thermal envelopes, inefficient heating/cooling, and limited access to modern energy systems.
- **Environmental Constraints:** Vulnerable to temperature extremes, urban heat island effects, and poor air quality; limited resilience to climate change impacts.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
- **Energy Poverty:** High energy costs, inadequate heating, and fuel poverty disproportionately affect residents[^1][^4].
- **Inefficient Buildings:** Most structures have poor insulation, outdated windows, and inefficient systems, resulting in high energy demand.
- **Health and Comfort:** Poor indoor environmental quality leads to adverse health outcomes.
- **Social Exclusion:** Vulnerable groups experience reduced access to decision-making and information.

**Triggering Events:**  
- Recognition of the gap between European energy directives (Nearly Zero Energy Buildings, 2020; carbon neutrality by 2050) and the reality in vulnerable Spanish neighborhoods[^1].
- Policy momentum from Madrid’s municipal recovery and sustainable development plans (e.g., MAD-RE)[^1].

**Stakeholder Landscape:**  
- Residents (directly affected, key informants, and participants)
- Local government and housing authorities (implementers)
- Academic partners (tool developers and evaluators)
- Community associations (mobilization and outreach)

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**  
- Municipal leadership via Madrid’s housing and land company.
- Academic oversight and technical expertise from IETCC-CSIC and universities[^1].

**Relevant Policies:**  
- EU directives on energy efficiency, building codes, and carbon neutrality.
- Madrid’s local sustainable development strategies and recovery plans.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
- Existing frameworks for energy certification and refurbishment.
- Barriers include fragmented data sources, bureaucratic delays, and limited funding for deep retrofits.

**Conceptual Framework:**

- Urban Data Science (UDS) methods for multi-criteria analysis and scenario modeling[^1].

**Physical/Spatial Design:**  
- No direct physical construction, but targeted at informing retrofits in multi-unit housing blocks.
- Spatial reorganization through identification and prioritization of high-impact refurbishment zones.

**Scope and Phasing:**  
- Stage 1: Sensitive area selection via socioeconomic and energy vulnerability indicators.
- Stage 2: Monitoring campaign in representative dwellings.
- Stage 3: Model validation and scenario analysis using collected data[^1].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**  
- **GIS Data Integration:** Mapping and spatial analysis of urban energy performance.
- **Smart Meters:** High-resolution energy consumption monitoring.
- **Environmental Sensors:** Measurement of temperature, CO₂, humidity for indoor air quality.
- **PV Potential Analysis:** Solar irradiance and rooftop shading studies for renewable integration.
- **Visualization Dashboard:** Mobile-accessible platform for real-time and historical data access by residents[^1].

**Innovation Type:**  
- Technological (data integration, monitoring, visualization)
- Social (resident engagement and empowerment)
- Process-oriented (multi-stage participatory methodology)

**Integration Approach:**

- Calibration of theoretical models with real-world monitoring data.
- Integration with existing municipal databases and open-access spatial information[^1].

**Stakeholder Engagement:**  
- Residents involved through surveys, monitoring consent, and dashboard access.

**Participatory Elements:**  
- Residents co-create data by participating in monitoring and providing feedback.
- Transparent sharing of results via accessible digital platforms.

**Project Management:**  
- Coordinated by IETCC-CSIC, with input from municipal and academic partners.
- Structured around iterative data collection, analysis, and stakeholder feedback[^1].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Harmonizing and processing heterogeneous datasets (spatial, environmental, socioeconomic)[^1].
- **Financial:** Limited funding for comprehensive retrofits or widespread deployment.
- **Social and Political:** Sustaining resident engagement and overcoming skepticism.
- **Administrative:** Coordination across agencies and data providers; navigating bureaucratic delays.
- **Environmental:** Accurately modeling complex urban microclimates and solar potential in dense settings.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- Developed automated data integration algorithms to manage large, diverse datasets.
- Refined participatory methods to encourage resident involvement (e.g., mobile dashboards, feedback loops).
- Validated theoretical models with real monitoring data to improve accuracy and relevance.
- Leveraged community associations for outreach and trust-building[^1].

#### **Lessons Learned During Implementation**

- Importance of combining top-down (spatial analysis) and bottom-up (household monitoring) approaches.
- Need for ongoing engagement and transparent communication with residents.
- Value of flexible, modular tool design for adaptation to different neighborhoods.
