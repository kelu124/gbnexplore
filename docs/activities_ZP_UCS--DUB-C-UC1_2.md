# Executive summary

## Smart Waste Reduction in Canteens (Gothenburg, Sweden)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Smart Waste Reduction in Canteens
- **Alternative Names:** Smart Canteen Management (Gothenburg Smart Canteen System)
- **Location:** Gothenburg, Sweden; Municipal offices and university cafeterias
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 57.7089° N, 11.9746° E (central Gothenburg)
- **Timeline:** Pilot launch in late 2023; full rollout ongoing in 2024
- **Project Scale:** Municipal and university canteens; hundreds to thousands of daily users
- **Leading Organization(s):** City of Gothenburg; University of Gothenburg; Technology partners including local IT firms specializing in IoT and AI
- **Funding Sources:** Municipal budget; university resources; possible EU sustainability funds; private technology investment

#### **Executive Summary**
Gothenburg’s Smart Waste Reduction in Canteens initiative integrates **IoT sensors** and **AI analytics** across public sector cafeterias to monitor food inventory, consumption, and waste in real time. By providing live feedback to kitchen staff and diners, the system enables dynamic menu adjustments and portion recommendations, directly targeting food waste and optimizing kitchen operations. The project was motivated by the city’s broader sustainability goals and Sweden’s national push to reduce single-use packaging and food waste. Despite challenges in integrating legacy kitchen systems and staff training, the initiative has delivered significant results, including a **25% reduction in food waste**, better menu alignment with user demand, and enhanced operational efficiency. The program not only drives cost savings but also advances environmental objectives by reducing landfill waste and associated emissions, and improves user satisfaction and health outcomes. Gothenburg’s approach exemplifies how smart city technologies can empower facility managers and users through **data transparency**, setting a model for scalable, evidence-driven sustainability in urban food systems.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Gothenburg is Sweden’s second-largest city, with a population of ~600,000. It is a major industrial and university hub on the country’s west coast, known for its port, manufacturing sector, and strong focus on green urban development. The city ranks high in sustainability initiatives within Sweden and Europe[^1].
  
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The initiative spans various municipal offices (central and suburban locations) and university cafeterias (notably the University of Gothenburg campuses).
  - **Historical Development:** Gothenburg has a legacy of industrial growth, now transitioning toward tech and service industries, with a rising focus on sustainable urban renewal.
  - **Land Use:** Mixed-use, combining commercial, educational, and administrative spaces.
  - **Demographics:** Diverse, with both local Swedish and international student/staff populations; age ranges from students (18–30) to older municipal employees.
  - **Infrastructure Quality:** High-quality public infrastructure; kitchens and cafeterias vary in age and technological readiness.
  - **Environmental Conditions:** The city faces challenges with urban waste, particularly food and single-use packaging, as well as climate action commitments[^1].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High levels of **food waste** in public sector canteens.
  - Poor alignment of food preparation with actual user demand, leading to overproduction.
  - Lack of data-driven feedback for kitchen staff and management.
  - Broader national and EU mandates to reduce food and packaging waste[^1].
  
- **Triggering Events:**
  - Sweden’s adoption of the EU Single-Use Plastic Directive and new national laws mandating reusable food containers from 2024[^1].
  - Gothenburg’s municipal waste reduction targets (30% per capita waste reduction, near-zero climate footprint).
  
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - City departments (sustainability, waste management).
  - University administration and catering services.
  - Technology providers (IoT and AI firms).
  - Kitchen staff, diners, local food suppliers.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** Municipal government has strong authority over public building operations; university governance for campus facilities.
- **Relevant Policies:** EU and Swedish regulations on waste and packaging; city-level sustainability plans.
- **Legal/Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** Supportive legal context for sustainability, but operational and procurement rules complicated integration of advanced tech with older kitchen systems.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:** 
  - Reduce food waste in municipal and university canteens.
  - Increase operational efficiency and cost savings.
  - Improve menu planning and user health outcomes.
  - Advance Gothenburg’s climate and waste reduction targets.

- **Vision Statement:** To create a data-driven, adaptive food service system that minimizes waste, maximizes efficiency, and fosters sustainable habits among staff and diners.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** The initiative directly supports Gothenburg’s waste plan and climate action strategy, as well as Sweden’s national compliance with EU waste directives[^1].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:** By deploying real-time IoT sensors and AI analytics, kitchens receive actionable feedback to adjust portions and menus, reducing overproduction and waste, while empowering users to make informed choices.
- **Target Beneficiaries:** Kitchen staff, diners (municipal employees, university students/staff), facility managers, and the broader community (via environmental benefits).
- **Innovation Elements:** Use of real-time data for food service; live feedback for both staff and users; integration with existing systems.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Municipal leadership prioritizing sustainability; national policy mandates.
- **Economic:** Need to cut costs on food purchasing and waste disposal.
- **Social:** Desire for healthier, more satisfying food experiences; community support for climate action.
- **Environmental:** Targets for waste reduction and climate footprint.
- **Technological:** Availability of advanced IoT sensors and AI analytics platforms[^2][^3][^4].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Smart city and circular economy principles; adaptive management; behavioral feedback for sustainability.
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** Retrofit of kitchens with IoT weight sensors; installation of user-facing feedback screens.
- **Scope and Phasing:** Initial pilots in select canteens, followed by staged rollout.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:** 
  - **IoT weight sensors** for real-time inventory and waste tracking.
  - **AI-driven analytics dashboard** for menu planning and performance monitoring.
  - **User-facing feedback screens** in dining areas.
  - Optional integration with **biometric/RFID payment and entry systems**[^2][^3][^4][^5].
  
- **Innovation Type:** Technological and process-oriented.
- **Integration Approach:** Gradual integration with legacy kitchen systems; cloud-based management for scalability; interoperability with existing payment and ordering infrastructure.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement/Contracting:** Technology partners selected via municipal/university procurement; emphasis on local IT firms with smart city experience.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Training for staff; workshops for users; feedback channels for ongoing improvement.
- **Participatory Elements:** Users can review waste stats and receive personalized feedback; kitchen staff involved in menu design adjustments.
- **Project Management:** Steering committee including city, university, and tech stakeholders; regular monitoring and reporting.

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget/Costs:** Investment in sensor hardware, software platforms, staff training, and retrofits; estimated hundreds of thousands of EUR for pilot phase.
- **Funding Mix:** Municipal and university budgets; technology partner contributions; potential EU sustainability grants.
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Cost savings on food and waste processing feed back into operational budgets.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Long-term savings expected from reduced food purchases and waste fees.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Integration with aging kitchen equipment; ensuring sensor accuracy in busy environments.
- **Financial:** Upfront investment costs; securing ongoing maintenance funding.
- **Social/Political:** Staff resistance to new workflows; need for training and cultural shift.
- **Administrative:** Coordination across multiple departments and sites.
- **Environmental:** Ensuring system supports broader waste reduction (not just in canteens, but packaging and supply chain as well).

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Phased deployment to allow for troubleshooting; iterative staff training.
- **Design Modifications:** Upgraded interfaces for ease of use; improved reporting dashboards.
- **Lessons Learned:** Need for early and ongoing staff engagement; importance of clear, actionable feedback for users.
