# Executive summary## Districlima: Barcelona District Heating and Cooling Network (Forum and 22@ Districts)

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** Districlima: Urban Network of Heat and Cold in Barcelona and Sant Adrià de Besòs
- **Alternative Names:** Districlima, Barcelona District Heating and Cooling Network, DHC Forum/22@ District
- **Location:** Barcelona, Spain; specifically Forum and 22@ technology districts, with expansion into Sant Adrià de Besòs
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 41.4100° N, 2.2189° E (Forum area)
- **Timeline:**
  - **Project Initiation:** 2002 (company formation)
  - **Operational Start:** 2004 (Forum area)
  - **Major Expansion:** 2005 (22@ District, following public competition)
  - **Current Status:** Ongoing, further expansion planned
- **Project Scale:**
  - Network length: Over 15 km
  - Area served: >300 ha
  - Buildings served: >90 (offices, housing, public facilities, businesses)
  - Floor area covered: >870,000 m² (as of 2016)
  - Heating capacity: 55–80 MW
  - Cooling capacity: up to 80 MW
  - Investment: €55 million (initial phase)
- **Leading Organizations:**
  - Districlima S.A. (consortium)
    - Engie (50.8%)
    - TERSA (20%)
    - Aguas de Barcelona (Agbar, 19.2%)
    - Catalan Energy Institute (ICAEN, 5%)
    - Institute for Energy Diversification and Saving (IDAE, 5%)
- **Funding Sources:** Public-private partnership (PPP), involving municipal government, private utility companies, and regional/national energy agencies

#### **Executive Summary**

Districlima is Barcelona’s flagship district heating and cooling (DHC) network, serving the Forum and 22@ technology districts. Conceived as a high-efficiency, low-carbon urban energy solution, it centralizes the production and distribution of thermal energy (for heating, cooling, and domestic hot water) to a diverse mix of over 90 buildings. The system’s innovation lies in harnessing waste heat—specifically, steam from municipal solid waste (MSW) incineration at the adjacent TERSA plant—combined with seawater-based cooling, drastically reducing reliance on fossil fuels. Launched in 2004 and expanded in 2005, Districlima now covers more than 15 km of underground piping, serving office towers, residential complexes, hospitals, schools, and public infrastructure in Barcelona’s rapidly developing urban areas.

Environmental sustainability is at the project’s core: the network avoids over 17,500 tonnes of CO₂ emissions annually, reduces fossil energy consumption by about 60%, and eliminates the need for hundreds of noisy, inefficient cooling towers. Socially, Districlima improves urban quality of life through quieter neighborhoods, safer air, and more reliable, affordable energy. Economically, it enhances building value, reduces maintenance, and supports local job creation. Technical and organizational challenges—including integration with diverse building types, tariff design, and phased construction—were addressed through robust governance, adaptive planning, and continuous monitoring using smart data networks. As a model of public-private collaboration and smart urban infrastructure, Districlima demonstrates how cities can leverage local resources and technology for climate action, resilience, and inclusive urban regeneration[^1][^2].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Barcelona, the capital of Catalonia, is a Mediterranean metropolis with a population of ~1.7 million (urban area >3 million), a diversified service and industrial economy, and a global reputation for urban innovation. The city is dense, compact, and known for its ambitious sustainability and smart city strategies.

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - **Forum Area:** Former industrial waterfront redeveloped for the 2004 Universal Forum of Cultures, now a mixed-use district with offices, hotels, and cultural venues.
  - **22@ District:** Barcelona’s “innovation district,” transformed from obsolete industrial land into a hub for technology, R&D, residential, and creative industries.
  - **Land Use:** High proportion of office, commercial, and public facility space, with integrated housing.
  - **Demographics:** Diverse, with a mix of young professionals, students, and long-term residents; increasing multiculturalism due to business and academic migration.
  - **Infrastructure:** High-quality transport, digital infrastructure, but challenged by energy dependence, urban heat, and legacy pollution.
  - **Environment:** Coastal Mediterranean climate (Csb), with mild, dry summers; urban heat island effect and air quality among policy priorities.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High dependence on imported fossil fuels (Spain imports ~70% of its energy)
  - Significant urban greenhouse gas emissions
  - Inefficient, decentralized building-level heating and cooling (high costs, noise, maintenance, and health risks from cooling towers)
  - Need for competitive, modern infrastructure to attract investment and support urban regeneration

- **Triggering Events:**
  - 2004 Forum of Cultures: Major urban transformation, opportunity for infrastructure innovation
  - City climate targets and European Union directives on energy and emissions

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - Barcelona City Council (urban regeneration, climate action)
  - Utility and infrastructure companies (Engie, Aguas de Barcelona)
  - Regional and national energy agencies (ICAEN, IDAE)
  - Building owners, residents, businesses, and local communities

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  - Project developed under a PPP model, with long-term concessions (25–27 years) for design, construction, and operation.
  - Governed by municipal and regional energy/climate policy frameworks.

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Agenda 21 local action plan
  - EU directives on energy efficiency, emissions, and renewable energy
  - Zoning and urban development policies for Forum and 22@ districts

- **Legal and Institutional Factors:**  
  - Urban development permit required
  - Supportive legal framework for waste-to-energy and district energy projects
  - Institutional capacity for multi-stakeholder coordination[^1]

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Reduce fossil fuel consumption and GHG emissions in urban energy use
  - Provide reliable, affordable, and efficient heating and cooling to support urban regeneration and economic growth
  - Enhance building sustainability and urban livability

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To create a competitive, resilient, and sustainable urban energy system that leverages local resources and innovation, making Barcelona a leader in climate-smart urban development.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Directly supports Barcelona’s climate action plan, energy transition policy, and smart city strategy; aligns with EU energy and climate targets[^2].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  Centralized production using local waste heat and seawater cooling enables economies of scale, higher efficiency, and reduced emissions. By distributing this energy through a network, the initiative transforms the environmental and economic performance of entire districts.

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Residents (lower bills, better air, less noise)
  - Businesses (cost savings, reliability, green branding)
  - City (progress toward climate goals, local job creation)
  - Investors/developers (higher property values, easier compliance)

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - First large-scale urban DHC network in Spain using waste-to-energy and seawater cooling
  - Smart city integration via data-driven network management
  - Public-private partnership financing and governance[^1]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  Demonstrate climate leadership, attract investment, fulfill EU directives

- **Economic:**  
  Lower energy costs, support for new economic clusters (22@), long-term savings

- **Social:**  
  Improve urban quality of life, reduce health risks, foster inclusive regeneration

- **Environmental:**  
  Mitigate climate change, improve air quality, reduce water and chemical use

- **Technological:**  
  Leverage advances in heat exchangers, smart metering, and waste-to-energy integration

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Centralized, high-efficiency, low-carbon energy production and distribution system integrated with urban redevelopment.

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Two main energy centers (Forum and Tànger)
  - 15+ km of underground piping (four-pipe system for heating and cooling)
  - Connections to >90 buildings across mixed land uses[^1]

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Phase 1: Forum area (2002–2004)
  - Phase 2: 22@ District expansion (2005 onward)
  - Ongoing: Extension to additional facilities (e.g., Hospital del Mar, new residential/commercial developments)

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - Waste-to-energy steam from TERSA MSW incineration plant (main heat source)
  - Seawater-cooled chillers (high efficiency, up to 4x better than conventional)
  - Ice water accumulator (5,000 m³ storage tank) for demand management
  - Smart data network for real-time monitoring and optimization
  - Backup gas boilers (for redundancy)[^1]

- **Innovation Type:**  
  Technological (energy, ICT), governance (PPP structure), process (phased, adaptive roll-out)

- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Direct connections to diverse new and existing buildings
  - Flexible resource allocation and easy scalability for new users

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Public tender for concession
  - Long-term contracts with private sector (Engie, Agbar, TERSA)

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - Coordination with city planners, developers, building owners
  - Ongoing outreach for new client connections

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Indirect: urban planning consultations; building owners opt-in to connect

- **Project Management:**  
  - Dedicated project company (Districlima S.A.)
  - Continuous performance tracking and reporting[^1]

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Initial investment: €55 million (pre-tax, 2002–2004 prices)
  - Cost breakdown: energy centers, piping network, ICT systems, connection infrastructure

- **Funding Mix:**  
  - Majority private investment (Engie, Agbar)
  - Public sector support (Barcelona City Council, ICAEN, IDAE)

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - User fees (energy tariffs for connected buildings)
  - Long-term service contracts

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - 25–27 year concession ensures stable revenue
  - Operational savings and economies of scale support ongoing viability

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Complex engineering for integration with diverse building types
  - Infrastructure extension in dense urban environment
  - Ensuring backup/redundancy for uninterrupted supply

- **Financial:**  
  - High upfront capital requirements
  - Tariff design to balance affordability and ROI for investors

- **Social/Political:**  
  - Need for broad stakeholder buy-in (building owners, residents)
  - Coordination between multiple government levels and private actors

- **Administrative:**  
  - Navigating permitting, legacy infrastructure, and bureaucratic processes

- **Environmental:**  
  - Site contamination risks (brownfield redevelopment)
  - Managing and monitoring emissions from MSW incineration

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Phased roll-out to manage construction impacts and risks
  - Use of redundant systems (backup boilers, dual energy centers)
  - Smart network management for continuous optimization[^1]

- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Addition of a “peaks central” (Tànger) to handle demand surges
  - Advanced storage solutions for balancing load

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Importance of flexible design for future scalability
  - Value of public-private collaboration and clear governance

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Pioneering use of waste-to-energy and seawater cooling in Spain
- Significant environmental and economic benefits, with robust monitoring and reporting
- Model public-private partnership and governance
- Strong alignment with smart city and climate action agendas[^1]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- High initial capital investment required
- Complexity of integrating old and new buildings
- Dependence on continued operation of MSW incineration plant for primary energy source

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Broad access to affordable energy for diverse urban populations
- No evidence of displacement or inequity linked to project; ongoing need to monitor for indirect gentrification effects

#### **Future Outlook**

- Continued expansion to new buildings and districts (e.g., Hospital del Mar)
- Ongoing upgrades in efficiency, digitalization, and integration with renewables (potential for solar thermal supplementation[^5])
- Model for replication in other Mediterranean and European cities facing similar energy and urban challenges
