# Executive summary## HABITA-RES: Energy Assessment and Refurbishment of Vulnerable Urban Areas

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** HABITA-RES: Energy Assessment and Refurbishment of Vulnerable Urban Areas
- **Alternative Names:** HABITA-RES Integrative Refurbishment Tool
- **Location:** Madrid, Spain (focus on multiple vulnerable neighborhoods, particularly on the outskirts)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Centered around Madrid, ~40.4168° N, 3.7038° W (neighborhood-level detail available in project documentation)
- **Timeline:** Initiated in the late 2010s; major phases between 2018–2023; data collection and monitoring 2019–2021; ongoing application and analysis
- **Project Scale:** Six representative neighborhoods, 26 monitored dwellings; city-wide vulnerability mapping; population affected: thousands in target districts
- **Leading Organization(s):** Institute of Construction Sciences Eduardo Torroja (IETcc-CSIC), with support from the Madrid City Council, Technical University of Madrid (ETSAM), and community associations
- **Funding Sources:** Public research funding (national and regional), with in-kind support from local authorities and academic institutions

#### **Executive Summary**
HABITA-RES is a pioneering urban regeneration initiative developed in Madrid to address the acute problem of energy vulnerability in post-war residential neighborhoods. These areas, often built between the 1940s and 1970s, are characterized by poor energy efficiency, deteriorating infrastructure, and high concentrations of low-income and elderly residents. The project developed and deployed an integrative digital tool that combines spatial data, real-time smart meter readings, building attributes, and user surveys to map and assess energy demand, indoor environmental quality, and refurbishment needs at both the building and neighborhood scale. This tool enables stakeholders—including local authorities, building managers, and residents—to visualize energy performance, identify high-impact retrofit measures, and evaluate the potential for on-site renewable energy (notably rooftop solar PV). The methodology prioritizes passive refurbishment strategies and empowers vulnerable populations to participate in decision-making by making information accessible and actionable. HABITA-RES stands out for its data-driven approach, combining bottom-up monitoring with top-down spatial analysis, and for directly addressing issues of energy poverty, health, and environmental sustainability in marginalized urban contexts. Its outcomes have informed local policy, improved indoor environmental quality for monitored homes, and provided a replicable model for other European cities facing similar challenges[^1][^2][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Madrid is Spain’s capital and largest city, with over 3 million residents in the city proper and a broader metropolitan region of more than 6 million. It has a service-based economy, a continental Mediterranean climate (hot summers, cool winters), and significant urban expansion since the 20th century. Madrid is a national leader but contains pockets of deprivation, especially in peripheral districts[^1][^4].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  Target areas are mostly on the city’s outskirts, developed rapidly between 1940–1980, often as social or worker housing. Land use is predominantly residential, with some commercial and public space. Demographically, these areas have higher concentrations of low-income households, elderly residents, and immigrants. Infrastructure is aged, with buildings lacking modern energy performance standards. Environmental conditions include exposure to urban heat island effects, poor insulation, and limited green space[^1][^4].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - High rates of energy poverty (inability to afford adequate heating/cooling)
  - Inefficient building envelopes (poor insulation, outdated windows, leaky façades)
  - Poor indoor air quality and thermal comfort
  - Socioeconomic vulnerability (low income, high unemployment, aging population)
  - Lack of access to data for informed refurbishment and energy management[^1][^3]

- **Triggering Events:**  
  No single disaster, but cumulative recognition of energy poverty as a structural urban issue, and local policy shifts (e.g., MAD-RE Plan) supporting energy retrofits[^1].

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  Key actors include local government (Municipal Housing and Land Company), academic researchers, community associations, and residents. Interests ranged from improving housing quality to reducing energy costs and meeting climate targets.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  City of Madrid, with support from regional and national bodies responsible for housing, energy, and urban planning.

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Spain’s Technical Building Code (CTE-DB-HE) on energy efficiency
  - MAD-RE program for urban regeneration
  - National and EU policies on energy poverty and climate adaptation

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  Open-access spatial and statistical data; supportive local policy. Barriers included fragmented building ownership and limited resident engagement[^1].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Improve habitability and indoor environmental quality in vulnerable neighborhoods
  - Reduce energy demand and dependency through integrative refurbishment
  - Empower residents via access to actionable information[^1]

- **Vision Statement:**  
  Toward energy self-sufficient, bio-healthy neighborhoods that are inclusive, resilient, and sustainable.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Supports Madrid’s climate action plan, national targets for energy poverty reduction, and EU sustainable urban development agendas.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  By collecting and integrating spatial, building, and behavioral data, the project identifies the most impactful and feasible refurbishment measures, leading to improved energy efficiency, lower bills, and better health for vulnerable residents.

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  Residents of energy-poor neighborhoods (especially elderly and low-income households); local authorities responsible for housing; policy-makers; the general public via reduced emissions.

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Integrated digital platform for energy assessment
  - Combination of top-down (spatial analysis) and bottom-up (in situ monitoring)
  - Participatory feedback loops with residents[^1][^2][^3]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  Addressing energy poverty and urban inequality; responding to policy mandates on climate and inclusion.

- **Economic:**  
  Potential for cost savings through targeted retrofits; leveraging public funding for regeneration.

- **Social:**  
  Community advocacy for better living conditions; reducing health disparities.

- **Environmental:**  
  Climate resilience, reduced emissions, adaptation to heat waves.

- **Technological:**  
  Advances in smart metering, spatial data integration, and digital visualization tools[^1].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Urban data science for integrative management of complex building, social, and environmental data; prioritizing passive over active refurbishment.

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  No new construction; focus on assessment, monitoring, and retrofitting existing buildings. Mapping and visualization of energy demand and solar potential.

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Stage 1: Identification and classification of sensitive/vulnerable areas
  - Stage 2: Detailed data collection and modeling
  - Stage 3: Calibration, validation, and participatory feedback[^1]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Energy analysis models (based on ISO 13790 standard)
  - Spatial databases (cadastre, census, weather)
  - Smart meters for real-time energy monitoring
  - User surveys and participatory apps
  - Visualization platforms (web tools, mobile access)
  - Rooftop solar PV potential analysis

- **Innovation Type:**  
  Primarily technological, with significant social/process innovation (resident engagement).

- **Integration Approach:**  
  Automated data collection and processing (Python, Pandas, GeoPandas); linking administrative, technical, and resident-facing systems[^1].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  Led by IETcc-CSIC, with institutional and community partners.

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  Residents recruited for monitoring; community associations involved in outreach; findings shared with occupants and local authorities.

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  Residents accessed their own data; surveys captured behavioral patterns and preferences; co-design of improvement measures.

- **Project Management:**  
  Academic-led, cross-sectoral coordination, using iterative feedback cycles[^1].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  Not fully disclosed; primarily public/academic research funding, with in-kind support.

- **Funding Mix:**  
  National/regional research grants, local government co-funding.

- **Revenue Mechanisms & Financial Sustainability:**  
  Not a revenue-generating project; benefits accrue via cost savings to residents and city over time.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  Difficulties in collecting comprehensive building and user data; integrating disparate databases; calibrating models with real-world data.

- **Financial:**  
  Limited resources for full-scale retrofits; dependence on public funding.

- **Social and Political:**  
  Engaging vulnerable residents; overcoming data privacy concerns; building trust.

- **Administrative:**  
  Coordination among multiple agencies and data holders.

- **Environmental:**  
  No major environmental constraints reported, but climate variability affects monitoring[^1].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  Stepwise data collection, starting with most vulnerable areas; flexible feedback loops; use of open-source tools for integration.

- **Design Modifications:**  
  Iterative model calibration; expanded resident engagement as trust grew.

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  Importance of participatory processes; need for multi-scale data; sensitivity to privacy and data ownership[^1].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**  
  No new infrastructure, but detailed energy and indoor environmental quality mapping for six neighborhoods; 26 homes monitored; web-based visualization tool developed.

- **Service Delivery:**  
  Residents gained access to actionable data; local authorities received guidance on retrofit priorities.

- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - 10,750,000 MWh/year estimated heating demand (citywide, 2011)[^1]
  - Extensive spatial and building data compiled

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  Improved indoor environmental quality during COVID-19 lockdowns; empowerment through information.

- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  Targeted the most vulnerable; direct support for low-income elderly; participatory engagement reduced barriers.

- **Displacement and Gentrification:**  
  No evidence of displacement; focus was on in-place improvement.

- **Social Capital:**  
  Enhanced through collaboration with community groups.

- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  Strengthened neighborhood identity by focusing on historic social housing[^1].

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  Limited, mainly through research, monitoring, and local engagement.

- **Economic Activity:**  
  Potential stimulus for retrofit sector; indirect benefits via energy savings.

- **Property Values:**  
  Not directly measured; improved building quality may enhance value over time.

- **Cost Savings:**  
  Residents benefited from lower energy bills; city may save on social support.

- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  Addressed energy poverty in marginalized populations[^1].

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  Projected reductions in greenhouse gas emissions through lower energy use and increased solar PV adoption.

- **Resource Management:**  
  Improved energy efficiency and renewable integration.

- **Environmental Quality:**  
  Better air quality and thermal comfort in monitored homes.

- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  Not directly addressed, though retrofits may reduce resource waste.

- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  Performance aligned with national and EU standards for energy efficiency; no formal certifications noted[^1].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  Developed new models and tools for citywide application; fostered inter-agency collaboration.

- **Policy Influence:**  
  Findings informed urban regeneration and retrofit policies in Madrid.

- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  Open-access data and participatory feedback increased transparency[^1].

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  High achievement in mapping, monitoring, and tool development; demonstrable impact on awareness and policy.

- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  None significantly reported; some initial resident skepticism overcome.

- **Long-term Viability:**  
  Tools and methods are replicable and remain in use; further scaling depends on funding and policy support[^1].

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:**  
  Relies on availability of spatial/building data, smart meter access, and community engagement capacity.

- **Critical Success Factors:**  
  Strong institutional partnership; resident trust; integration of multiple data streams; actionable visualization.

- **Scalability:**  
  Methodology is scalable to other neighborhoods and cities, especially those with similar housing stock and data access[^1][^3].

#### **Comparative Analysis**

- **Similar Initiatives:**  
  Related to energy poverty mapping in London, and EU projects on smart retrofitting, but HABITA-RES is distinctive in its multi-level data integration.

- **Best Practices:**  
  - Use of both top-down and bottom-up data
  - Participatory feedback
  - Public access to actionable information

- **Cautionary Lessons:**  
  - Data privacy must be prioritized
  - Resident engagement is essential, not optional
  - Full retrofit impact requires financial resources beyond assessment[^1].

  Use of smart meters, spatial analysis, digital dashboards.

- **Transferable Strategies:**

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Holistic integration of spatial, technical, and social data
- Prioritization of the most vulnerable populations
- Transparent, participatory process
- Scalable and replicable digital tools[^1][^3]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Retrofit implementation depends on external funding and policy
- Data privacy concerns required careful management
- Some challenges in initial resident engagement
- Limited direct measurement of long-term economic impacts[^1]

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Advanced equity by focusing on energy-poor neighborhoods and involving residents in decision-making
- No evidence of displacement or negative gentrification effects

#### **Future Outlook**

- Ongoing use and scaling of the digital assessment tool
- Potential for broader citywide application, subject to funding
- Future phases may focus more on implementation and post-retrofit monitoring
- Continued adaptation to evolving urban climate and demographic trends[^1][^3]
