# Executive summary

## I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab (part of the broader I-CHANGE Project)
- **Alternative Names:** I-CHANGE Living Lab; Milan Climate Action and Environmental Engagement Initiative
- **Location:** Milan, Italy (with coordination through the Department of Physics and Astronomy "Augusto Righi" at the University of Bologna, though Milan-specific activities focus on urban neighborhoods and schools throughout the city)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approximately 45.46°N, 9.19°E (Milan city center)
- **Timeline:** 2021-present (ongoing); part of the broader I-CHANGE European project spanning multiple years with continuous monitoring and community engagement activities
- **Project Scale:** City-wide implementation across multiple schools and urban neighborhoods; serves student populations, families, and broader Milan residents; encompasses air quality monitoring, sustainable mobility initiatives, and energy efficiency programs across multiple districts
- **Leading Organization(s):** University of Bologna (coordination and research); I-CHANGE Project consortium; Milan municipal authorities; local schools; regional environmental protection agencies[^1][^2]
- **Funding Sources:** European Union funding through the I-CHANGE European research and innovation action; national and regional funding mechanisms; institutional support from University of Bologna and municipal resources

#### **Executive Summary**

The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab represents a pioneering citizen science and community engagement initiative designed to address Milan's pressing environmental challenges, particularly air pollution and climate change impacts.[^2] Launched in 2021 as part of a broader European research initiative, the project transforms Milan into an experimental laboratory where citizens, researchers, municipal authorities, and educational institutions collaborate to develop practical solutions for urban sustainability.[^1] The initiative operates through a "quadruple helix" framework connecting government, academia, industry, and civil society to tackle interconnected environmental issues including air quality degradation, energy inefficiency in buildings, unsustainable transport patterns, and urban biodiversity loss.[^2]

The project's distinctive approach places advanced monitoring technology directly in the hands of citizen scientists, including students and community members, empowering them to collect environmental data while simultaneously raising awareness about climate change impacts.[^2] By combining innovative meteorological monitoring systems, smart mobility tools, and nature-based solutions, the Living Lab generates real-time environmental data that informs both policy decisions and behavioral change.[^1] The initiative is particularly significant given Milan's status as one of Europe's most air-polluted metropolitan areas, where air quality frequently exceeds European Union limits, creating substantial public health risks.[^3] Rather than imposing top-down solutions, the I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab embodies a participatory governance model that recognizes citizens as active agents in climate action, fostering inclusive deliberations and co-creating context-specific recommendations for sustainable urban development.[^3] This approach aligns with Milan's broader Sustainability Strategy and Air and Climate Plan (PAC), adopted in 2021, which commits the city to achieving carbon neutrality by 2050 while maintaining social equity and inclusion as central principles.[^3]

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:** Milan is Italy's second-largest metropolitan area and one of Europe's most economically dynamic cities, functioning as a global hub for fashion, design, finance, and manufacturing.[^4] With a metropolitan population exceeding 1.3 million residents in the city proper and approximately 3.2 million in the greater metropolitan area, Milan represents a dense, economically productive urban center characterized by high-intensity commercial and residential development. The city's economic base traditionally centered on industrial manufacturing, though it has undergone significant tertiary sector expansion in recent decades, with fashion, design, and financial services now dominating the economic landscape.[^4] Geographically, Milan sits in the Padana Plain in northern Italy, a region characterized by limited natural air circulation and frequent atmospheric stagnation, particularly during winter months. This geographic position, combined with high population density and intensive vehicle traffic, creates chronic air quality challenges that exceed European air quality standards.[^3]

Milan's position within Italy's urban hierarchy is paramount—it functions as the national economic capital and a major European metropolitan center. The city has increasingly positioned itself as a leader in urban sustainability and innovation, particularly following its hosting of Expo 2015, which catalyzed numerous urban regeneration projects and sustainability initiatives.[^4] Within the European urban system, Milan represents a "Global City" with significant influence over regional and continental development patterns.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The I-CHANGE Living Lab operates across multiple neighborhoods and districts throughout Milan, though specific focus areas include inner-city districts with high school concentrations and areas experiencing significant air pollution and traffic congestion. Milan's neighborhoods exhibit considerable heterogeneity in terms of historical development, socioeconomic composition, and environmental conditions. The city's structure reflects historical patterns of industrial development, with older industrial districts now undergoing regeneration and transformation into mixed-use, innovation-focused neighborhoods.[^5] 

Current land use composition in Milan reflects a complex mixture of residential areas, commercial districts, industrial zones (many now transitioning to other uses), and limited green space. The city faces significant green space deficiency compared to other major European cities, with green infrastructure comprising a lower percentage of total urban area than northern European comparatives. Many neighborhoods experience high population density with limited public green space access, contributing to urban heat island effects and reduced quality of life.

Demographic profiles vary significantly across Milan's neighborhoods. Central and northern districts tend to have higher income levels and more established residents, while southern and western districts often exhibit greater socioeconomic diversity, with larger immigrant populations and more economically vulnerable households. Age distribution reflects typical metropolitan patterns, with significant youth populations in certain districts and aging populations in others. This demographic diversity creates varied stakeholder interests and engagement capacities for sustainability initiatives.

Existing infrastructure quality shows considerable variation. Central districts feature well-developed public transportation networks, while peripheral areas often have less comprehensive transit access. Schools throughout the city vary in their technical capacity and resources for participating in citizen science initiatives. Environmental conditions across Milan are characterized by chronic air quality challenges, with particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) frequently exceeding European standards, particularly in districts with high traffic volume and limited air circulation.[^3]

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:** Milan faced multiple interconnected environmental and sustainability challenges that motivated the I-CHANGE Living Lab initiative.[^3] Most prominently, the city experienced persistent air pollution exceeding European Union limits, with air quality standards regularly violated and causing documented public health impacts. Particulate matter concentrations near schools and in high-traffic areas created particular concern for vulnerable populations, especially children whose respiratory systems are still developing. The city's transportation system remained heavily automobile-dependent, generating substantial greenhouse gas emissions and contributing to air quality degradation. Energy efficiency in Milan's building stock remained inadequate, with many older residential and commercial buildings characterized by poor insulation, inefficient heating systems, and high energy consumption. Urban biodiversity had declined due to limited green space, habitat fragmentation, and intensive development patterns. Additionally, climate change impacts—including increasing summer temperatures, urban heat island effects, and extreme weather events—posed growing risks to urban residents and infrastructure.[^3]

Beyond these environmental challenges, Milan faced a more fundamental governance challenge: the need to engage citizens meaningfully in climate action and sustainability planning. Traditional top-down policy approaches had not generated sufficient behavioral change or community support for ambitious sustainability objectives. There existed a significant knowledge gap among residents regarding climate change causes, local environmental conditions, and available solutions.

**Triggering Events:** Several catalysts prompted Milan's development of the I-CHANGE Living Lab and related sustainability initiatives. Milan's designation as one of 112 European "Mission Cities" for climate neutrality created institutional pressure and funding opportunities for ambitious climate action.[^5] The city's adoption of its Air and Climate Plan (PAC) in 2021 represented a strategic commitment to carbon neutrality by 2050, creating the policy framework within which the Living Lab operates.[^3] International climate commitments, including the European Green Deal and Paris Agreement obligations, provided broader policy momentum. Additionally, ongoing public health concerns related to air pollution created persistent pressure from health advocates, schools, and environmental organizations for meaningful action.

**Stakeholder Landscape:** Before the I-CHANGE initiative, Milan's sustainability landscape included diverse stakeholders with varying interests and capacities. Municipal government agencies held formal authority over planning and environmental policy but often lacked sufficient technical capacity and community engagement mechanisms for implementing complex sustainability initiatives. Educational institutions (schools and universities) had environmental concerns but limited resources for environmental monitoring and climate education. Community organizations and environmental NGOs advocated for action but operated with limited resources. Private sector actors, including energy companies, transportation providers, and real estate developers, had economic interests that sometimes aligned with and sometimes conflicted with sustainability objectives. Residents, particularly parents concerned about children's health due to air pollution, represented a constituency with strong motivations for environmental improvement but limited access to information and decision-making processes.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:** Milan operates within Italy's multi-level governance system, with authority distributed among the European Union, national government, regional government (Lombardy Region), provincial government (Metropolitan City of Milan), and municipal government. The I-CHANGE Living Lab operates within this complex framework, with European Union research funding providing coordination and methodological guidance, while municipal government provides local implementation support and policy integration.[^2][^3]

**Relevant Policies:** The initiative is embedded within multiple policy frameworks. The European Green Deal establishes the overarching commitment to climate neutrality by 2050 and guides EU funding for climate action initiatives like I-CHANGE.[^2] The EU's Air Quality Directive establishes standards for particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide that Milan frequently violates, creating regulatory pressure for air quality improvement. Italy's National Climate and Energy Plan outlines national commitments to emissions reductions and renewable energy expansion. The Lombardy Region's environmental policies and sustainability strategies provide regional context. Most significantly for Milan, the 2021 Air and Climate Plan (PAC) represents the city's comprehensive sustainability strategy, establishing specific targets for emissions reductions, air quality improvement, sustainable mobility expansion, and circular economy development.[^3] The PAC explicitly incorporates social equity and inclusive participatory governance as central principles, creating policy alignment with the I-CHANGE approach.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** European Union funding mechanisms and research framework agreements facilitate the I-CHANGE initiative by providing financial resources and legitimizing citizen science approaches within academic and policy contexts. Italian environmental regulations establish baseline standards that motivate improvement. However, several institutional barriers exist. Italy's complex multi-level governance creates coordination challenges and potential conflicts between different governmental levels. Municipal budget constraints limit the resources available for sustainability initiatives. Educational institutions face regulatory constraints on curriculum flexibility that can complicate integration of citizen science activities into formal schooling. Data privacy regulations require careful management of citizen-collected information. Transportation and energy systems involve powerful incumbent interests (fossil fuel companies, automobile manufacturers, transit operators) that may resist sustainability transitions.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab pursues multiple, interconnected objectives that operate at different levels of ambition and timeframe.[^1][^2][^3] Immediate operational goals include establishing functional citizen monitoring networks for air quality and traffic patterns, equipping participating schools and community groups with environmental monitoring technology, and generating reliable environmental data at local scales. Medium-term goals encompass raising public awareness about climate change causes and local environmental conditions, fostering behavioral change toward sustainable mobility and energy consumption patterns, and building community capacity for environmental monitoring and advocacy. Long-term strategic goals include contributing to Milan's achievement of carbon neutrality by 2050, reducing air pollution to comply with European Union standards, transitioning transportation systems toward sustainable modes, improving urban energy efficiency, and enhancing urban biodiversity.[^3]

**Vision Statement:** The I-CHANGE initiative envisions Milan as a city where citizens actively participate in understanding and addressing climate change through direct engagement in scientific data collection, collaborative problem-solving, and behavioral transformation.[^2] The vision encompasses a future Milan characterized by cleaner air, sustainable mobility patterns, energy-efficient buildings, thriving urban green space, and inclusive governance processes where residents feel empowered to shape climate action based on ground realities and scientific understanding.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab directly supports and operationalizes Milan's Air and Climate Plan (PAC), which establishes the city's comprehensive sustainability strategy.[^3] The initiative exemplifies the PAC's commitment to participatory governance and social equity by placing citizens at the center of climate action. The Living Lab contributes to the PAC's specific targets for air quality improvement, sustainable mobility expansion, energy efficiency enhancement, and circular economy development. At the European level, the initiative aligns with the European Green Deal's commitment to climate neutrality by 2050 and supports the EU's broader agenda for citizen engagement in climate action.[^2] Milan's designation as a Mission City reinforces this alignment and creates institutional support for the initiative.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab operates on a sophisticated theory of change that recognizes multiple pathways from intervention activities to intended outcomes.[^2] The core logic posits that providing citizens with direct access to environmental monitoring technology and scientific data creates several reinforcing effects: (1) direct data collection generates accurate environmental information at local scales, filling knowledge gaps and enabling evidence-based policy decisions; (2) the act of participating in data collection raises individual awareness about environmental conditions and personal behavioral impacts; (3) access to real-time environmental data creates psychological motivation for behavioral change by making abstract climate issues concrete and locally relevant; (4) community-based data collection fosters social connections and collective efficacy, strengthening community networks and civic engagement; (5) citizen-generated data carries greater legitimacy and persuasive power within local communities than externally-produced information, enhancing receptiveness to sustainability messages; (6) participatory processes build trust in municipal government and create social license for ambitious climate policies; and (7) accumulated citizen data and community insights inform more effective, contextually-appropriate policy design.[^2][^3]

**Target Beneficiaries:** The initiative targets multiple beneficiary groups with different intended benefits. Students and school staff benefit from enhanced environmental literacy, improved health from reduced air pollution, and empowerment through participation in meaningful scientific work. Families benefit from healthier urban environments and information enabling behavioral change toward sustainable practices. Broader Milan residents benefit from improved air quality, reduced traffic congestion, enhanced urban green space, and improved quality of life. Municipal government benefits from citizen-generated data, enhanced policy effectiveness, and strengthened community support for sustainability initiatives. Future generations benefit from climate change mitigation and adaptation investments made possible by current action.

**Innovation Elements:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab incorporates several innovative elements that distinguish it from conventional urban sustainability initiatives.[^2] The "quadruple helix" governance model connecting government, academia, industry, and civil society represents an innovative institutional arrangement that transcends traditional public-private partnership models. Placing advanced environmental monitoring technology directly in citizens' hands represents a democratization of scientific practice and data generation. The integration of citizen science with formal education systems creates innovation in pedagogical approaches. The use of digital dashboards and mobile applications (including the Challenge Yeti app) to make environmental data accessible and actionable represents technological innovation in citizen engagement. The explicit focus on behavioral change through gamification and reward mechanisms incorporates insights from behavioral economics and psychology into urban sustainability practice. The commitment to social equity and inclusive participation represents a normative innovation in how sustainability transitions are governed.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers:** Milan's designation as a European Mission City created formal political commitment to ambitious climate action and provided access to significant European Union funding.[^5][^3] Political leadership at the municipal level, particularly through the Urban Resilience Department, prioritized climate action and citizen engagement as policy priorities. Electoral cycles and political competition created incentives for visible, measurable progress on sustainability issues that resonate with constituents. International climate commitments and EU policy frameworks created external political pressure for ambitious local action.

**Economic Drivers:** Economic interests in Milan's sustainability transition include opportunities for green technology companies, renewable energy providers, sustainable transportation businesses, and energy efficiency retrofit contractors. The circular economy represents an economic opportunity for waste reduction and resource efficiency innovations.[^4] EU funding for climate action creates financial incentives for project development. Long-term economic concerns about climate change impacts on urban infrastructure, property values, and business continuity motivate preventive investment in resilience and mitigation.

**Social Drivers:** Public health concerns about air pollution, particularly impacts on children's respiratory health, created strong social motivation for air quality improvement.[^3] Environmental justice considerations regarding unequal distribution of pollution burdens across neighborhoods motivated equity-focused approaches. Community organizations and environmental advocates mobilized around sustainability issues, creating social pressure for municipal action. Growing public concern about climate change, particularly among younger generations, created social demand for meaningful action. Quality of life considerations—including desires for more walkable neighborhoods, reduced traffic, cleaner air, and enhanced green space—motivated resident support for sustainability initiatives.

**Environmental Drivers:** Milan's chronic violation of European Union air quality standards created regulatory pressure and environmental necessity for air quality improvement.[^3] Climate change impacts, including increasing summer temperatures and extreme weather events, created urgency around emissions reductions and adaptation. Urban heat island effects and limited green space created environmental challenges motivating nature-based solutions. Biodiversity decline and habitat loss motivated urban greening initiatives. Resource constraints and sustainability concerns motivated energy efficiency and circular economy approaches.

**Technological Drivers:** Advances in environmental monitoring technology, including affordable air quality sensors (Smart Citizen Kits) and meteorological monitoring systems (MeteoTrackers), made citizen science feasible at scale.[^2] Development of user-friendly digital platforms and mobile applications enabled data accessibility and engagement. Smart city technologies and IoT (Internet of Things) systems created opportunities for integrated environmental management. Artificial intelligence and machine learning capabilities enabled sophisticated data analysis and pattern recognition.[^4]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab draws on multiple conceptual frameworks that inform its design philosophy. The "living lab" concept, originating from innovation management literature, emphasizes real-world experimentation, iterative learning, and user co-creation within specific geographic and social contexts.[^2] This approach rejects traditional linear models of technology development and policy implementation in favor of adaptive, participatory processes. Citizen science frameworks emphasize the capacity of non-professional scientists to contribute meaningful data to scientific understanding while simultaneously building scientific literacy and environmental awareness.[^2] The quadruple helix model recognizes that addressing complex sustainability challenges requires integrated participation from government, academia, industry, and civil society, each bringing distinct capacities and perspectives.[^2]

The initiative also draws on behavioral economics and psychology, incorporating insights about how information, social norms, and incentive structures influence behavioral change.[^2] The use of gamification and reward mechanisms in the Challenge Yeti app reflects this theoretical grounding. Additionally, the project embodies principles of environmental justice and inclusive governance, recognizing that sustainability transitions must address equity concerns and incorporate marginalized voices in decision-making processes.[^3]

**Physical/Spatial Design:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab does not involve traditional physical infrastructure development in the sense of constructing new buildings or neighborhoods. Rather, it operates through distributed networks of monitoring stations, school facilities, and community spaces throughout Milan's neighborhoods. Air quality monitoring equipment is deployed at strategic locations including schools, high-traffic areas, and residential neighborhoods to capture spatially distributed environmental data.[^1] Traffic counting equipment is positioned near schools and along major transportation corridors to track mobility patterns.[^2] Digital infrastructure—including cloud-based data platforms, mobile applications, and web-based dashboards—represents the primary technological spatial intervention, creating virtual spaces where data is aggregated, analyzed, and made accessible to diverse audiences.

The project operates through existing institutional spaces (schools, municipal offices, community centers) rather than constructing new dedicated facilities. This approach minimizes capital requirements while embedding the initiative within established community infrastructure and social networks.

**Scope and Phasing:** The I-CHANGE project operates across eight Living Labs in different European cities (Amsterdam, Barcelona, Bologna, Dublin, Genoa, Hasselt, Jerusalem, and Ouagadougou), with Milan as one of the primary European urban sites.[^2] The Milan-specific initiative began in 2021 as part of the broader multi-year project. Implementation follows a phased approach beginning with establishment of monitoring networks and baseline data collection, progressing through community engagement and behavioral intervention campaigns, and continuing with ongoing monitoring, adaptation, and scaling of successful approaches. The project timeline extends through at least 2025, with expectations for continued evolution and integration into permanent municipal sustainability systems.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab employs a suite of complementary technologies designed to enable citizen participation in environmental monitoring and data analysis.[^2] The MeteoTracker represents an advanced meteorological monitoring device that collects detailed atmospheric data including temperature, humidity, pressure, wind speed and direction, and precipitation. More than 100 MeteoTrackers were deployed across the I-CHANGE Living Labs, with significant deployment in Milan.[^2] The Smart Citizen Kit comprises an integrated sensor package measuring air quality parameters including particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and other pollutants, along with temperature and humidity data. Over 50 Smart Citizen Kits were deployed across the project sites.[^2] Digital photography equipment enables citizens to document environmental conditions and urban landscapes, contributing visual data to environmental monitoring efforts.

The Environmental Impact Hub represents a central data platform that collects, integrates, and makes accessible environmental data from across the Living Labs and citizen monitoring networks.[^2] This platform enables data sharing among researchers, policymakers, and the public, supporting evidence-based decision-making. The I-CHANGE Dashboard provides visualization and interpretation of environmental data in formats accessible to non-technical audiences, supporting public understanding and awareness-raising.[^2] The Challenge Yeti mobile application represents a gamified tool for personal carbon footprint tracking and behavioral change, incorporating reward mechanisms and social comparison features to motivate sustainable lifestyle choices.[^2]

**Innovation Type:** The technological innovations employed span multiple categories. Hardware innovations include the development and deployment of affordable, user-friendly environmental monitoring devices (Smart Citizen Kits, MeteoTrackers) that enable distributed citizen science. Software innovations include the development of data platforms, dashboards, and mobile applications that make environmental data accessible and actionable. Social innovations include the quadruple helix governance model, participatory research methodologies, and integration of citizen science with formal education systems. Financial innovations include EU funding mechanisms specifically designed to support citizen engagement in climate action research. Process innovations include methodologies for participatory data collection, community engagement protocols, and adaptive management approaches that incorporate learning from implementation experience.

**Integration Approach:** The technologies are integrated into Milan's existing urban systems and institutional structures rather than operating as isolated pilot projects. Environmental monitoring networks are positioned to complement and enhance municipal environmental management systems. Data generated through citizen monitoring feeds into municipal decision-making processes, with particular emphasis on school-level decisions about transportation and air quality management.[^2] Digital platforms are designed for interoperability with existing municipal information systems and European environmental data infrastructure. Educational integration embeds citizen science activities into school curricula and extracurricular programs, connecting environmental monitoring to formal learning objectives. Integration with municipal sustainability planning ensures that citizen-generated insights inform implementation of the Air and Climate Plan.[^3]

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:** The I-CHANGE project operates through a European research consortium model rather than traditional procurement processes. The University of Bologna coordinates the project, with participating universities and research institutions in each Living Lab city responsible for local implementation.[^2] Equipment procurement follows standard research project protocols, with competitive bidding for technology providers and service contracts. Schools and community organizations participate as implementation partners through formal agreements rather than commercial contracts, reflecting the collaborative nature of the initiative.

**Stakeholder Engagement:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab employs sophisticated stakeholder engagement strategies designed to build broad participation and ownership.[^2] Municipal government agencies are engaged as formal partners in governance structures, with representation in project steering committees and integration of project activities into official municipal programs. Schools are engaged as primary implementation sites, with formal partnerships establishing roles and responsibilities for teachers, students, and administrative staff. Community organizations and environmental NGOs are engaged as outreach partners and advocates. Residents are engaged through multiple pathways including direct participation in monitoring activities, attendance at community events and workshops, engagement with digital platforms and mobile applications, and participation in focus groups and surveys assessing project impacts.

**Participatory Elements:** Participatory elements are central to the I-CHANGE methodology.[^2] Citizens are not merely data subjects but active co-creators of knowledge, with direct responsibility for data collection and analysis. Community input shapes project priorities and implementation approaches through participatory planning processes. Schools participate in curriculum co-design, integrating citizen science activities into educational programs in ways aligned with pedagogical objectives. Residents contribute to interpretation of environmental data and identification of priority environmental concerns. Feedback mechanisms enable continuous adaptation of project activities based on participant input and emerging learning.

**Project Management:** The project employs collaborative governance structures and adaptive management approaches. A project steering committee brings together representatives from research institutions, municipal government, schools, and community organizations to provide strategic direction and oversight. Working groups focused on specific thematic areas (air quality, sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, biodiversity) coordinate implementation activities. Regular monitoring and evaluation processes track progress toward objectives and identify necessary adaptations. Knowledge-sharing mechanisms, including workshops, webinars, and publications, facilitate learning across the eight Living Lab sites and broader dissemination of insights.

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:** The I-CHANGE project, as a European Union research and innovation action, operates with substantial financial resources, though specific budget figures for the Milan component are not detailed in available documentation. The project's multi-year timeline, involvement of multiple universities and research institutions, deployment of advanced monitoring equipment, and development of digital platforms suggest significant investment. Equipment costs include procurement of MeteoTrackers, Smart Citizen Kits, traffic counters, and related monitoring devices. Personnel costs support researchers, project coordinators, and community engagement specialists. Technology development costs support platform design, software development, and application development. Training and capacity-building costs support workshops and educational programs for participants. Dissemination and knowledge-sharing costs support publications, conferences, and communication activities.

**Funding Mix:** European Union funding provides the primary financial source through research and innovation action mechanisms.[^2] National and regional funding from Italian sources likely supports local implementation activities. Institutional contributions from the University of Bologna and participating schools represent in-kind support. Municipal government contributions, including staff time and facility access, represent additional in-kind support.

**Revenue Mechanisms:** The I-CHANGE project does not employ traditional revenue-generating mechanisms. Rather, it operates as a publicly-funded research initiative with the expectation that benefits accrue to society broadly rather than generating direct financial returns. However, the project's approach to citizen engagement and data generation may create value for municipal government in the form of enhanced policy effectiveness and community support, representing indirect benefits.

**Financial Sustainability:** Long-term financial sustainability represents a significant consideration for the I-CHANGE initiative. The project's current funding is time-limited, extending through the research grant period. Sustainability of the initiative beyond this period depends on integration into permanent municipal systems and identification of ongoing funding sources. Potential approaches include integration of citizen monitoring networks into municipal environmental management systems with dedicated municipal budgets, continued EU funding for related initiatives, development of partnerships with private sector actors (technology companies, energy providers) with interests in sustainability, and integration of citizen science activities into school curricula with associated educational budgets. The project's design, which emphasizes integration with existing institutions rather than creating parallel structures, supports long-term sustainability by embedding activities within permanent institutional frameworks.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges:** Maintaining accuracy and reliability of distributed sensor networks represents a significant technical challenge. Air quality sensors require regular calibration and maintenance to ensure data quality, yet distributed deployment across schools and community sites complicates quality assurance processes. Environmental monitoring equipment operates in outdoor conditions subject to weather extremes, dust, and other environmental stressors that can degrade sensor performance. Data transmission and integration from distributed monitoring devices into central platforms requires robust technological infrastructure and cybersecurity measures. Integration of diverse data sources (meteorological data, traffic counts, air quality measurements) into coherent analytical frameworks requires sophisticated data management and quality control protocols.

**Financial Challenges:** Securing sustained funding for long-term operations represents a critical challenge. The project's current funding is time-limited through the European research grant period, with uncertain prospects for continuation beyond this timeframe. Equipment replacement and maintenance costs accumulate over time, creating ongoing financial requirements. Expansion of monitoring networks to cover additional neighborhoods or schools requires additional investment. Competition for limited public resources between the I-CHANGE initiative and other municipal priorities creates budgetary pressures.

**Social and Political Challenges:** Engaging diverse populations in citizen science activities presents significant challenges. Participation rates may decline over time as initial enthusiasm wanes and the novelty of the initiative fades. Ensuring representative participation across different socioeconomic, ethnic, and age groups requires deliberate outreach and removal of participation barriers. Language barriers may limit engagement of non-Italian speaking residents. Skepticism about government intentions or concerns about data privacy may deter participation. Political changes and shifts in municipal leadership priorities could reduce support for the initiative. Opposition from automobile industry interests or fossil fuel companies threatened by sustainability transitions could create political obstacles.

**Administrative Challenges:** Coordination across multiple institutional actors (universities, municipal government, schools, community organizations) involves complex administrative arrangements and potential conflicts of interest or priority. Bureaucratic processes in educational institutions and municipal government can slow implementation and create delays. Capacity constraints within schools and community organizations may limit their ability to effectively implement project activities. Ensuring accountability and quality standards across distributed implementation sites requires robust monitoring and evaluation systems.

**Environmental Challenges:** Climate change impacts, including extreme weather events, may disrupt monitoring equipment or implementation activities. Urban heat waves and other climate extremes may create immediate public health emergencies that divert attention and resources from longer-term sustainability initiatives. Seasonal variations in air quality (with winter months typically showing worse air quality in Milan) create temporal variations in environmental conditions that complicate interpretation of data and assessment of intervention impacts.

#### **Adaptive Responses**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:** The I-CHANGE project employs adaptive management methodologies that enable continuous learning and modification based on implementation experience.[^2] Regular monitoring and evaluation processes identify emerging challenges and track progress toward objectives. Participatory reflection sessions with implementers and participants generate insights about what is working and what requires modification. The project's multi-site structure (eight Living Labs across Europe) creates opportunities for cross-site learning, with successful approaches from one site adapted and adopted in others. Technical support from the University of Bologna and project partners provides expertise for addressing technical challenges. Collaborative problem-solving processes bring together diverse stakeholders to collectively identify solutions to emerging obstacles.

**Design Modifications:** Based on implementation experience, the project has adapted its approaches to address emerging challenges. For example, the Challenge Yeti application has undergone iterative refinement based on user feedback and engagement data, with modifications to reward mechanisms and interface design to enhance usability and motivation. Data platform interfaces have been simplified and improved based on user testing with non-technical audiences, making environmental data more accessible and understandable. Engagement strategies have been modified to improve participation among underrepresented populations, with increased outreach to immigrant communities and lower-income neighborhoods. Training programs for school staff and community monitors have been expanded and refined based on feedback about knowledge gaps and support needs.

**Lessons Learned During Implementation:** Several important insights have emerged from the I-CHANGE implementation experience.[^2] The project has learned that citizen science activities are most effective when closely integrated with formal education and institutional decision-making rather than operating as isolated activities. Participation is higher when citizens perceive direct connection between their data collection efforts and concrete policy or management decisions. Technology alone is insufficient for driving behavioral change; social factors, community norms, and institutional support are equally important. Diverse stakeholder participation requires deliberate attention to equity and inclusion, with specific outreach and accommodation strategies needed to engage marginalized populations. Long-term sustainability requires early attention to integration with permanent institutional structures rather than relying on temporary project funding. Data quality and scientific rigor remain important even in citizen science contexts, requiring training, quality assurance protocols, and professional oversight.

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

**Physical Outputs:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab has generated tangible physical outputs in the form of deployed environmental monitoring infrastructure. Over 100 MeteoTrackers and 50 Smart Citizen Kits were deployed across the I-CHANGE project sites, with significant deployment in Milan and other European cities.[^2] Traffic counting equipment has been installed at strategic locations near schools and along major transportation corridors. This monitoring infrastructure remains in place and continues generating environmental data, creating a persistent physical presence of the initiative in Milan's urban landscape.

**Service Delivery:** The project has established new environmental monitoring and data analysis services that did not previously exist at comparable scales. Real-time air quality data is now available to residents, schools, and municipal decision-makers through digital platforms and mobile applications. Environmental education services have been expanded through integration of citizen science activities into school curricula. Community engagement services, including workshops, training programs, and participatory planning processes, represent new institutional capacity for sustainability governance.

**Quantitative Metrics:** More than 1,000 climate change-related photographs were collected by citizen scientists across the I-CHANGE project sites.[^2] Thousands of air quality, meteorological, and traffic measurements have been generated through distributed monitoring networks. Hundreds of students and community members have participated directly in data collection and analysis activities. Digital platforms and applications have been accessed by thousands of users seeking environmental information.

#### **Social Impacts**

**Community Benefits:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab has generated multiple community benefits related to quality of life, health outcomes, and social cohesion.[^2] Improved air quality resulting from behavioral changes toward sustainable mobility and policy responses to citizen-generated data directly benefits community health, particularly for vulnerable populations including children and elderly residents with respiratory sensitivities. Enhanced environmental awareness among students and families creates psychological and behavioral benefits through increased sense of agency and empowerment regarding climate action. Expanded green space and urban greening initiatives, supported by project activities, improve quality of life through aesthetic, recreational, and psychological benefits. Reduced traffic congestion resulting from increased active transportation (walking and cycling) improves community safety, reduces noise pollution, and enhances neighborhood livability.

**Equity Outcomes:** The I-CHANGE project has explicitly prioritized equity and inclusion in its design and implementation, though outcomes in this dimension remain mixed and ongoing.[^3] The project's commitment to participatory governance and social equity aims to ensure that benefits of sustainability transitions are equitably distributed across different social groups. Targeted outreach to lower-income neighborhoods and immigrant communities seeks to ensure that environmental improvements benefit all residents rather than concentrating benefits among affluent populations. However, risks of gentrification and displacement remain present, as improved environmental quality and neighborhood amenities may attract investment and new residents, potentially driving up housing costs and displacing existing residents. The project's integration with municipal housing and social policy aims to mitigate these risks through affordability protections and community stabilization measures.

**Displacement and Gentrification:** Comparative analysis from other European cities suggests that green urban renewal initiatives can inadvertently trigger gentrification processes, with improved environmental quality and neighborhood amenities attracting investment and new residents, ultimately displacing lower-income existing residents.[^6] Vienna's experience demonstrates that integration of green urban renewal with strong municipal housing policies and affordability protections can mitigate gentrification risks, while Copenhagen's experience shows that without such protections, green renewal can concentrate benefits among higher-income populations.[^6] Milan's approach, which explicitly incorporates social equity as a central principle of the Air and Climate Plan and the I-CHANGE initiative, aims to learn from these comparative experiences and implement safeguards against displacement.[^3] However, the risk remains that environmental improvements in lower-income neighborhoods could trigger market pressures for gentrification unless accompanied by robust affordable housing protections and community stabilization policies.

**Social Capital:** The I-CHANGE initiative has generated increases in social capital through multiple mechanisms.[^2] Direct participation in citizen science activities creates social connections among participants, building community networks and relationships. Participatory governance processes create opportunities for residents to engage with municipal officials and researchers, potentially building trust and social cohesion. Community events, workshops, and training programs create gathering spaces that strengthen community bonds. However, risks exist that participation may skew toward already-engaged populations with higher social capital, potentially widening inequalities in political voice and influence.

**Cultural Impacts:** The I-CHANGE initiative contributes to cultural shifts regarding environmental consciousness and climate responsibility. Integration of environmental monitoring and sustainability into school curricula shapes cultural values among younger generations, potentially creating lasting changes in environmental attitudes and behaviors. Public communication about environmental data and climate impacts contributes to normalization of climate consciousness in everyday urban life. However, cultural resistance from populations with vested interests in unsustainable practices (automobile owners, fossil fuel industry workers) may create countervailing cultural dynamics.

#### **Economic Impacts**

**Job Creation:** The I-CHANGE initiative has generated direct employment through project staff positions (researchers, project coordinators, community engagement specialists) and indirect employment through contracts with technology providers, equipment manufacturers, and service providers.[^2] Training and capacity-building activities have created employment opportunities for educators and trainers. However, these employment effects are primarily concentrated in higher-skill, higher-wage positions (research and technical roles) rather than creating broad-based employment opportunities for lower-skilled workers.

**Economic Activity:** The initiative stimulates economic activity in the green technology and environmental monitoring sectors through procurement of equipment and services. Integration of sustainability into urban development and real estate markets may stimulate investment in green building retrofits, renewable energy installations, and sustainable transportation infrastructure. However, quantification of these economic effects remains limited in available documentation.

**Property Values:** Improved environmental quality and neighborhood amenities resulting from the I-CHANGE initiative may increase property values in participating neighborhoods, creating wealth effects for property owners. However, this outcome carries the risk of triggering gentrification and displacement of lower-income residents, as discussed above. The project's integration with affordable housing policies aims to ensure that property value increases do not result in displacement.

**Cost Savings:** Behavioral changes toward sustainable mobility (increased walking and cycling, reduced automobile use) generate cost savings for households through reduced transportation expenses. Energy efficiency improvements in buildings reduce energy costs for residents and building owners. Reduced traffic congestion generates economic benefits through time savings and reduced vehicle operating costs. However, quantification of these cost savings remains limited in available documentation.

**Economic Inclusion:** The project's commitment to inclusive participation and equitable benefit distribution aims to ensure that economic benefits reach diverse populations rather than concentrating among affluent groups. However, risks exist that economic benefits may skew toward populations with higher initial capacity to adopt sustainable behaviors (higher-income households with resources to invest in energy efficiency, bicycles, or transit passes). Targeted outreach and support for lower-income populations aims to mitigate these risks.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

**Climate and Emissions:** The I-CHANGE initiative contributes to greenhouse gas emissions reductions through multiple mechanisms.[^2] Behavioral changes toward sustainable mobility (increased walking, cycling, and public transit use; reduced automobile use) directly reduce transportation-related emissions. Energy efficiency improvements in buildings reduce energy consumption and associated emissions. Urban greening and nature-based solutions support carbon sequestration and climate adaptation. However, quantification of total emissions reductions attributable to the I-CHANGE initiative remains limited in available documentation. Given the initiative's relatively recent launch (2021) and ongoing implementation, long-term emissions impacts are still emerging.

**Resource Management:** The project contributes to improved resource management through enhanced monitoring of energy and water consumption and behavioral changes toward conservation. Integration of smart mobility tools and sustainable transportation options reduces resource consumption associated with private automobile use. Nature-based solutions for urban cooling and stormwater management improve water resource management. However, comprehensive quantification of resource efficiency improvements remains limited.

**Environmental Quality:** Air quality improvements represent the most directly measurable environmental impact of the I-CHANGE initiative. Citizen-generated air quality data documents baseline conditions and tracks changes over time. Behavioral changes toward sustainable mobility and policy responses to citizen-generated data contribute to reduced air pollution, particularly near schools and in high-traffic areas.[^2] Reduced traffic congestion contributes to noise pollution reduction. Expanded green space and urban greening initiatives improve urban environmental quality through aesthetic, ecological, and climatic benefits.

**Waste and Pollution:** The I-CHANGE initiative addresses pollution reduction, particularly air pollution, as a central objective. Participation in the initiative's behavioral change programs (Challenge Yeti app, sustainable mobility campaigns) encourages waste reduction and pollution prevention behaviors. Integration with Milan's circular economy initiatives supports waste reduction and pollution prevention. However, comprehensive quantification of pollution reduction outcomes remains limited in available documentation.

**Sustainability Metrics:** The I-CHANGE initiative operates within frameworks of established environmental standards and sustainability metrics. Air quality performance is measured against European Union air quality standards (PM10, PM2.5, NO₂ limits).[^3] Energy performance is measured against building energy efficiency standards and carbon emissions targets. Sustainable mobility performance is measured through modal share metrics (percentage of trips by walking, cycling, public transit, private automobile). Urban green space is measured through percentage of city area covered by green infrastructure. However, comprehensive sustainability certification or formal environmental management system certification is not documented in available sources.

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

**Institutional Capacity:** The I-CHANGE initiative has strengthened institutional capacity for environmental monitoring and sustainability governance within Milan. Municipal government agencies have developed enhanced capacity for environmental data collection, analysis, and utilization in decision-making. Schools have developed new capacity for environmental education and citizen science implementation. Community organizations have enhanced capacity for environmental advocacy and community engagement. Universities have expanded capacity for participatory research and community-engaged scholarship.[^2]

**Policy Influence:** The I-CHANGE initiative has influenced Milan's sustainability policy through multiple mechanisms. Citizen-generated environmental data informs implementation of the Air and Climate Plan by providing local-scale environmental information that guides policy priorities and intervention design.[^3] Participatory governance processes create social license for ambitious sustainability policies by building public understanding and support. The initiative's success in engaging citizens in sustainability action demonstrates feasibility of participatory approaches, potentially influencing adoption of similar methodologies in other policy domains.

**Transparency and Accountability:** The I-CHANGE initiative enhances governance transparency by making environmental data publicly accessible through digital platforms and mobile applications.[^2] Participatory governance processes create accountability mechanisms by requiring municipal officials to respond to citizen input and justify policy decisions based on evidence. However, questions remain about accountability for outcomes, particularly regarding whether the initiative achieves its stated environmental and social objectives.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

**Success Metrics:** Assessing the I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab's performance against objectives requires consideration of the initiative's multiple, sometimes competing goals and the relatively recent stage of implementation. Regarding the objective of establishing functional citizen monitoring networks, the initiative has succeeded in deploying environmental monitoring equipment and recruiting and training citizen monitors, with over 100 MeteoTrackers and 50 Smart Citizen Kits deployed across project sites.[^2] Regarding the objective of raising public awareness about climate change and local environmental conditions, the initiative has achieved measurable success, with initial evaluation results indicating "heightened public awareness and acceptance of the city's sustainability policies due to improved understanding of climate complexities and local governance processes."[^3]

Regarding the objective of fostering behavioral change toward sustainable mobility and energy conservation, preliminary evidence suggests positive outcomes, with the initiative documenting "increased active travel rates" and "measured decrease in particulate matter near schools."[^2] However, comprehensive assessment of behavior change persistence and scale remains limited. Regarding the objective of contributing to Milan's achievement of carbon neutrality by 2050, it is premature to assess long-term success, as the initiative is still in relatively early implementation stages. However, the initiative's contribution to the broader portfolio of sustainability actions supporting the Air and Climate Plan is evident.

**Unintended Consequences:** Several unintended consequences of the I-CHANGE initiative warrant attention. The initiative's success in raising environmental awareness may create pressure for rapid policy changes that outpace technical or institutional capacity for implementation, potentially creating backlash if expectations are not met. Enhanced environmental monitoring and data availability may create new conflicts between different stakeholder groups regarding environmental priorities and resource allocation. Participation in citizen science activities may skew toward populations with higher education and higher social capital, potentially amplifying existing inequalities in political voice and influence. Environmental improvements in lower-income neighborhoods may trigger gentrification and displacement pressures, despite project efforts to prevent this outcome.

**Long-term Viability:** Assessment of the I-CHANGE initiative's long-term viability depends on resolution of several critical factors. Financial sustainability requires identification of ongoing funding sources beyond the current European research grant period. Integration into permanent municipal systems and school curricula requires institutional commitment and budget allocation. Sustaining citizen participation requires ongoing engagement strategies and demonstrated connection between participation and concrete outcomes. Political support from municipal leadership must persist through electoral cycles and potential changes in political priorities. The initiative's design, which emphasizes integration with existing institutions rather than creating parallel structures, supports long-term viability by embedding activities within permanent institutional frameworks.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab incorporates elements that are highly transferable across different urban contexts and elements that are more context-specific. The core methodology of citizen science combined with participatory governance is broadly applicable across diverse urban settings, as demonstrated by the I-CHANGE project's successful implementation across eight different European and African cities (Amsterdam, Barcelona, Bologna, Dublin, Genoa, Hasselt, Jerusalem, and Ouagadougou).[^2] The specific technologies employed (MeteoTrackers, Smart Citizen Kits, digital platforms) are designed for broad applicability across geographic contexts, though local adaptation regarding specific environmental parameters monitored may be necessary based on local environmental priorities.

However, several elements of the Milan implementation are context-specific and would require adaptation in other settings. Milan's particular air pollution challenges, driven by geographic location in the Padana Plain with limited air circulation, specific industrial and transportation patterns, and specific regulatory framework, create particular environmental conditions that shape the initiative's focus and design.[^3] Milan's relatively strong municipal governance capacity, institutional resources, and existing commitment to sustainability (reflected in the Air and Climate Plan) create enabling conditions that may not exist in all urban contexts. Milan's relatively high levels of education and environmental awareness among certain populations create different baseline conditions than cities with lower environmental consciousness. The Italian regulatory and policy environment, including EU membership and specific Italian environmental policies, creates specific governance contexts that differ from other national contexts.

**Critical Success Factors:** Several elements appear critical to the I-CHANGE initiative's success and would be necessary for successful replication in other contexts. Strong municipal commitment to sustainability and willingness to integrate citizen-generated data into decision-making processes is essential. Adequate funding and institutional resources to support ongoing implementation and maintenance of monitoring networks are necessary. Educational institutions (schools, universities) willing and able to participate as implementation partners are important. Existing or nascent community environmental consciousness and willingness to engage in citizen science activities facilitates implementation. Technical capacity within municipal government and partner institutions to manage data systems and analytical processes is necessary. Participatory governance capacity and willingness among municipal officials to engage citizens in decision-making processes is important. Clear connection between citizen monitoring activities and concrete policy or management outcomes is critical for sustaining participation and demonstrating value.

**Scalability:** The I-CHANGE initiative demonstrates scalability across multiple dimensions. Geographic scalability is demonstrated through implementation across eight different cities with different geographic, economic, and social contexts. The methodology has been successfully adapted to different environmental priorities (air quality in Bologna and Milan, traffic patterns in Dublin, flooding and waste management in Ouagadougou).[^2] Scaling within cities is feasible, with expansion of monitoring networks to additional neighborhoods and schools technically and organizationally possible. Scaling to different populations is achievable through targeted outreach and adaptation of engagement strategies for different demographic groups. Scaling to different sectors (from schools to businesses, residential buildings, municipal operations) is feasible, with similar monitoring and behavioral change approaches applicable across different institutional contexts.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

**Similar Initiatives:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab shares methodological and conceptual similarities with other European urban sustainability initiatives. The Bologna Living Lab, also part of the I-CHANGE project, focuses on similar environmental issues (air quality, energy efficiency, sustainable transport, biodiversity) with comparable methodologies.[^1] Vienna's green urban renewal initiatives share commitments to integrating environmental sustainability with social equity and affordable housing protection, though Vienna's approach emphasizes municipal housing provision rather than citizen science.[^6] Copenhagen's green urban renewal initiatives share focus on environmental quality improvements and sustainable mobility, though Copenhagen's approach has been less explicitly focused on social equity and has resulted in greater gentrification pressures than Vienna's approach.[^6] Milan's Permanent Citizens' Assembly on Climate represents a complementary governance innovation focused on participatory deliberation regarding climate policy, operating alongside the I-CHANGE Living Lab.[^3]

**Best Practices:** Several elements of the I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab represent exemplary practice in urban sustainability and citizen engagement. The integration of citizen science with formal education systems, embedding environmental monitoring into school curricula and extracurricular activities, represents best practice in environmental education and creates lasting impacts on student environmental consciousness and behaviors. The quadruple helix governance model, connecting government, academia, industry, and civil society, represents innovative institutional arrangements that transcend traditional governance silos and enable more holistic problem-solving. The explicit commitment to equity and inclusion in participatory governance, with deliberate outreach to marginalized populations and attention to equitable benefit distribution, represents best practice in environmental justice. The integration of behavioral economics insights (gamification, reward mechanisms, social comparison) into sustainability initiatives represents best practice in behavioral change design. The emphasis on real-time, locally-relevant environmental data and transparent communication of environmental conditions represents best practice in environmental transparency and public engagement.

**Cautionary Lessons:** The I-CHANGE initiative also offers cautionary lessons for other cities considering similar approaches. The risk of gentrification and displacement following environmental improvements requires proactive policy responses (affordable housing protections, community stabilization policies) rather than assuming that environmental improvements automatically benefit all residents.[^6] Participation in citizen science and sustainability initiatives may skew toward populations with higher education, higher income, and greater social capital, potentially amplifying existing inequalities in political voice and influence if not deliberately addressed through targeted outreach and participation support. Citizen engagement without clear connection to concrete policy or management outcomes risks creating cynicism and disengagement if citizens perceive that their participation has no actual influence on decisions. Long-term sustainability of citizen science initiatives requires integration into permanent institutional structures and identification of ongoing funding sources rather than relying on temporary project funding. Technical capacity and quality assurance processes are necessary even in citizen science contexts to ensure data reliability and scientific credibility.

#### **Parallels to Green Building Neighborhoods**

**Contextual Similarities:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab shares several contextual similarities with the green building neighborhood (GBN) use case being reviewed. Both initiatives operate within the context of dense European metropolitan areas facing environmental challenges (air pollution, climate change, resource consumption) and seeking to transition toward sustainable urban development. Both initiatives recognize that sustainability transitions require behavioral change among residents and workers, not merely technical or infrastructural changes. Both initiatives incorporate elements of community engagement and participatory governance, recognizing that sustainable development is more effective when residents are actively involved in planning and implementation. Both initiatives address interconnected environmental challenges (air quality, energy efficiency, sustainable mobility, green space) rather than treating environmental issues in isolation. Both initiatives operate within policy frameworks emphasizing carbon neutrality targets and climate action (Milan's Air and Climate Plan, European Green Deal).[^3]

**Methodological Parallels:** The I-CHANGE initiative and GBN initiatives employ complementary methodologies that could be effectively integrated. Both emphasize data-driven decision-making, with environmental monitoring informing policy and management decisions. Both employ technological innovations (smart sensors, digital platforms, mobile applications) to enable environmental monitoring and behavioral change. Both emphasize participatory approaches to sustainability, recognizing that community engagement enhances both effectiveness and legitimacy of sustainability initiatives. Both address multiple environmental and social objectives simultaneously rather than optimizing for single outcomes, recognizing the interconnectedness of sustainability challenges.

**Technological Overlaps:** The I-CHANGE initiative and GBN initiatives employ overlapping technologies that could be effectively integrated. Environmental monitoring technologies (air quality sensors, energy consumption monitors, traffic counters) deployed in GBN contexts could be complemented by distributed citizen science networks employing similar technologies. Digital platforms and mobile applications for environmental data visualization and behavioral change support could serve both GBN residents and broader urban populations. Smart building technologies for energy management and occupant comfort could be complemented by neighborhood-scale environmental monitoring and community engagement platforms.

**Stakeholder Dynamics:** Both the I-CHANGE initiative and GBN initiatives involve complex stakeholder dynamics requiring careful governance. Municipal government agencies hold formal authority and responsibility for urban development but require community engagement and input for legitimacy and effectiveness. Educational institutions (schools, universities) represent important partners for environmental education and research support. Community organizations and environmental NGOs provide advocacy, outreach, and community connections. Private sector actors (developers, technology companies, energy providers) bring resources and innovation capacity but may have profit motives that conflict with equity and sustainability objectives. Residents represent the ultimate beneficiaries and participants in sustainability initiatives but bring diverse interests, capacities, and constraints.

**Transferable Strategies:** Several specific strategies from the I-CHANGE initiative could be effectively transferred to GBN contexts. Integration of environmental monitoring into school curricula and extracurricular activities creates lasting impacts on student environmental consciousness while generating valuable environmental data. Gamification and reward mechanisms for behavioral change (as employed in the Challenge Yeti app) could be adapted to encourage sustainable behaviors among GBN residents (walking, cycling, energy conservation, waste reduction). Participatory governance processes for sustainability planning could engage GBN residents in defining sustainability priorities and monitoring progress toward objectives. Real-time environmental data visualization and transparent communication of environmental conditions could enhance resident awareness and motivation for sustainable behaviors. Community events and workshops could build social capital and community networks within GBN neighborhoods.

**Adaptation Considerations:** Transferring I-CHANGE strategies to GBN contexts would require several adaptations. GBN initiatives typically involve more intensive focus on specific neighborhoods or districts than the I-CHANGE initiative's city-wide approach, allowing for more granular environmental monitoring and community engagement. GBN initiatives typically involve significant physical infrastructure development (building retrofits, green space creation, transportation infrastructure) that complements behavioral change strategies employed in I-CHANGE. GBN initiatives may involve more intensive community displacement risks than the I-CHANGE initiative, requiring stronger proactive policies for affordable housing protection and community stabilization. GBN initiatives may involve more complex property ownership and management structures than school-based I-CHANGE implementations, requiring adapted governance arrangements. Integration of I-CHANGE citizen science methodologies with GBN physical development could create powerful synergies, with environmental monitoring and community engagement supporting more effective and equitable GBN implementation.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab demonstrates several significant strengths that position it as exemplary practice in urban sustainability and citizen engagement. **Methodological innovation** represents a core strength, with the integration of citizen science, participatory governance, and behavioral change strategies creating a comprehensive approach to sustainability that transcends traditional top-down policy models. The initiative's **explicit commitment to equity and inclusion** distinguishes it from many sustainability initiatives that inadvertently concentrate benefits among affluent populations. The deliberate outreach to marginalized communities, attention to equitable benefit distribution, and integration with social equity principles create potential for more just sustainability transitions than conventional approaches.[^3]

The initiative's **integration with formal education systems** creates lasting impacts on environmental consciousness and behaviors among younger generations while generating valuable environmental data. This pedagogical integration ensures that sustainability is embedded in cultural values and practices rather than remaining peripheral to mainstream society. The **quadruple helix governance model** represents institutional innovation that brings together diverse stakeholder perspectives and capacities (government, academia, industry, civil society), enabling more holistic problem-solving than traditional governance silos.[^2]

The **emphasis on real-time, locally-relevant environmental data** and transparent communication creates powerful tools for raising environmental awareness and informing evidence-based policy decisions. Making environmental data publicly accessible through digital platforms democratizes environmental information and empowers residents to understand local environmental conditions and personal behavioral impacts.[^2] The initiative's **flexibility and adaptive management approach** enables continuous learning and modification based on implementation experience, with successful approaches adapted and scaled while unsuccessful approaches are modified or abandoned.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

Despite significant strengths, the I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab faces several important limitations and weaknesses that warrant critical assessment. **Participation bias** represents a significant challenge, with citizen science and sustainability initiatives typically attracting populations with higher education, higher income, and greater social capital.[^2] While the initiative includes deliberate outreach to marginalized populations, risks remain that participation will skew toward already-engaged populations, potentially amplifying existing inequalities in political voice and influence. This participation bias raises questions about whether the initiative genuinely serves all residents equitably or primarily serves populations already predisposed toward environmental engagement.

**Limited quantification of environmental outcomes** represents a methodological weakness. While the initiative documents increased awareness and some behavioral changes, comprehensive quantification of environmental impacts (emissions reductions, air quality improvements, energy savings) remains limited in available documentation.[^2] This limitation complicates assessment of whether the initiative's substantial investment generates proportionate environmental benefits or whether alternative sustainability approaches might achieve greater environmental impact per unit of investment.

**Gentrification and displacement risks** remain inadequately addressed despite the initiative's equity commitments. Improved environmental quality and neighborhood amenities resulting from the initiative may trigger property value increases and market pressures for gentrification, potentially displacing lower-income residents unless accompanied by robust affordable housing protections and community stabilization policies.[^6] While the initiative is integrated with municipal sustainability strategy, the extent to which complementary policies (affordable housing protection, community stabilization) are actually implemented remains unclear from available documentation.

**Sustainability of participation and long-term viability** represent significant uncertainties. The initiative's current funding is time-limited through the European research grant period, with uncertain prospects for continuation and scaling beyond this timeframe. Sustaining citizen participation over time requires ongoing engagement strategies and demonstrated connection between participation and concrete outcomes. Political support must persist through electoral cycles and potential changes in municipal leadership priorities. Without clear pathways for long-term financial and institutional sustainability, the initiative risks becoming a temporary project rather than a permanent transformation of urban governance and sustainability practice.

**Limited direct environmental impact at scale** represents a potential weakness. While the initiative generates valuable environmental data and raises awareness, direct environmental impacts (emissions reductions, air quality improvements) may be modest at city scale, particularly given that behavioral change typically occurs incrementally and unevenly across populations. The initiative's impact on overall city-wide sustainability trajectories, while potentially significant, remains to be demonstrated through long-term monitoring and evaluation.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab's approach to equity and environmental justice represents both a significant strength and an area requiring ongoing critical attention. **Positive equity dimensions** include the initiative's explicit commitment to social equity as a central principle, deliberate outreach to lower-income and marginalized communities, and integration with municipal policies emphasizing equitable sustainability transitions.[^3] The initiative's emphasis on participatory governance and community voice in sustainability planning represents a justice-oriented approach that recognizes residents' right to influence decisions affecting their environments and lives.

However, **equity risks and challenges** warrant critical attention. Participation in citizen science activities may skew toward populations with higher education and greater social capital, potentially creating new forms of environmental privilege where certain populations have greater access to environmental data and influence over sustainability decisions. Improved environmental quality and neighborhood amenities may trigger gentrification and displacement pressures, potentially harming the very lower-income populations the initiative aims to serve.[^6] Environmental improvements concentrated in certain neighborhoods while others remain degraded could amplify spatial inequalities. Behavioral change expectations regarding sustainable mobility (cycling, walking) may be more feasible for affluent populations with resources to invest in bicycles and flexible work schedules than for lower-income populations constrained by economic necessity to rely on available transportation options.

**Environmental justice** dimensions include questions about whether the initiative addresses root causes of environmental inequality or merely treats symptoms. Air pollution in Milan results from transportation patterns, industrial activities, and energy systems shaped by broader economic and political structures. While the I-CHANGE initiative addresses behavioral and policy responses to these patterns, fundamental transformation of these systems may require more structural changes than the initiative directly addresses. The initiative's focus on citizen science and behavioral change, while valuable, may inadvertently place responsibility for environmental improvement on individual citizens rather than on economic and political systems and institutions bearing primary responsibility for environmental degradation.

#### **Future Outlook**

**Long-term trajectory:** The I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab's future trajectory depends on several critical factors. If the initiative successfully demonstrates environmental and social benefits, and if municipal government integrates citizen science and participatory governance methodologies into permanent sustainability systems, the initiative could catalyze fundamental transformation of how Milan approaches urban sustainability and governance. If the initiative generates sustained citizen engagement and builds lasting institutional capacity, it could serve as a model for other European cities and contribute to broader transformation of urban sustainability practice. However, if the initiative remains a temporary project dependent on external funding, if participation declines over time, or if demonstrated environmental impacts remain modest, the initiative's long-term influence may be limited.

**Ongoing challenges:** Several ongoing challenges will shape the initiative's future evolution. Sustaining citizen participation over time requires continued attention to engagement strategies and demonstrated connection between participation and concrete outcomes. Addressing gentrification and displacement risks requires complementary policies beyond the I-CHANGE initiative's direct scope, including affordable housing protections and community stabilization measures. Scaling the initiative to reach broader populations and achieve greater environmental impact at city scale requires significant expansion of resources and institutional commitment. Ensuring equity in participation and benefits requires ongoing attention to inclusion and deliberate outreach to marginalized populations.

**Planned and needed evolution:** The initiative's planned evolution includes continued expansion of monitoring networks to additional neighborhoods and schools, integration of additional environmental parameters beyond current focus areas, and deeper integration with municipal decision-making processes. Needed evolution includes development of clearer pathways for long-term financial and institutional sustainability, stronger integration with complementary sustainability initiatives (housing policy, transportation planning, energy systems transformation), and more comprehensive evaluation of environmental and social outcomes. Greater attention to structural barriers to sustainability transitions and to economic and political systems driving environmental degradation could strengthen the initiative's transformative potential. Explicit integration of climate justice principles and greater attention to power dynamics and structural inequalities could enhance the initiative's equity and justice dimensions.

This comprehensive case study analysis demonstrates that the I-CHANGE Milan Living Lab represents an innovative and multifaceted approach to urban sustainability that integrates citizen science, participatory governance, behavioral change strategies, and environmental monitoring into a coherent framework for advancing climate action and environmental quality. The initiative's strengths in methodological innovation, equity commitment, educational integration, and adaptive governance position it as exemplary practice worthy of study and potential replication. However, significant challenges regarding participation equity, long-term sustainability, gentrification risks, and direct environmental impact at scale require ongoing critical attention and adaptive responses. The initiative's ultimate success will depend on sustained institutional commitment, adequate long-term funding, effective integration with complementary sustainability policies, and demonstrated environmental and social benefits that justify continued investment and support.
