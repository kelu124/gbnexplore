# Executive summary## REMOURBAN: REgeneration MOdel for smart URBAN transformation (Nottingham Demonstrator)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** REMOURBAN: REgeneration MOdel for smart URBAN transformation
- **Alternative Names:** REMOURBAN City Demonstrator Project; REMOURBAN Nottingham
- **Location:** Nottingham, United Kingdom; Focus on Sneinton district
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 52.9566° N, 1.1505° W (Nottingham city center)
- **Timeline:** 2015–2019 (Project duration); Demonstration activities primarily over this period[^1][^2][^3]
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Nottingham city population: ~307,000
  - Sneinton demonstrator: ~8,100 residents, 463 retrofitted homes, city-level e-mobility systems[^1][^2][^3]
  - Total project budget (all cities): €24 million[^3]
- **Leading Organization(s):** Nottingham City Council, Nottingham Trent University, Nottingham City Homes, Nottingham Energy Partnership, INFOHUB Ltd., SASIE Ltd.[^2][^3]
- **Funding Sources:** European Union Horizon 2020 (Lighthouse project scheme), City Council, private partners[^1][^2][^3]

#### **Executive Summary**
REMOURBAN is a pioneering five-year EU Horizon 2020 “lighthouse” project that aimed to deliver a scalable model for urban regeneration integrating energy, mobility, and ICT. In Nottingham, the project focused on demonstrating how a deprived urban district—Sneinton—could be transformed through deep retrofitting of social housing, deployment of e-mobility solutions (including electric buses, car clubs, and smart parking), and the implementation of a city-wide ICT platform for real-time monitoring and control. The key objectives were to reduce carbon emissions, improve energy efficiency, make sustainable mobility accessible, and foster social inclusion through deep citizen engagement.

The project involved retrofitting 463 homes, expanding the electric vehicle charging network, and introducing smart management tools for energy and mobility. It targeted a 40% reduction in building energy demand and a 40% cut in local CO₂ emissions, while creating a replicable model for other cities. Citizen engagement was a cornerstone, ensuring interventions met local needs and building social acceptance. The project has delivered measurable impacts: significant CO₂ reductions, lower energy costs, improved air quality, and better resident satisfaction. REMOURBAN’s innovations have positioned Nottingham as a UK leader in sustainable urban transformation, setting a benchmark for integrated, citizen-focused smart city initiatives[^1][^4][^2][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**
- **City Profile:** Nottingham is a mid-sized UK city with a population of ~307,000. Historically an industrial center, it now features a mixed economy (services, academia, creative industries) and a diverse, multicultural population. The city’s geography is urban, with dense neighborhoods, historic housing, and a legacy of industrial infrastructure[^1][^2].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
  - **Historical development:** Sneinton, east of the city center, is characterized by late-19th and early-20th century terraced housing, much of it social housing managed by Nottingham City Homes[^3].
  - **Land use:** Predominantly residential, with pockets of commercial and community facilities.
  - **Demographics:** Includes a high proportion of low-income residents, diverse ethnic backgrounds, and a mix of age groups[^3].
  - **Infrastructure quality:** Prior to REMOURBAN, much of the housing was energy-inefficient, with poor insulation and dated heating systems; transport infrastructure was conventional, with limited sustainable mobility options[^3].
  - **Environmental conditions:** Urban air quality challenges, high energy consumption, and notable fuel poverty in older housing stock[^3].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**
- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High carbon emissions from buildings and transport
  - Fuel poverty and poor thermal comfort in social housing
  - Traffic congestion and limited access to low-emission mobility
  - Social deprivation and lack of resident engagement in urban change processes[^1][^3]
- **Triggering Events:** European policy drives for low-carbon cities, local government’s adoption of ambitious energy/carbon strategies, and EU funding opportunities under Horizon 2020[^1][^3].
- **Stakeholder Landscape:** Key actors included city government, social housing providers, local SMEs, academia, and residents—each with interests in cost savings, better living standards, local economic opportunity, and sustainable urban futures[^2][^3].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**
- **Governance Framework:** Led by Nottingham City Council, with strong partnerships between local government, academia, housing providers, and SMEs; supported by EU project governance[^2][^3].
- **Relevant Policies:** Nottingham City’s Energy (and Carbon) Strategy 2020; local development plans; UK and EU climate goals; Horizon 2020 smart city policy framework[^2].
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** EU public procurement and state aid rules; housing regulations; local authority powers for infrastructure delivery; need to coordinate between city and EU-level compliance[^2][^3].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**
- **Primary Goals:**
  - Demonstrate a scalable model for urban regeneration integrating energy, mobility, and ICT
  - Achieve deep reductions in energy use and CO₂ emissions
  - Enhance quality of life and affordability for residents
  - Increase citizen engagement and social inclusion[^1][^4][^2][^3]
- **Vision Statement:** “A smart city where energy flows are planned, mapped and monitored”—enabling Nottingham’s transition to a low-carbon, socially inclusive, and resilient urban environment[^2].
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** Directly supports Nottingham’s and the UK’s carbon neutrality ambitions, aligns with the EU’s urban innovation and sustainability strategies[^1][^4][^2].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**
- **Theory of Change:** Retrofit poor-quality housing to improve energy performance and comfort; deploy smart mobility solutions to reduce car use and emissions; integrate digital tools to optimize resources; engage citizens to ensure uptake and social buy-in—thus delivering improved sustainability, affordability, and equity[^1][^4][^3].
- **Target Beneficiaries:** Social housing residents, low-income households, city commuters, local businesses, and the wider urban community[^3].
- **Innovation Elements:** Large-scale, multi-technology retrofitting; integrated urban ICT platform; participatory resident engagement; replicable business and governance models for city-scale deployment[^1][^4][^2][^3].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**
- **Political:** Local and EU leadership on climate and social policy; city’s ambition for carbon neutrality[^1][^2].
- **Economic:** EU funding leverage, cost savings for residents, local job creation, and SME innovation[^2][^3].
- **Social:** Addressing fuel poverty, improving wellbeing, fostering community pride and empowerment[^3].
- **Environmental:** Meeting EU and city CO₂ reduction targets, improving air quality, climate adaptation[^1][^4][^2].
- **Technological:** Harnessing advances in ICT, energy management, and e-mobility platforms[^1][^2].

---

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**
- **Conceptual Framework:** Urban Regeneration Model (URM) based on cross-sector integration (energy, mobility, ICT); prioritizes citizen engagement and replicability[^1][^4].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** Deep retrofit of 463 homes in Sneinton (insulation, new heating, PV, smart meters); deployment of EV charging infrastructure and electric buses; city-scale digital information platform for real-time monitoring[^1][^2][^3].
- **Scope and Phasing:** Initial scoping and engagement, staged roll-out of retrofit and e-mobility solutions, iterative refinement based on feedback and monitoring[^3].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**
- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - Integrated city ICT platform for data management and visualization
  - Smart energy meters and user apps
  - Real-time EV charge management, smart parking
  - Low-carbon district heating (low-temperature network)
  - Electric vehicle charging points and car clubs
  - Building Integrated Photovoltaics (BIPV)[^1][^4][^2][^3]
- **Innovation Type:** Technological (ICT, energy, mobility), governance (collaborative partnerships), social (citizen engagement), financial (new models for retrofitting)[^1][^4][^3].
- **Integration Approach:** Technologies were layered onto existing city infrastructure, with robust data interfaces and interoperability planning; direct resident engagement in technology adoption[^1][^2].

#### **Implementation Methodology**
- **Procurement and Contracting:** Competitive tendering among local SMEs and specialized contractors for retrofitting and tech integration; compliance with EU procurement rules[^3].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Extensive participatory process with residents (workshops, home visits, feedback loops), ongoing stakeholder dialogue (city, academia, business)[^3].
- **Participatory Elements:** Residents involved in specification-setting, decision-making, and post-occupancy evaluation; continuous engagement throughout the project[^3].
- **Project Management:** Multi-stakeholder consortium with clear roles and responsibilities, centralized coordination by city council, iterative monitoring and reporting[^2][^3].

#### **Financing Model**
- **Budget and Costs:** Nottingham demonstrator’s share of €24m total project budget; cost breakdown includes retrofitting, mobility infrastructure, ICT platform, engagement[^3].
- **Funding Mix:** Weighted toward EU Horizon 2020 grant funding, with additional city and private investment[^1][^2][^3].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Energy cost savings for residents, operational efficiencies for council, exploration of user fees for e-mobility[^3].
- **Financial Sustainability:** Emphasis on business models with <15-year return on investment for retrofits, <5-year ROI for energy supply interventions[^4].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**
- **Technical:** Integrating new ICT and energy systems with legacy infrastructure; ensuring interoperability across platforms; engineering challenges in retrofitting varied housing stock[^1][^3].
- **Financial:** Upfront investment costs; aligning funding streams; ensuring affordability and value for money[^3].
- **Social and Political:** Engaging hard-to-reach residents; overcoming skepticism; balancing diverse stakeholder interests[^1][^3].
- **Administrative:** Coordinating a complex, multi-partner project; managing procurement and compliance burdens[^3].
- **Environmental:** Managing disruption during works; ensuring resilience to future climate conditions[^3].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**
- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Continuous resident engagement to build trust and refine interventions; iterative technical troubleshooting; leveraging local SMEs for flexible delivery[^3].
- **Design Modifications:** Customizing retrofit approaches to property archetypes; adjusting ICT tools for usability; extending engagement to private landlords as uptake lagged[^3].
- **Lessons Learned:** Early and sustained resident involvement is vital; flexibility in technology deployment improves outcomes; real-time data enables responsive management[^3].

---

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**
- **Physical Outputs:** 463 homes retrofitted (insulation, heating, PV, smart meters); new EV charging points; electric bus fleet expansion; city ICT platform deployed[^1][^2][^3].
- **Service Delivery:** Enhanced thermal comfort and energy services for residents; public access to e-mobility; digital tools for information and booking[^1][^3].
- **Quantitative Metrics:**
  - 39.7% energy savings in retrofitted homes
  - 40% reduction in CO₂ emissions in pilot area
  - 30% reduction in conventional car use; 20% increase in EV charger utilization[^1][^3]
  - 3 GWh/year energy savings; 550 t/year CO₂(eq) reduction (housing component)[^3]

#### **Social Impacts**
- **Community Benefits:** Better health and wellbeing; reduced energy poverty; improved satisfaction and pride among residents[^3].
- **Equity Outcomes:** Targeted at deprived communities; ensured social housing residents received direct benefits[^3].
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** No significant displacement reported; project designed to prevent negative impacts on vulnerable residents[^3].
- **Social Capital:** Strengthened networks via participatory processes; enhanced trust between residents and authorities[^3].
- **Cultural Impacts:** Supported local identity through place-based interventions; promoted a culture of sustainability[^3].

#### **Economic Impacts**
- **Job Creation:** Supported local SMEs; created jobs in retrofit and smart city sectors[^2][^3].
- **Economic Activity:** Stimulated green business activity; attracted further investment to the city[^2].
- **Property Values:** Likely upward pressure on property values due to improved quality; data not specifically reported.
- **Cost Savings:** Lower energy bills for residents; operational savings for city services[^3].
- **Economic Inclusion:** Focused on low-income households; provided affordable access to new technologies[^3].

#### **Environmental Impacts**
- **Climate and Emissions:** 40% CO₂ reduction in pilot area; notable progress toward city’s net-zero targets[^1][^4][^3].
- **Resource Management:** Higher energy efficiency, integration of renewables, smarter peak demand management[^1][^2].
- **Environmental Quality:** Improved air quality due to modal shift and electrification; reduced noise and pollution[^1][^4][^3].
- **Waste and Pollution:** Indirect benefits from reduced fossil fuel use and more efficient resource management[^1][^3].
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Exceeded initial targets for energy and CO₂ reductions in some sectors[^1][^4][^3].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**
- **Institutional Capacity:** Enhanced city’s ability to manage complex, integrated projects; built expertise for future initiatives[^1][^2][^3].
- **Policy Influence:** Informed city and national policy on retrofit and sustainable mobility; model being replicated by other cities[^1][^4].
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Real-time public data and participatory processes improved governance quality[^3].

#### **Performance Against Objectives**
- **Success Metrics:** Achieved or exceeded targets for energy, emissions, and resident satisfaction[^1][^4][^3].
- **Unintended Consequences:** No major negative consequences reported; some challenges in scaling private sector engagement[^3].
- **Long-term Viability:** Early results point to lasting benefits and the potential for city-wide roll-out; business models designed for sustainability[^1][^4][^3].

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**
- **Contextual Dependencies:** Success benefitted from strong city leadership, EU funding, and a large stock of similar social housing; core model adaptable elsewhere[^1][^4].
- **Critical Success Factors:** Deep resident engagement, multi-sector partnerships, robust ICT integration, and flexible retrofit approaches[^3].
- **Scalability:** Model designed for replication at city, regional, and national scales; already being tested in other EU “follower” cities[^1][^4].

#### **Comparative Analysis**
- **Similar Initiatives:** Comparable to other EU “lighthouse” smart city projects (e.g., Triangulum, GrowSmarter); Nottingham’s strength was the integration of ICT with retrofit and e-mobility in a socially deprived area[^1][^4][^2].
- **Best Practices:** Early and sustained citizen engagement; holistic integration of energy, mobility, and digital platforms; customization to local housing archetypes[^3].
- **Cautionary Lessons:** Engaging private landlords and the private sector can be difficult; technical complexity requires strong project management; flexibility is essential[^3].

#### **Parallels to [Your Specific Use Case]**
- **Contextual Similarities:** Urban area with aging housing, fuel poverty, need for mobility solutions, and desire for real-time resource management.
- **Methodological Parallels:** Participatory resident engagement, integration of ICT for monitoring and control, multi-technology retrofitting.
- **Technological Overlaps:** Use of smart meters, user apps, EV charging, real-time analytics.
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Public-led, multi-partner delivery with strong community involvement.
- **Transferable Strategies:** Layering digital tools onto existing infrastructure; embedding monitoring and feedback; focusing on social inclusion.
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Funding context, housing typologies, and regulatory environment may differ and require tailored approaches.

---

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- Comprehensive integration of energy, mobility, and digital solutions.
- Strong focus on equity and resident participation.
- Significant, measurable environmental and social impacts.
- Creation of a replicable, evidence-based model for urban regeneration[^1][^4][^3].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- Complexity of technical and organizational integration.
- Challenges in engaging private sector participants, particularly private landlords.
- Upfront investment needs may be a barrier in less well-resourced contexts[^3].

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- Project advanced urban equity by targeting deprived neighborhoods and involving residents at every stage.
- Model designed to avoid displacement and ensure benefits for low-income households[^3].

#### **Future Outlook**
- Nottingham continues to build on REMOURBAN’s legacy, scaling e-mobility and retrofit programs city-wide.
- Ongoing monitoring and adaptation required to ensure long-term sustainability.
- Next phases could focus on further private sector engagement and integration of additional smart city services[^1][^4][^3].
