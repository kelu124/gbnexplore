# Executive summary## Mapping Green Dublin – Community Engagement for Urban Trees

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Mapping Green Dublin – Community Engagement for Urban Trees
- **Alternative Names:** Mapping Green Dublin (MGD), Strategic Pathways to Community-led Greening
- **Location:** Dublin 8, Dublin City, Ireland
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 53.3406° N, 6.2850° W (center of Dublin 8)
- **Timeline:** 2019–2021 (core project period); ongoing community impacts and spin-offs
- **Project Scale:** Focused on Dublin 8 (an inner-city district); population affected: ~37,000 residents; area: approx. 4.5 km²
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - School of Geography, University College Dublin (UCD)
  - Common Ground (arts organization)
  - Artist Seoidín O’Sullivan
  - Connect the Dots (community engagement facilitators)
- **Funding Sources:**
  - Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Research Programme (Government of Ireland initiative)
  - Department of the Environment, Climate and Communications

#### **Executive Summary**

**Mapping Green Dublin (MGD)** is a collaborative action research project launched in Dublin 8, an inner-city neighbourhood experiencing significant green space deficits. Funded by the Irish EPA, the project sought to map all urban trees and green infrastructure, identify gaps, and empower residents to co-design greening strategies responsive to local needs[^1][^2][^3][^4]. Through the use of GIS mapping, participatory digital platforms, and a series of workshops and events, MGD gathered scientific data and local knowledge, enabling residents, artists, and community organizations to engage meaningfully with policymakers and practitioners.

The initiative was structured around three core phases: (1) mapping tree and green space assets, deficits, and future needs; (2) co-creation of greening solutions through curated community events and workshops; and (3) formulation of targeted action points and policy recommendations. This bottom-up, community-led approach ensured interventions had high social and cultural value, maximized wellbeing, and addressed both environmental and equity challenges. The project produced open-access datasets, influenced city-level policy, and established new collaborative governance mechanisms such as the D8 Green Bridge Forum. Its outcomes included increased tree cover, enhanced community identity, improved air quality, biodiversity, and climate resilience. Importantly, MGD set a precedent for inclusive urban greening strategy-making in Dublin and serves as a replicable model for other cities seeking to balance technical rigor with grassroots empowerment[^1][^2][^3][^4].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Dublin is Ireland’s capital and largest city, with a population over 550,000 (greater metropolitan area: ~1.4 million). It is a key economic and cultural hub in national and European contexts, characterized by rapid growth, significant urban regeneration, and persistent inequalities in access to green space[^1][^3][^4].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics (Dublin 8):**
  - **Historical Patterns:** Traditionally an industrial and working-class area, Dublin 8 has undergone substantial transformation over the past two decades, including large-scale regeneration, new housing developments, and increasing pressures from student accommodation and urban redevelopment[^3][^4].
  - **Land Use Composition:** Mix of residential, social housing complexes (e.g., Dolphin House, St. Teresa’s Gardens), commercial zones, and sporadic green assets. Significant loss of public greenspace due to development pressures[^3].
  - **Demographics:** Diverse and changing, with long-standing local communities, growing immigrant populations, and a younger age profile due to student housing. Persistent deprivation and inter-generational social challenges are noted[^3].
  - **Infrastructure Quality:** Varied; some areas benefit from proximity to major parks (Phoenix Park, St Anne’s Park), while others experience pronounced deficits in green amenities and public space[^1][^3].
  - **Environmental Conditions:** Air pollution hotspots, heat islands, and limited street tree coverage in many sub-districts. Water management and flood risk are local concerns[^4].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - Uneven distribution of green infrastructure and urban trees
  - Deficits in community access to quality green space
  - Fragmented governance and lack of coordinated greening strategy
  - Environmental justice concerns: neighborhoods “left behind” in green investment[^1][^3][^4]
- **Triggering Events:**
  - Strategic city policies to enhance green infrastructure (Dublin City Council)
  - Recognition of environmental deficiencies via mapping and population/traffic overlays
  - Community mobilization around perceived neglect and environmental health[^1][^3]
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - Residents, community development organizations, artists, local government, environmental agencies, private landowners, and advocacy groups[^3][^5]

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** Dublin City Council, EPA, Department of Housing, Planning and Local Government; fragmented responsibilities for green infrastructure across agencies[^3][^4].
- **Relevant Policies:** Dublin City Council’s Green Infrastructure Strategy (draft at the time), local Strategic Development and Regeneration Area (SDRA) frameworks[^4].
- **Legal/Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** Lack of a dedicated office for greening coordination; complex land ownership (public/private); enabling role of EPA-funded research in policy development[^3][^4].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Map existing tree cover and green infrastructure
  - Identify green deficits and prioritize interventions
  - Engage community in co-design and co-creation of greening strategies
  - Influence policy and practice for more just, sustainable urban greening[^1][^2][^4]
- **Vision Statement:** To create a healthier, more resilient, and socially just urban environment through evidence-based, community-led greening[^3][^4].
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** Supports Dublin’s draft Green Infrastructure Strategy, regional plans for climate adaptation and urban liveability, and national objectives for sustainable development[^4].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:** Data-driven identification of green deficits → participatory co-creation of solutions → targeted interventions → improved wellbeing, equity, and environmental quality[^1][^2][^3][^4].
- **Target Beneficiaries:** Local residents (especially those experiencing green deficits), vulnerable populations, broader city through improved ecosystem services[^3][^5].
- **Innovation Elements:**
  - Use of GIS and open data for transparency and planning
  - Integration of community arts and creative engagement
  - Novel governance structures (D8 Green Bridge Forum)[^2][^5]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Alignment with local government priorities; public pressure for environmental equity[^3][^4].
- **Economic:** Long-term value creation through ecosystem services; potential for increased property values[^1][^3].
- **Social:** Desire for greater agency, improved health, and stronger community identity[^3][^5].
- **Environmental:** Urgency of climate adaptation, biodiversity loss, air quality improvement[^1][^3][^4].
- **Technological:** Advances in GIS, digital mapping, and participatory platforms[^1][^2][^3].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Community-led action research; co-creation; environmental and social justice; evidence-based planning[^2][^3][^4].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** Mapping of tree cover and green assets; identification of priority intervention areas; creation and enhancement of micro green spaces; proposals for green corridors and intergenerational spaces[^3].
- **Scope and Phasing:**
  - Phase 1: Data mapping and gap analysis
  - Phase 2: Community engagement and co-creation
  - Phase 3: Action planning and policy recommendations[^2][^3]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - GIS mapping of trees and green spaces
  - Participatory digital platforms for reporting and engagement
  - High-resolution aerial imagery (BlueSky 12cm), open census data[^1][^3][^4]
- **Innovation Type:** Technological (GIS/data), social (co-creation/arts), governance (new forums), process-oriented (participatory strategy-making)[^2][^3][^5].
- **Integration Approach:** Scientific data presented in accessible formats; integration with local knowledge; collaboration across disciplines and stakeholder groups[^2][^3][^5].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:** Led by UCD Geography; collaboration with arts and community organizations; EPA-funded[^3][^5].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Focus groups, workshops, community launches, urban prototyping, open forums; strong emphasis on inclusivity and empowerment[^2][^3][^5].
- **Participatory Elements:** Residents involved in mapping, design, decision-making; arts-based methods to surface local values and aspirations[^2][^3][^5].
- **Project Management:** Coordinated by UCD; regular steering meetings; iterative feedback loops; documentation and dissemination of process and outcomes[^3][^5].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** Core funding from EPA Research Programme (~€490,000 estimated for carbon storage value); additional support from stakeholders and area businesses[^3][^4].
- **Funding Mix:** Primarily public (EPA), supplemented by business contributions (D8 Green Bridge Forum)[^5].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** No direct revenue generation; emphasis on public good and ecosystem service valuation[^3][^4].
- **Financial Sustainability:** Ongoing community forums (e.g., D8 Green Bridge Forum) funded by local businesses; recommended integration into city policy for long-term viability[^5][^4].

---

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Data gaps, limitations in mapping privately-owned trees, complexity of urban land use[^1][^3][^4].
- **Financial:** Short duration (24 months); need for sustained funding to move from strategy to implementation[^6][^5].
- **Social and Political:** Securing buy-in from private landowners; overcoming skepticism; maintaining engagement during pandemic; balancing social justice and gentrification risks[^3][^4].
- **Administrative:** Fragmented governance of green infrastructure; lack of a single coordinating office[^3][^4].
- **Environmental:** Site contamination, legacy issues from industrial land use, climate change impacts[^3][^4].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Use of open data and transparent processes to build trust; arts-based and creative engagement to surface values; ongoing forums to maintain momentum[^2][^3][^5].
- **Design Modifications:** Shift toward micro green spaces and incremental interventions; integration of intergenerational and cultural elements[^3][^5].
- **Lessons Learned:** Importance of bottom-up strategy-making; necessity of multi-stakeholder collaboration; need for flexible, adaptive governance structures[^5][^4].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** Comprehensive tree maps and datasets; creation/enhancement of micro green areas; pilot projects (e.g., Turvey Park)[^1][^5].
- **Service Delivery:** New platforms for community reporting and monitoring; support for local greening actions[^1][^5].
- **Quantitative Metrics:**
  - Carbon storage: 3,361 tons
  - Carbon sequestration: 97.8 tons/year
  - Oxygen production: 261 tons/year
  - Avoided runoff: 162,900 cubic feet/year[^3]

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** Improved health, wellbeing, and sense of place; greater agency over local environment[^1][^2][^3][^5].
- **Equity Outcomes:** Focus on areas with most pronounced deficits; efforts to counteract gentrification by embedding social justice in design[^4].
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** Addressed risks via community governance; not fully resolved but actively managed[^4].
- **Social Capital:** Strengthened networks; ongoing forums (D8 Green Bridge) for civic engagement[^5].
- **Cultural Impacts:** Integration of arts and local history; celebration of community identity[^3][^5].

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Direct jobs in mapping, engagement, and park creation; indirect employment through ongoing greening activities[^5].
- **Economic Activity:** Potential boost to local business and property values through improved amenity[^1][^3].
- **Cost Savings:** Ecosystem services quantified; avoided healthcare costs from improved air quality and mental health[^3][^4].
- **Economic Inclusion:** Targeting deprived areas; efforts to spread benefits broadly[^3][^4].

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Increased tree cover supports climate resilience and adaptation; measurable reduction in urban heat and improved air quality[^1][^3][^4].
- **Resource Management:** Improved stormwater management, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity[^3][^4].
- **Environmental Quality:** Enhanced green space, air quality, and ecosystem services[^1][^3][^4].
- **Waste and Pollution:** No direct waste management component; secondary benefits from improved green infrastructure[^3].
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Performance tracked through open data; alignment with city and national standards[^3][^4].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Strengthened via training and new collaborative forums; empowerment of community organizations[^5].
- **Policy Influence:** Direct input into Dublin’s draft Green Infrastructure Strategy; advocacy for micro green areas and inclusive greening[^5][^4].
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Open data and participatory processes improved trust and accountability[^1][^3][^5].

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** High achievement in mapping, community engagement, and policy influence; ongoing work required for full implementation[^1][^3][^5][^4].
- **Unintended Consequences:** Raised profile of greening agenda citywide; some risk of future gentrification if not carefully managed[^4].
- **Long-term Viability:** Embedded in new city policy; sustained forums and ongoing community action indicate lasting impact[^5][^4].

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Comprehensive data-driven approach:** Led to evidence-based decision-making and targeted interventions[^1][^3][^4].
- **Inclusive, participatory process:** Empowered residents and ensured solutions were locally relevant[^2][^3][^5].
- **Policy influence:** Tangible impact on city-level strategy and establishment of new governance mechanisms[^5][^4].
- **Interdisciplinary collaboration:** Integration of arts, science, and community knowledge enriched outcomes[^3][^5].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Short project duration:** Limited ability to implement long-term interventions and secure sustained funding[^6][^5].
- **Fragmented governance:** Persistent challenges coordinating across agencies and landowners[^3][^4].
- **Risks of gentrification:** Despite efforts, potential for unintended social displacement remains[^4].
- **Data gaps:** Some difficulties in mapping privately-owned green assets and capturing informal greenspace[^1][^3].

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Proactive focus on deprived areas:** Addressed historic neglect and environmental justice concerns[^3][^4].
- **Community empowerment:** Strengthened local agency and capacity for future action[^5].
- **Ongoing vigilance needed:** Equity risks (e.g., gentrification, displacement) require continued monitoring and adaptive management[^4].

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Long-term trajectory:** Embedded in city policy; ongoing forums and community-led projects continue to drive action[^5][^4].
- **Ongoing challenges:** Need for sustained funding, improved coordination, and vigilance against equity risks[^3][^4].
- **Evolution/next phases:** Expansion to other neighborhoods; integration with broader climate adaptation and regeneration strategies; continued innovation in participatory governance and technology use[^3][^5][^4].
