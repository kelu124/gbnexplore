# Executive summary## Heerlen Mijnwater 5th Generation District Heating and Cooling (D2Grids Project)

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Heerlen Mijnwater 5th Generation District Heating and Cooling (5GDHC) Demonstration (D2Grids Project)
- **Alternative Names:** Mijnwater 5GDHC, D2Grids Heerlen demonstration
- **Location:** Heerlen, Limburg, Netherlands
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 50.8876° N, 5.9810° E (city center)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Initial mine water network: 2005 (pilot), major expansion 2013 onward
  - D2Grids Project: 2018–2022 (with ongoing expansion and operation)[^1][^2]
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Serves approx. 250,000 m² of floor area (offices, retail, residential, public buildings)
  - Network covers much of Heerlen and is expanding to Parkstad Limburg region[^2]
- **Leading Organization(s):** Mijnwater B.V. (public-private utility); D2Grids consortium (Interreg North-West Europe, Horizon 2020)
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - European Union (Interreg NWE, Horizon 2020)
  - Dutch government
  - Local and regional authorities
  - Mijnwater B.V. investment[^1][^2]

#### **Executive Summary**
The **Heerlen Mijnwater 5GDHC Demonstration** is a flagship project for next-generation district heating and cooling, utilizing abandoned coal mines as a vast underground thermal energy storage and exchange medium. Managed by Mijnwater B.V. and supported under the D2Grids Horizon 2020 initiative, the system is a pioneer in Europe for implementing a truly **circular, decentralized, and low-temperature thermal grid**. Key innovations include bi-directional energy flows, decentralized heat pumps at the building level, and advanced smart controls to optimize energy sharing between users. The grid leverages mine water at different depths for **seasonal thermal storage**, capturing and redistributing waste heat and cold from local buildings, data centers, supermarkets, and industrial processes. 

By integrating renewable sources and maximizing local energy reuse, the system has achieved **over 50% reduction in urban energy demand and up to 65% cut in CO₂ emissions** compared to conventional systems. The Heerlen case stands as a scalable model of urban energy transition, enhancing **energy autonomy, economic resilience, and local job creation**. Its success underpins the broader D2Grids effort to replicate 5GDHC networks in other European cities, demonstrating the feasibility and transformative potential of highly flexible, decentralized energy infrastructure for climate-neutral urban development[^3][^1][^2][^4].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**
- **City Profile:** Heerlen is a medium-sized city (pop. approx. 87,000) in the Limburg province, historically a regional coal mining center until the 1970s. The post-mining economy is mixed, with public sector, services, and some light industry. Heerlen is part of the Parkstad Limburg urban region, which has a population of around 250,000[^5].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** 
  - The 5GDHC network covers central Heerlen and is expanding across Parkstad Limburg.
  - Land use: a mix of commercial zones, public buildings, offices, supermarkets, and residential blocks[^2].
  - Demographics: Diverse, with a legacy of working-class populations from mining; some areas face economic and social challenges post-coal.
  - Infrastructure: Modernized since the 2000s, with good accessibility but aging building stock.
  - Environmental: The region deals with legacy mine pollution but also benefits from the unique resource of flooded mine galleries.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**
- **Problems/Challenges Identified:** 
  - High fossil fuel dependence for heating/cooling
  - Urban CO₂ emissions significantly above national targets
  - Economic decline and job losses after coal mine closures
  - Underutilized legacy mine infrastructure
  - Need for energy resilience and affordable heating/cooling[^2][^4]
- **Triggering Events:** 
  - Closure of regional coal mines (1970s), leading to flooding of galleries
  - EU and Dutch climate goals, and local push for economic revitalization in the 2000s
- **Stakeholder Landscape:** 
  - Municipality of Heerlen (owner/facilitator)
  - Mijnwater B.V. (project developer/operator)
  - Local businesses, building owners, residents
  - Regional development agencies
  - EU funding bodies, research institutions

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**
- **Governance Framework:** 
  - Local authority leadership with strong regional and EU partnership[^1]
- **Relevant Policies:** 
  - Dutch and EU climate action plans
  - Urban sustainability and energy transition strategies
  - Supportive zoning for utility infrastructure
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
  - Public-private partnership model enabled flexible investment
  - Regulatory support for renewable heat/cold and innovative grid models

#### **Strategic Vision**
- **Primary Goals:** 
  - Achieve near-zero emission urban heating and cooling
  - Maximize local, renewable, and residual energy use
  - Create a scalable, resilient, and economically viable energy model[^3][^1]

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** 
  - Directly supports Dutch/EU decarbonization and energy transition targets
  - Model for replication (D2Grids: 5 pilot sites across NW Europe)[^1][^6][^2]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**
- **Theory of Change:** 
  - By utilizing mine water as a thermal reservoir and enabling bi-directional, demand-driven energy flows between buildings, the system drastically reduces fossil fuel use, cuts emissions, and supports urban resilience[^5][^2].
- **Target Beneficiaries:** 
  - Local residents, businesses, public sector users
  - Broader urban region (through job creation and knowledge transfer)
- **Innovation Elements:** 
  - Bidirectional low-temperature grids
  - Seasonal underground thermal storage
  - Real-time digital monitoring and smart controls[^5][^2][^4]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**
- **Political Drivers:** 
  - Local leadership commitment; regional economic renewal
- **Economic Drivers:** 
  - Cost-effective reuse of legacy infrastructure; local job creation
- **Social Drivers:** 
  - Improved comfort, affordability, and urban quality
- **Environmental Drivers:** 
  - Climate adaptation, circular economy, resource efficiency
- **Technological Drivers:** 
  - Advances in heat pump and smart grid technologies; EU research funding[^3][^6][^5]

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**
- **Conceptual Framework:** 
  - Urban energy circularity; 5GDHC principles (demand-driven, bi-directional, modular, decentralized)[^3][^5]
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** 
  - Network of “hubs” supplying and exchanging thermal energy via insulated pipes
  - Buildings equipped with individual heat pumps for both heating and cooling
  - Use of deep (700m) and shallow (200m) mine water wells for thermal storage[^2][^4]
- **Scope and Phasing:** 
  - Stepwise expansion: initial pilot (2005), city-wide scaling (post-2013), regional replication (2018–2022 under D2Grids)[^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**
- **Technologies Deployed:** 
  - Mine water reservoirs (thermal source/sink)
  - Decentralized building-level heat pumps
  - Bi-directional, low-temperature insulated grid
  - Smart digital control systems for demand-supply matching
  - Integration of waste heat sources (data centers, supermarkets, etc.)[^3][^5][^2][^4]
- **Innovation Type:** 
  - Technological (grid, heat pumps, storage)
  - Governance (public-private partnership, stakeholder engagement)
  - Process-oriented (stepwise retrofitting, adaptive phasing)
- **Integration Approach:** 
  - Legacy mine infrastructure adapted for energy use
  - Gradual integration with existing building stock

#### **Implementation Methodology**
- **Procurement and Contracting:** 
  - Mijnwater B.V. as the main implementation body; partners selected via public procurement
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** 
  - Strong municipal leadership; consultation with building owners, local businesses
  - Public outreach, including education and training (Open Universiteit)[^1][^6][^7]
- **Participatory Elements:** 
  - Co-design with major building operators; community information sessions
- **Project Management:** 
  - Dedicated project management by Mijnwater B.V. supported by D2Grids consortium
- **Financing Model:**
  - **Budget and Costs:** Not publicly disclosed in detail; significant EU and national funding involved[^1][^2]
  - **Funding Mix:** EU (Interreg NWE, Horizon 2020), national/local public funds, Mijnwater B.V. private capital
  - **Revenue Mechanisms:** User tariffs for energy supply (regulated), cost-sharing for building connections
  - **Financial Sustainability:** Revenue from energy sales; scalability ensures broader financial viability[^1]

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**
- **Technical Challenges:** 
  - Complexity of integrating legacy mine infrastructure with modern grid
  - Need for advanced, robust digital control and metering
  - Seasonal balancing and ensuring reliability during peak demand[^2][^4]
- **Financial Challenges:** 
  - High initial capital costs for network expansion and retrofitting
  - Ensuring long-term financial sustainability
- **Social and Political Challenges:** 
  - Gaining buy-in from diverse building owners
  - Coordinating multiple stakeholders (public, private, residential, commercial)
- **Administrative Challenges:** 
  - Navigating regulatory frameworks for new energy models
  - Project management across multiple funding and governance layers
- **Environmental Challenges:** 
  - Monitoring groundwater quality and preventing potential contamination

#### **Adaptive Strategies**
- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** 
  - Phased implementation to manage risk and build confidence
  - Continuous stakeholder engagement and transparent communication
  - Technical innovation (modular grid design, smart controls)
- **Design Modifications:** 
  - Shift from linear to fully bi-directional grid structure for flexibility[^5]
  - Expansion of underground storage and integration of more waste heat sources
- **Lessons Learned During Implementation:** 
  - Importance of modular, scalable design
  - Value of combining old (mines) and new (ICT) infrastructure for resilience
  - Need for ongoing training and capacity building[^1][^7]

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**
- **Physical Outputs:** 
  - Over 250,000 m² of buildings connected
  - 20 TJ/year of heating and 20 TJ/year of cooling supplied[^2]
- **Service Delivery:** 
  - Reliable, low-emission heating and cooling to mixed-use buildings
- **Quantitative Metrics:** 
  - Over 50% reduction in local energy demand
  - Up to 65% reduction in CO₂ emissions[^2][^5]

#### **Social Impacts**
- **Community Benefits:** 
  - Improved comfort and indoor climate for residents, workers, and visitors
  - Local job creation in operations, maintenance, and retrofitting[^2]
- **Equity Outcomes:** 
  - Broad inclusion of public and private buildings; affordability focus
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** 
  - No evidence of negative displacement; focus is on existing buildings
- **Social Capital:** 
  - Strengthened local networks through shared infrastructure
- **Cultural Impacts:** 
  - Reinvention of local identity from “post-mining” to “clean energy pioneer”

#### **Economic Impacts**
- **Job Creation:** 
  - Direct jobs (utility operation, technical services) and indirect (construction, retrofitting)[^2]
- **Economic Activity:** 
  - Attractiveness for new businesses, especially those seeking sustainable locations
- **Property Values:** 
  - Likely positive impact, especially for energy-efficient, future-proofed buildings
- **Cost Savings:** 
  - Reduced energy bills for connected users over time[^2]
- **Economic Inclusion:** 
  - Access to affordable energy for a broad cross-section of the community

#### **Environmental Impacts**
- **Climate and Emissions:** 
  - Major cuts in GHG emissions; pathway to net-zero with more renewables[^2][^5]
- **Resource Management:** 
  - Closed-loop, circular energy flows minimize waste
  - Efficient use of abandoned mine infrastructure
- **Environmental Quality:** 
  - Improved air quality; reduced reliance on fossil fuels
- **Waste and Pollution:** 
  - Reduced emissions and waste heat release; groundwater monitored for safety
- **Sustainability Metrics:** 
  - 5GDHC KPIs include energy loop closure, share of renewables, self-sufficiency[^7]

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**
- **Institutional Capacity:** 
  - Strengthened local and regional capabilities in advanced energy systems
- **Policy Influence:** 
  - Model adopted for replication in other D2Grids pilot cities
- **Transparency and Accountability:** 
  - Public reporting of performance; engagement with EU and national policy forums

#### **Performance Against Objectives**
- **Success Metrics:** 
  - Met or exceeded targets for emissions, energy savings, and user engagement
- **Unintended Consequences:** 
  - Positive: Enhanced city reputation, new investment interest
  - Negative: Complexity in scaling to legacy buildings; need for ongoing digital upgrades
- **Long-term Viability:** 
  - Demonstrated viability; ongoing expansion and refinement

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**
- **Contextual Dependencies:** 
  - Use of flooded mines is unique, but principles transferable to other local thermal storage resources (aquifers, boreholes)
- **Critical Success Factors:** 
  - Strong political support, public-private partnership, phased implementation, robust digital controls, stakeholder engagement
- **Scalability:** 
  - Approach is modular and can be expanded across districts or cities[^5][^2]

#### **Comparative Analysis**
- **Similar Initiatives:** 
  - Paris-Saclay (France), Bochum (Germany), Glasgow and Nottingham (UK) under D2Grids; other minewater projects in Europe[^1][^6][^5]
- **Best Practices:** 
  - Integration of waste heat/cold, bi-directional grids, digital management, leveraging legacy infrastructure
- **Cautionary Lessons:** 
  - Need for adaptive management, careful regulatory navigation, and continuous community involvement

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- **Pioneering circular energy system** with proven large-scale CO₂ cuts
- **Scalable, modular model** adaptable to various urban contexts
- **Effective use of legacy infrastructure** for sustainability and economic renewal
- **Strong stakeholder engagement** and local capacity-building[^1][^2][^4]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- **Complexity of control systems** and integration with legacy buildings
- **High upfront costs** and dependence on public/EU funding for initial deployment
- **Technology and knowledge transfer** may be challenging for less experienced cities

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- **Inclusive access** to affordable, clean energy across sectors
- **No evidence of displacement or social exclusion**; focus on existing buildings and users
- **Empowerment through local job creation** and skill development

#### **Future Outlook**
- **Continued expansion** in Parkstad Limburg and other regions
- **Integration of additional renewables** and further emissions reduction
- **Potential for digital upgrades** (blockchain, AI-driven management) to optimize performance[^1][^7]
- **Ongoing need for investment** in technical training, stakeholder engagement, and adaptation to new regulatory contexts
