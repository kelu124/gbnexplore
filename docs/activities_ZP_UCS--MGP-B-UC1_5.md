# Executive summary## Vitoria-Gasteiz DHC Planning with the THERMOS Tool (Horizon 2020)

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Vitoria-Gasteiz District Heating and Cooling (DHC) Planning with the THERMOS Tool
- **Alternative Names:** THERMOS Project – Vitoria-Gasteiz Case Study
- **Location:** Vitoria-Gasteiz, Basque Country, Spain
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 42.8467° N, 2.6727° W
- **Timeline:** Project initiated in 2016 as part of the Horizon 2020 THERMOS Project; key milestones include the selection of Vitoria-Gasteiz as a pilot city (2016), software testing and scenario planning (2017–2020), and continued optimization and planning phases (2020–present)[^1][^2].
- **Project Scale:** City-wide planning, focusing on pilot districts (e.g., Coronación), with 750 dwellings and multiple public buildings initially targeted; affects a population of over 200,000[^3][^4].
- **Leading Organization(s):** Vitoria-Gasteiz City Council, THERMOS Consortium (includes academic, municipal, and private sector partners such as Veolia), Horizon 2020 (EU)[^1][^2].
- **Funding Sources:** European Union (Horizon 2020), local and regional government funds, public-private partnerships (notably with Veolia), and possible national or regional grants for renewable energy[^3][^1].

#### **Executive Summary**

The Vitoria-Gasteiz DHC Planning initiative, embedded within the EU Horizon 2020 THERMOS project, exemplifies the use of advanced modeling and scenario analysis tools to accelerate the shift toward sustainable, low-carbon district heating and cooling (DHC) networks. Leveraging the THERMOS software, the city developed high-resolution energy demand maps and tested a series of network expansion scenarios, with a strong focus on integrating renewable energy sources and optimizing for key performance indicators (KPIs) such as system efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and greenhouse gas emission reductions[^1][^2].

The initiative targeted districts characterized by high energy demand and outdated, carbon-intensive heating infrastructure, particularly in areas with predominantly individual gas or electric heating. By employing the THERMOS modeling tool, planners could quickly identify the most technically and financially viable network layouts, estimate investment needs, and quantify the social, economic, and environmental benefits of various design options[^1][^3].

Key features include the use of biomass and other renewables in the DHC system, a strong emphasis on stakeholder engagement, and the implementation of a public-private partnership model for network ownership and operation. The project not only resulted in a clear roadmap for DHC expansion but also set a precedent for KPI-driven, transparent, and participatory planning in the Spanish context. The outcomes—lower energy costs for users, accelerated renewable uptake, and improved urban air quality—demonstrate the potential of data-driven planning for sustainable urban transformation[^3][^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:**  
  Vitoria-Gasteiz is the capital of the Basque Autonomous Community in northern Spain, with a population of over 250,000. It serves as a regional administrative center and has been recognized for its strong sustainability agenda, having won the European Green Capital award. The city's economic base includes public administration, services, light industry, and logistics. It is located on a plateau at approximately 525 meters elevation, surrounded by agricultural and forested land[^5][^4].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  The THERMOS case study focused on mixed-use districts such as Coronación, characterized by:
  - **Historical Patterns:** Predominantly mid-20th-century apartment blocks, mixed residential and some commercial/institutional uses.
  - **Land Use:** Dense urban fabric with limited open space, aging building stock, and substantial retrofit needs.
  - **Demographics:** Socioeconomically diverse, with many middle- and lower-income households, elderly residents, and a mix of long-term residents and newer arrivals.
  - **Infrastructure:** Historically reliant on individual gas or electric heating; limited prior experience with collective DHC.
  - **Environmental Conditions:** Urban heat island effect, air quality concerns, and increasing vulnerability to climate-related events (e.g., heatwaves, flooding)[^3][^5].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High building-level energy demand and inefficiency.
  - Heavy reliance on fossil fuel-based, individual heating systems.
  - Poor air quality and high GHG emissions.
  - Social equity concerns, with energy poverty affecting vulnerable groups.
  - Existing infrastructure constraints for DHC implementation[^3][^4].

- **Triggering Events:**
  - Adoption of ambitious local and EU climate goals (Covenant of Mayors, city climate strategies).
  - Availability of EU funding for innovation in urban energy systems.
  - Community and policy recognition of the need for building retrofit and infrastructure renewal[^3][^4].

- **Stakeholder Landscape:**
  - City council (driving climate action and urban renewal).
  - Residents and neighborhood associations (critical for user buy-in).
  - Private sector partners (Veolia, energy service providers).
  - EU institutions (funders and technical support).
  - Regional government (policy alignment, co-financing)[^3][^1].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:**  
  Led by the city council in partnership with private operators, with oversight from regional and EU bodies.

- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - National and regional climate action plans.
  - Urban planning and building codes promoting energy efficiency and renewable integration.
  - EU directives on energy efficiency, renewable energy, and air quality[^3][^4].

- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Legal frameworks for public-private partnerships in energy.
  - Subsidies and incentives for renewables and building retrofits.
  - Challenges included complex permitting, stakeholder coordination, and the novelty of collective heat supply in a context accustomed to individual systems[^3][^4].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Accelerate the decarbonization of urban heating and cooling.
  - Deploy data-driven, scenario-based planning to optimize network expansion.
  - Maximize the share of renewables and minimize emissions and costs[^1][^2].

- **Vision Statement:**  
  To create a replicable, efficient, and socially equitable DHC system that supports Vitoria-Gasteiz’s ambition of becoming a carbon-neutral city.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  Integral to the city’s climate action and adaptation strategies, and aligned with EU Green Deal and Horizon 2020 energy objectives[^3][^4][^2].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  By integrating advanced modeling tools (THERMOS), stakeholders can identify optimal DHC scenarios that are technically, financially, and socially viable, leading to accelerated renewable integration, cost savings, and improved urban sustainability[^1].

- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  Local residents (especially in retrofitted districts), building owners, vulnerable households (energy poverty reduction), city government, and the broader urban environment[^3][^4].

- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Use of open-source, high-resolution energy planning software.
  - Application of KPI-driven decision-making.
  - New public-private ownership models for DHC in Spain[^1][^3].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:**  
  Local and EU climate leadership, public pressure for sustainability.

- **Economic:**  
  Need for cost-effective infrastructure renewal, job creation, and energy cost reductions.

- **Social:**  
  Energy equity, improved comfort, and health outcomes.

- **Environmental:**  
  Emission reduction, air quality, resilience to climate risks.

- **Technological:**  
  Availability of advanced modeling (THERMOS), digital mapping, and renewable heat solutions[^1][^3].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  Data-driven, scenario-based urban energy planning; integration of building retrofits with collective energy infrastructure; participatory governance.

- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  Deployment of new DHC networks (biomass, solar, geothermal), building insulation and energy system upgrades in pilot districts, spatial mapping of heat demand and supply[^3][^4].

- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  Initial focus on high-demand, high-potential districts; phased rollout based on scenario analysis and stakeholder readiness[^3].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **THERMOS Modeling Software:** For mapping, scenario analysis, and optimization[^1][^2].
  - **Renewable Energy Systems:** Biomass (wood chips), solar thermal, geothermal.
  - **Smart Controls:** Integrated energy management at building and network levels.
  - **Building Retrofit Technologies:** Insulation, efficient windows, HVAC upgrades[^3][^4].

- **Innovation Type:**  
  Primarily technological (modeling, renewables), with governance/process innovation (PPP models, participatory planning).

- **Integration Approach:**  
  DHC network integrated with new and existing buildings, aligned with broader smart city and climate resilience strategies[^3][^4].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  Public-private partnership (Veolia and Basque Energy Department, 50:50 ownership)[^3].

- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  Extensive community outreach, engagement of building owners, neighborhood associations, and public information campaigns[^3][^4].

- **Participatory Elements:**  
  Opportunities for resident feedback, co-design of billing and service models, education on renewable benefits[^3].

- **Project Management:**  
  Coordinated by city energy and urban planning departments, with technical support from THERMOS project partners and EU oversight[^1][^2].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  Substantial investment for DHC infrastructure, retrofits, and renewables. Example: Green Urban Infrastructure Strategy budgeted over €10 million for related projects[^5].

- **Funding Mix:**  
  EU Horizon 2020, local and regional government, private sector contributions.

- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  User energy bills, possible subsidies for low-income households.

- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  Designed for long-term viability through efficient operations, renewable integration, and stable user base[^3].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  Detailed data collection for accurate modeling, integration with legacy infrastructure, network optimization for variable demand[^3][^1].

- **Financial:**  
  Upfront investment requirements, ensuring cost-competitiveness versus individual systems[^3].

- **Social and Political:**  
  Securing resident trust and buy-in (especially moving from individual to collective heating), managing expectations, billing and control issues[^3].

- **Administrative:**  
  Coordination among public, private, and community actors; regulatory hurdles for new DHC models; capacity building for city staff[^3][^1].

- **Environmental:**  
  Sourcing sustainable biomass, minimizing disruption during network construction.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  Iterative scenario analysis to refine network layouts, targeted outreach to anchor clients (e.g., public buildings), flexible business models[^3][^1].

- **Design Modifications:**  
  Adjustments to network phasing, inclusion of additional renewables, responsive tariff structures.

- **Lessons Learned:**  
  Importance of granular data, early and continuous stakeholder engagement, and the need for strong public sector leadership in novel infrastructure projects[^3][^1].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**  
  New or expanded DHC networks, retrofitted residential and public buildings, integrated energy management systems[^3][^4].

- **Service Delivery:**  
  Reliable, lower-carbon heat supply; improved comfort and air quality for residents.

- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  Example: Coronación pilot retrofitted 26 buildings (524 dwellings), reducing energy demand by 3 GWh/year and emissions by 1,000 tCO₂e/year[^4].

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  Lower energy bills, improved indoor comfort, public health co-benefits (air quality).

- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  Targeting of vulnerable districts and households, potential reduction in energy poverty.

- **Displacement and Gentrification:**  
  No significant displacement reported, but ongoing monitoring required.

- **Social Capital:**  
  Increased civic engagement through participatory planning processes.

- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  Strengthening of local identity as a green and innovative city[^3][^4].

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  Construction, retrofit, and ongoing network maintenance roles.

- **Economic Activity:**  
  Stimulus for local supply chains (e.g., biomass providers, construction firms).

- **Property Values:**  
  Potential uplift due to improved infrastructure and comfort.

- **Cost Savings:**  
  Long-term reduction in user energy costs and municipal energy expenditures.

- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  Focus on equitable access to efficient energy solutions[^3][^4].

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  Significant GHG reduction, progress toward city’s carbon neutrality targets.

- **Resource Management:**  
  Efficient use of local renewables (biomass, solar), improved energy system resilience.

- **Environmental Quality:**  
  Improved air quality, reduced fossil fuel use, potential reduction in urban heat island effect.

- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  Positive impacts from waste biomass utilization and lower emissions.

- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  Performance tracked via KPIs for efficiency, renewable share, and emissions[^3][^4].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  Enhanced skills and tools for municipal energy planners.

- **Policy Influence:**  
  Model for other Spanish and European cities; input into regional and national guidelines.

- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  Open data, participatory planning, and KPI-driven monitoring[^1][^3].

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  Achieved key targets for retrofit, renewable integration, and emission reduction in pilot areas.

- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  Need for ongoing user education and support; some initial resistance to collective heating.

- **Long-term Viability:**  
  System designed for scalability and financial sustainability; further expansion planned[^3][^1][^4].

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- Pioneering use of open-source, high-resolution energy planning tools.
- Strong alignment with climate and social policy objectives.
- Effective stakeholder engagement and participatory governance.
- Demonstrated emission reduction, cost savings, and improved quality of life.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- Data collection and scenario modeling are resource-intensive.
- Early resistance from residents unfamiliar with collective heating.
- Financial model reliant on sustained user base and ongoing policy support.
- Complexity in integrating new systems with legacy infrastructure[^3].

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Positive focus on vulnerable populations and energy poverty.
- Participatory processes promoted inclusion, but ongoing vigilance required to ensure equitable benefit distribution.

#### **Future Outlook**

- Continued expansion and optimization of DHC networks citywide.
- Potential for further integration of renewables and digital controls.
- Model likely to influence national and European policy.
- Ongoing monitoring of social acceptance, financial sustainability, and environmental impact needed for long-term success[^3][^4][^1][^2].
