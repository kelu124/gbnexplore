# Executive summary## READY Project: Integrated Energy Retrofitting Demonstrator (READY Site Aarhus – Integrated Smart City Energy Solutions)

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** READY – Resource Efficient Cities Implementing ADvanced Smart CitY Solutions (Aarhus Demonstrator)
- **Alternative Names:** READY Site Aarhus, READY Project Aarhus Demonstrator
- **Location:** Aarhus, Denmark (primarily in neighborhoods adjacent to the new University Hospital and Ringgården)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 56.1629° N, 10.2039° E (center of Aarhus)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Project Start: 2014
  - Key Milestones: Building envelope retrofits, low-temperature district heating deployment, installation of PV-thermal modules, waste heat integration, smart grid pilots (2015–2018)
  - Completion/Ongoing Status: Demonstration phase concluded; ongoing monitoring and knowledge dissemination[^1][^2]
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Area: Several neighborhoods, including social housing, administrative and private buildings (exact area not specified)
  - Population Affected: Several thousand residents directly; citywide implications for 362,000 inhabitants[^1][^3]
  - Investment Value: €33,340,202.60 (total project across sites; Aarhus share significant but not isolated)[^2]
- **Leading Organization(s):** 
  - COWI A/S (Coordinator)
  - Aarhus Municipality
  - Aarhus University
  - Boligforeningen Ringgården (Housing Association)
  - Kredsløb (district heating and waste utility)
  - Several technology, energy, and manufacturing partners (Danfoss, Kamstrup, Dong Energy, E.ON Denmark, etc.)[^2][^3]
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - Predominantly European Union funding (FP7), national and municipal contributions, private sector partners[^2]

#### **Executive Summary**

The READY Project’s Aarhus demonstrator is a flagship initiative for **deep urban energy retrofitting and integrated smart city energy solutions**. As part of Aarhus’s ambition to become carbon neutral by 2030, the project retrofitted residential and administrative buildings, implemented **low-temperature district heating**, integrated **PV-thermal hybrid systems**, and deployed **smart grid controls**. The demonstrator sought to achieve **near-zero fossil energy demand** and substantial **CO₂ reductions**, targeting a replicable model for other European cities[^1][^2]. 

Among its innovations were **building envelope upgrades**, **waste heat recovery** (including from the University Hospital’s cooling system and residential wastewater), and **battery storage** pilots using second-life EV batteries. The demonstrator featured advanced **system integration**, with surplus cooling energy routed to the district heating grid and smart controls optimizing energy flows. The project’s outcomes include an annual **primary energy reduction of 10,938 MWh** and **CO₂ savings of 2,706 tonnes**, alongside improved comfort and lower energy bills for residents[^1].

Challenges involved coordinating across building types, technical integration of diverse energy systems, and stakeholder alignment. Nevertheless, the READY Aarhus site stands as a strong example of **systematic evaluation of design alternatives**, **district-scale optimization**, and **real-time performance feedback**, with significant potential for replication in similar urban contexts across Europe[^2].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Aarhus is Denmark’s second largest city, with a population of approximately 362,000, growing at 1.5% annually[^3]. It is a regional economic and educational hub, home to Aarhus University and a diverse industrial, cultural, and service economy. The city is situated on the east coast of Jutland, with a mild maritime climate. Aarhus is known for its progressive sustainability and climate policies, aiming for carbon neutrality by 2030[^1][^3].

- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:** The READY interventions focused on neighborhoods with a mix of **social housing (Ringgården)**, **administrative buildings**, and **single-family homes**, adjacent to the new **Aarhus University Hospital**. These areas reflect:
  - **Historical Patterns:** Mixed-use, postwar development with substantial multi-family housing.
  - **Land Use:** Residential, institutional, and some commercial/institutional mix.
  - **Demographics:** Includes elderly, families, students (especially near the hospital and university), and lower-income groups in social housing.
  - **Infrastructure:** Already high district heating penetration (>90%), but aging building stock needing energy upgrades; robust public services and transport.
  - **Environmental Conditions:** Moderate climate, but with energy challenges related to older buildings and heating needs.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High energy demand and fossil fuel dependency in existing buildings.
  - CO₂ emissions from heating and electricity.
  - Need for integration of renewable energy sources and smart technologies.
  - Lack of optimized energy flows between heating, cooling, and electricity systems[^1][^2].

- **Triggering Events:**
  - Aarhus’s strategic commitment to carbon neutrality (2030 goal)[^3].
  - EU policy incentives for smart city and energy innovation.
  - Local institutional push for demonstration of replicable low-carbon solutions.

- **Stakeholder Landscape:** 
  - **Municipality:** Climate and energy planners, utility managers.
  - **Residents:** Social and private housing occupants, concerned with comfort and bills.
  - **Utilities:** Kredsløb (district heating/waste), electricity companies.
  - **Industry:** Tech providers (Danfoss, Kamstrup, etc.), consultants.
  - **Academia:** Aarhus University, research and monitoring partners.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** Aarhus Municipality holds authority for energy and climate planning, with a tradition of democratic, integrated urban governance[^3].
- **Relevant Policies:** 
  - National and municipal climate action plans (carbon neutrality by 2030).
  - Danish district heating regulation (support for renewables, not-for-profit operation).
  - EU directives on energy efficiency and renewable integration.
- **Legal/Institutional Enablers:** 
  - Strong municipal ownership and control over utilities.
  - Supportive national regulatory environment for district heating and building retrofits.
  - Complexities in aligning policies across electricity, heating, and building codes.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Achieve **deep energy savings** and **CO₂ emissions reductions** in urban neighborhoods.
  - Develop and demonstrate **integrated, replicable solutions** for building and district energy systems.
  - Enable transition to **near-zero fossil energy demand** through smart system design[^1][^2].

- **Vision Statement:** To establish a scalable model of **resource-efficient, climate-neutral urban energy systems** for European cities, with Aarhus as a leading demonstrator.

- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** Directly supports Aarhus’s and Denmark’s carbon neutrality goals; aligns with EU smart city and energy transition directives.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:** Upgrading buildings and integrating smart, renewable-powered district energy systems will drive down energy use, reduce CO₂, and provide affordable, resilient energy services, setting a model for urban decarbonization[^2].
- **Target Beneficiaries:** Residents (esp. social housing), municipal buildings, broader city population (via knowledge transfer and system upgrades).
- **Innovation Elements:** 
  - **Technical:** Hybrid PV-thermal modules, waste heat recovery, smart grid controls, second-life battery storage.
  - **Integration:** Linking building retrofits to district-scale energy flows for both heating and cooling.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Strong municipal climate leadership; policy mandates for carbon reduction.
- **Economic:** Cost savings for residents, reduced energy imports, local job creation in retrofit and tech sectors.
- **Social:** Improved living conditions, air quality, and affordability.
- **Environmental:** Urgency of climate mitigation; need for circular resource use (e.g., waste heat).
- **Technological:** Maturity of district heating, emergence of smart grid and renewable integration technologies.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Whole City Approach—holistic, integrated planning of energy systems and building retrofits[^2].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** 
  - Retrofit of building envelopes (insulation, windows, etc.).
  - Deployment of low-temperature district heating infrastructure.
  - Installation of PV-thermal modules as roofing.
  - Heat recovery and storage systems linked to hospitals and residential districts.
- **Scope and Phasing:** 
  - Initial pilots in select neighborhoods and buildings.
  - Sequential integration of energy systems (retrofit, then heating/cooling, then smart controls).

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Building Envelope Retrofits:** Insulation, window upgrades.
  - **Low-Temperature District Heating:** Supply for both new and retrofitted buildings.
  - **PV-Thermal Modules:** Large-format, frameless hybrid solar panels as roofing material.
  - **Waste Heat Recovery:** Heat from hospital cooling and residential wastewater channeled to district heating.
  - **Smart Grid Controls:** Real-time monitoring, demand response.
  - **Battery Storage:** Use of second-life EV batteries for energy storage and grid balancing[^1][^2].
  - **Electric Mobility:** EV charging infrastructure integrated with the energy system.

- **Innovation Type:** Technological (energy systems, controls), social (resident engagement), process (whole city/district integration).
- **Integration Approach:** Close coupling of building and district systems; real-time optimization and feedback loops.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement/Contracting:** Competitive tendering for technology providers and construction; partnership model with clear roles for each sector.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** 
  - Multi-actor steering groups (municipality, housing, tech, academia).
  - Workshops and feedback sessions for residents and facility managers.
- **Participatory Elements:** Resident information campaigns, performance dashboards, feedback on comfort and costs.
- **Project Management:** Led by COWI A/S, with dedicated project managers, technical work packages, and regular cross-partner coordination[^2].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget:** €33.3 million (total project; Aarhus share significant).
- **Funding Mix:** EU FP7 grant, national/municipal contributions, private partner in-kind and cash investments[^2].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Cost-based tariffs for district heating; pilot studies for new business models (e.g., battery storage, EV integration).
- **Financial Sustainability:** Long-term cost savings from reduced energy use; infrastructure designed for 20+ year lifespan.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Integrating diverse new and existing energy systems; ensuring seamless operation of hybrid PV-thermal and waste heat systems; retrofitting buildings without major disruption[^1][^2].
- **Financial:** Upfront capital costs, aligning funding timelines, ensuring long-term cost-effectiveness.
- **Social/Political:** Coordinating multiple stakeholders; achieving buy-in from residents, especially in social housing.
- **Administrative:** Managing complex project partnerships, aligning with existing utility operations.
- **Environmental:** Ensuring waste heat systems do not create unforeseen environmental impacts.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving:** Iterative pilot testing; phased deployment to address technical issues before scaling up.
- **Design Modifications:** Adjustments to heat recovery systems based on building and user feedback; flexibility in PV-thermal module configurations.
- **Lessons Learned:** Importance of early and sustained stakeholder engagement; need for robust monitoring and feedback systems[^1].
