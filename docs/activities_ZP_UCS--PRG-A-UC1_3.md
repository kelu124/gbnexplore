# Executive summary## Energy Consumption Measurement Digitization – Smart Prague Initiative

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Energy Consumption Measurement Digitization (ECMD)
- **Alternative Names:** Smart Prague – Energy Digitization Project; Remote Metering for Municipal Buildings
- **Location:** Prague, Czech Republic (citywide, focused on municipal buildings)
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 50.0755° N, 14.4378° E (Prague city center)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Start: Circa 2017 (Smart Prague program launch)
  - Key Milestones: Pilot phase (2022–2023); city-wide scaling (ongoing as of 2024)
  - Status: Ongoing, with progressive expansion and integration
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Pilot: 80 municipal buildings (schools, retirement homes, offices, cultural centers)
  - City Portfolio: Over 1,200 city-managed buildings targeted
  - Population Affected: Hundreds of thousands (directly via public facilities; indirectly citywide)
  - Investment Value: Not publicly disclosed; significant public-sector investment
- **Leading Organization(s):** Operátor ICT, a.s. (OICT, municipal ICT company), Prague City Council, Environmental Protection and Energy Manager’s Departments
- **Funding Sources:** Public funding (city budget), potential for future PPP involvement

#### **Executive Summary**
The Energy Consumption Measurement Digitization (ECMD) initiative, under the Smart Prague program, seeks to transform the city’s management of energy use in public-sector buildings. By deploying a digital, remote metering infrastructure and centralized data analytics platform, Prague aims to gain real-time and historical insights into the energy and water consumption patterns of its diverse building stock. The project’s pilot phase included 80 municipal buildings and demonstrated the value of granular, timely data for optimizing resource use, informing retrofits, and prioritizing investment in energy-efficiency measures.

Key features include a citywide digital energy management platform, integration of legacy and new smart meters, and the development of facility assessment methodologies and a building atlas. The system supports city leadership in targeting high-impact interventions, reducing operational costs, and advancing sustainability policy objectives. Early outcomes indicate improved transparency, identification of significant savings potential, and enhanced institutional capacity for data-driven decision-making. The ECMD initiative is positioned as a cornerstone of Prague’s broader ambitions to become a model green city, leveraging smart technologies to meet climate, fiscal, and governance targets[^1].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**
- **Prague** is the capital and largest city of the Czech Republic, with a population of approximately 1.3 million.
- Economic base: Service-oriented, with strengths in tourism, technology, and creative industries.
- Geography: Set on the Vltava River, featuring historic urban fabric, mixed-use neighborhoods, and significant green space.
- Regional/national role: Political, economic, and cultural center of Central Europe.

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
- The project targets a **diverse array of municipal buildings** scattered citywide, including heritage zones.
- Historical patterns: Many public assets are located in central districts, often in protected historical areas, which creates constraints for physical retrofits.
- Land use: Public facilities are integrated into mixed-use neighborhoods.
- Demographics: Buildings serve a cross-section of Prague’s residents—students, elderly, municipal workers—across socioeconomic spectra.
- Infrastructure: Varied, with some modernized facilities and others with outdated utilities and inefficient systems.
- Environmental conditions: Urban heat island effects, energy price volatility, and pressure for decarbonization.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**
- High and rising energy costs for city-owned properties.
- Fragmented, manual, or outdated metering systems; limited access to real-time consumption data.
- Difficulty in identifying and prioritizing energy-saving investments.
- Policy imperative to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and comply with EU sustainability directives[^1].

**Triggering Events:**
- Energy market volatility and price spikes (especially post-2021).
- City’s climate commitments and public-sector efficiency mandates.
- Technological opportunities arising from smart city trends.

**Stakeholder Landscape:**
- **Primary:** City government (departments of environment, ICT, facilities)
- **Secondary:** Building occupants (residents, staff), technology providers, academic collaborators (CTU, UCEEB), and Prague’s broader citizenry.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**
- Led and overseen by Prague City Council, with OICT as technical lead.
- Collaboration with academic and specialist partners for methodology development.

**Relevant Policies:**
- Smart Prague program (citywide digital innovation strategy)
- Czech and EU energy efficiency and climate action legislation.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**
- Regulations on data privacy, building interventions (especially in heritage areas), procurement complexity.
- Institutional support for smart infrastructure but challenges in integrating with legacy systems[^1].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:**
- **Digitize energy and water consumption measurement** in city buildings.
- Enable real-time and historical monitoring and analytics.
- Identify and realize energy savings; reduce costs and emissions.

**Vision Statement:**
- To create a **transparent, data-driven municipal energy management system** that empowers decision-makers, optimizes resource use, and advances Prague’s sustainability leadership.

**Alignment with Broader Plans:**
- Directly supports Smart Prague, Prague’s Climate Plan, and EU commitments for low-carbon urban development.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:**
- Digital metering → granular, timely data → targeted retrofits/investments → lower consumption and costs → climate and fiscal benefits.

**Target Beneficiaries:**
- City government (budget savings, operational efficiency)
- Building users (improved comfort, reliability)
- Citizens (environmental quality, fiscal stewardship)

**Innovation Elements:**
- Centralized, city-scale digital analytics platform.
- Integration of real-time and historical data for complex portfolios.
- Methodology for cross-asset assessment and prioritization.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** City leadership’s commitment to climate and digital transformation.
- **Economic:** Rising utility costs, need for fiscal discipline.
- **Social:** Public demand for transparency, stewardship, and climate action.
- **Environmental:** EU and city climate targets; need to reduce emissions.
- **Technological:** Growing maturity of metering, IoT, and big data solutions[^1][^2].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**
- Smart city principles (data-driven governance, digital infrastructure).
- Energy ecosystem model—holistic, interconnected facility management[^1].

**Physical/Spatial Design:**
- Deployment of remote metering devices (electricity, gas, heat, water) in diverse building types.
- Creation of a digital “building atlas” for technical, consumption, and potential data[^1].

**Scope and Phasing:**
- Pilot: 80 buildings (2022–2023), diverse in age, type, and location.
- Planned scaling to all 1,200 municipal buildings.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**
- Remote, digital meters for energy and water.
- Centralized data platform (Golemio) for real-time analytics and visualization.
- Custom facility assessment methodology and online dashboards.

**Innovation Type:**
- Primarily technological (IoT, data analytics), with governance/process innovations (cross-departmental data sharing, new assessment models).

**Integration Approach:**
- Gradual replacement/upgrade of legacy meters.
- Automated data feeds to city dashboards.
- Interoperability focus to accommodate varied building systems[^1][^2].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:**
- Public procurement for device and platform supply.
- Collaboration with academic partners for methodology development.

**Stakeholder Engagement:**
- Internal: City staff (technical, managerial).
- External: Academic experts, technology providers.

**Participatory Elements:**
- Limited direct public engagement; transparency via public dashboards.

**Project Management:**
- Led by OICT, with cross-departmental coordination and regular reporting to city leadership.

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:**
- Not fully disclosed; public-sector funded, with costs for hardware, software, and staffing.

**Funding Mix:**
- Predominantly municipal funds; potential for leveraging national/EU grants.

**Revenue Mechanisms:**
- Not revenue-generating; cost savings anticipated from reduced utility bills.

**Financial Sustainability:**
- Long-term savings expected to justify capital outlay; financial model under development for reinvestment of savings into further retrofits[^1].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** Integrating new meters with old infrastructure; interoperability with varied legacy systems.
- **Financial:** Upfront investment needs; demonstrating ROI to justify public expenditure.
- **Social/Political:** Navigating heritage protection regulations; gaining buy-in across city departments.
- **Administrative:** Coordinating multiple departments and building managers.
- **Environmental:** Retrofit restrictions in conservation zones; balancing technical upgrades with preservation needs[^1].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Developed a tailored assessment methodology to account for building diversity and regulatory constraints.
- **Design Modifications:** In protected buildings, focused on internal equipment upgrades rather than envelope interventions.
- **Lessons Learned:** Need for robust data integration, cross-departmental planning, and flexible adaptation to building constraints.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Comprehensive, data-driven approach** to municipal energy management.
- **Integration of legacy and new systems** via flexible methodology.
- **Transparency and accountability** through public dashboards.
- **Strong institutional collaboration** between ICT, environment, and academic sectors.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Limited direct public engagement**; benefits are largely internal to city operations.
- **Upfront investment costs** may be a barrier in less affluent contexts.
- **Complexity of integrating legacy systems** and regulatory hurdles in heritage zones.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Broad-based public benefits, but limited targeted equity interventions.
- No evidence of negative impacts on vulnerable populations; potential to extend benefits via public-facing tools and education.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Planned expansion** to all city-managed buildings.
- **Continuous improvement** of data analytics and assessment tools.
- **Potential for integration** with broader urban data platforms and smart city initiatives.
- **Ongoing challenges:** Ensuring interoperability, scaling up retrofits, maintaining stakeholder engagement.
