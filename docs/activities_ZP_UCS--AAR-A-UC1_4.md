# Executive summary## Spørring Circular Energy Park – CO-SHAPE Project

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Spørring Circular Energy Park – CO-SHAPE Project
- **Alternative Names:** CO-SHAPE Pilot, Spørring Energy Park, NetZeroCities Pilot in Aarhus
- **Location:** Spørring, peri-urban village, City of Aarhus, Central Denmark Region, Denmark
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 56.3°N, 10.0°E (Spørring, Aarhus Municipality)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Concept and planning: 2022–2023
  - Pilot launch: 2023
  - Major implementation: 2024–2025 
  - Ongoing, with key milestones aligning with Aarhus’ climate neutrality target by 2030[^1][^2].
- **Project Scale:** Approx. 1,000 hectares; affects hundreds of residents and landowners directly; part of a municipal strategy impacting broader Aarhus (pop. ~350,000)[^1][^2].
- **Leading Organization(s):** Aarhus Municipality, in partnership with local citizen groups, landowners, energy developers, and the NetZeroCities Pilot Cities Programme; includes Aarhus School of Architecture and various technical partners[^1][^2].
- **Funding Sources:** Mixed public funding (municipal, national, EU through NetZeroCities), private sector investment (energy developers, agribusiness), and in-kind contributions from research and civil society partners[^1][^2].

#### **Executive Summary**
The Spørring Circular Energy Park, under the CO-SHAPE Project, is a pioneering initiative in Denmark’s second-largest city, Aarhus, aiming to transform a peri-urban village into a model for circular, low-emission urban energy systems. The project integrates multiple renewable energy and resource recovery technologies—including biogas, pyrolysis, solar PV, and Power-to-X (conversion of green hydrogen and CO₂ into synthetic fuels)—within a 1,000-hectare landscape. What distinguishes Spørring is not just its technical ambition but its governance innovation: decision-making is shared among citizens, developers, landowners, and municipal authorities through participatory “solution rooms” and digital planning platforms, making the process highly iterative and responsive to local needs and concerns.

This comprehensive approach addresses both climate mitigation and social acceptance, directly targeting barriers like NIMBYism (Not-in-My-Backyard attitudes), fragmented planning, and the need for new models of cross-sectoral collaboration. The anticipated impact is substantial: an annual reduction of over 100,000 tonnes of CO₂, improved local air and soil quality, creation of green jobs, and enhanced resilience to climate and market shocks. The project is designed as a replicable model, with its outputs, governance frameworks, and technical playbooks being shared for adaptation by other cities across Europe. Spørring’s experience is already influencing municipal policy in Aarhus and offers transferable lessons for cities seeking to combine technical innovation with participatory, equitable urban transformation[^1][^2].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

- **City Profile:** Aarhus, population ~350,000, is Denmark’s second-largest city and a regional hub for education (notably Aarhus University), culture, and innovation. The city is economically diverse with strengths in logistics, food/agriculture, IT, and green technology. Its geography is defined by a mix of urban core, peri-urban villages, agricultural hinterland, and significant coastal and forested landscapes, fostering a high quality of life and progressive environmental policies[^2][^3].
- **Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
  - **Historical development:** Spørring is a small, historically agricultural village north of Aarhus, now at the urban-rural interface. It has seen gradual suburbanization but retains a rural character, with scattered housing, farms, and industrial-scale biogas production.
  - **Current land use:** Mix of farmland, small settlements, renewable energy facilities (notably Bånlev Biogas), and some natural habitats.
  - **Demographics:** Predominantly Danish, with a mix of farming families, recent in-migrants, and some aging population; moderate to upper-middle income, but with rural-urban diversity.
  - **Infrastructure:** Basic utilities and road access; existing biogas infrastructure; limited public transport; variable digital connectivity.
  - **Environmental conditions:** Fertile soils, moderate climate, but with sustainability challenges related to agricultural runoff, emissions, and land-use conflicts[^1][^4].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
  - Reliance on fossil energy and need for rapid decarbonization to meet Aarhus’ 2030 net-zero target.
  - Public opposition to new energy infrastructure (NIMBYism), especially in peri-urban/rural settings.
  - Fragmented governance and limited mechanisms for cross-sectoral, cross-departmental energy planning.
  - Underutilized synergies between agriculture, energy, and land management.
  - Social mistrust and lack of co-ownership in major development projects[^1][^2].
- **Triggering Events:**  
  - Adoption of Aarhus’ ambitious 2030 climate neutrality goal and related climate action plans[^3][^5].
  - Need to scale up biogas and other renewables as part of Denmark’s national energy transition.
  - Community concerns over the expansion of Bånlev Biogas and related land use[^4].
- **Stakeholder Landscape:**  
  - Local residents and landowners (concerned about quality of life, property values, and environmental impacts).
  - Farmers and agribusinesses (interested in new value streams and sustainable practices).
  - Energy developers and technology providers (seeking investment opportunities).
  - Municipal authorities and technical experts (responsible for planning, regulation, and public engagement)[^1][^2].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** Aarhus Municipality has strong authority over land use, energy planning, and citizen engagement, operating within Danish national frameworks for energy, environment, and spatial planning[^1][^3].
- **Relevant Policies:**  
  - Aarhus Climate Strategy 2020–2030 and associated four-year climate plans[^3][^5].
  - Danish national energy policy (targets for renewables, biogas, Power-to-X, resource circularity).
  - Land use and zoning regulations facilitating renewable energy and agricultural transitions.
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
  - Supportive legal frameworks for renewable energy.
  - Challenges: complexity of land use change, need for multi-level coordination, and sometimes conflicting property rights or local opposition[^1][^2].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**  
  - Achieve significant CO₂ emission reductions (>100,000 tonnes/year).
  - Integrate multiple renewable energy and circular economy technologies in a peri-urban context.
  - Develop and test new participatory governance models for energy and land-use planning.
  - Create scalable, transferable models and toolkits for other European cities[^1][^2].
- **Vision Statement:**  
  - To co-create a climate-neutral, resilient, and socially inclusive peri-urban district that serves as a European reference for circular energy and participatory urban transformation.
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
  - Directly supports Aarhus’ 2030 net-zero goals and Denmark’s national energy and climate strategies.
  - Contributes to the EU Mission for 100 Climate-Neutral and Smart Cities[^2][^3].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:**  
  - By integrating diverse energy and resource recovery technologies with participatory governance, the project expects to overcome technical, social, and institutional barriers to rapid decarbonization.
  - Iterative co-creation ensures local acceptance, minimizes conflict, and increases system efficiency.
- **Target Beneficiaries:**  
  - Local residents (improved environment, jobs, influence over development).
  - Farmers and landowners (new economic opportunities, sustainable practices).
  - Municipal government (meeting climate targets, innovation leadership).
  - Broader city and region (replicable models, knowledge transfer)[^2].
- **Innovation Elements:**  
  - Holistic integration of biogas, pyrolysis, solar, Power-to-X, and resource loops.
  - Participatory digital planning and “solution rooms.”
  - Transferable playbooks and governance innovations[^1][^2].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** Strong municipal leadership, climate neutrality commitments, citizen expectations[^3].
- **Economic:** Attract investment, create green jobs, futureproof agriculture and energy sectors[^2].
- **Social:** Build trust, overcome NIMBYism, empower local communities[^2].
- **Environmental:** Decarbonization, soil and air quality improvement, circular resource management[^1][^2].
- **Technological:** Leverage new renewable energy and digital engagement platforms[^2].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:**  
  - Comprehensive, landscape-scale planning integrating energy, agriculture, and land use.
  - Participatory democracy and governance innovation at the core[^1][^2].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:**  
  - Cluster of renewable energy facilities (biogas, pyrolysis, solar PV, Power-to-X).
  - Landscape modifications to facilitate resource flows and minimize impact.
  - Adaptive land use for grass protein production, biochar application, and biodiversity enhancement[^2].
- **Scope and Phasing:**  
  - Pilot phase: focus on technical planning, community engagement, and initial facility integration (2023–2025).
  - Scaling phase: expand capacity, transfer model to other districts/municipalities (2025+)[^1][^2].

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**  
  - Biogas production from agricultural residues and organic waste.
  - Pyrolysis for biochar (carbon sequestration and soil health).
  - Solar PV arrays for renewable electricity.
  - Power-to-X: production of e-fuels using green hydrogen and captured CO₂.
  - Digital participatory planning tools for scenario-building and feedback[^1][^2].
- **Innovation Type:**  
  - Technological (integrated energy systems, Power-to-X).
  - Social/governance (co-creation, participatory decision-making).
  - Process (cross-sectoral planning, iterative design).
- **Integration Approach:**  
  - Sector coupling (energy, agriculture, transport).
  - Circular flows (waste-to-energy, biochar to soil, heat recovery).
  - Close coordination with existing infrastructure (e.g., Bånlev Biogas)[^2][^4].

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement and Contracting:**  
  - Public tendering and selection of energy technology providers.
  - Partnership agreements with landowners, citizen groups, and agribusinesses.
- **Stakeholder Engagement:**  
  - “Solution rooms”: iterative workshops involving residents, developers, and officials.
  - Participatory digital platforms for scenario visualization and feedback.
- **Participatory Elements:**  
  - Co-creation embedded in all phases; local input shapes facility siting, landscape design, and governance rules.
- **Project Management:**  
  - Led by Aarhus Municipality, with cross-departmental teams and external technical experts.
  - Regular monitoring, evaluation, and knowledge sharing with other cities[^1][^2].

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:**  
  - Detailed figures not public; significant investment in energy infrastructure, planning, and engagement.
  - Example: Aarhus investing DKK 8.4 billion in citywide energy transition (not Spørring-specific)[^3].
- **Funding Mix:**  
  - EU NetZeroCities grant, municipal budgets, private developer investment, and in-kind contributions[^1][^2].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:**  
  - Energy sales (biogas, electricity, e-fuels).
  - Potential user fees for energy or land use.
- **Financial Sustainability:**  
  - Focus on creating investable, operationally viable energy systems.
  - Long-term revenue through circular value chains and energy sales[^2].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:**  
  - Complexity of integrating diverse energy and agricultural technologies.
  - Managing emissions, odors, and noise from energy facilities[^2].
- **Financial:**  
  - Upfront capital requirements; ensuring reliable revenue flows.
  - Aligning public and private investment timelines.
- **Social and Political:**  
  - Overcoming NIMBYism and local skepticism.
  - Negotiating trade-offs between land uses and stakeholder interests.
- **Administrative:**  
  - Cross-departmental coordination; breaking down municipal silos.
  - Learning new participatory governance models[^1][^2].
- **Environmental:**  
  - Managing unintended impacts (e.g., landscape disruption, biodiversity loss).
  - Ensuring true circularity and sustainability of resource flows.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
  - Use of “solution rooms” for real-time conflict resolution.
  - Flexible planning and scenario-building to adjust to emerging issues.
- **Design Modifications:**  
  - Facility siting and design adapted in response to resident feedback.
  - Enhanced mitigation measures for odor/noise.
- **Lessons Learned:**  
  - Early and continuous engagement is key to acceptance.
  - Governance innovation is as challenging as technical innovation.
  - Institutional change is slow but possible; pilot now influencing wider municipal policy[^2].

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:**  
  - Integrated cluster of renewable energy facilities (biogas plant, pyrolysis, solar PV, Power-to-X in planning).
  - Improved landscape design to support circular flows and biodiversity.
- **Service Delivery:**  
  - Reliable provision of renewable energy and bio-based products.
  - New participatory planning services for local residents.
- **Quantitative Metrics:**  
  - >100,000 tonnes CO₂ equivalent avoided per year (approx. 10% of city emissions)[^2].
  - 1,000 hectares transformed into a model circular energy landscape.

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:**  
  - Improved air and soil quality.
  - Increased trust in local government and planning processes.
- **Equity Outcomes:**  
  - Deliberate inclusion of diverse resident and stakeholder voices.
  - No evidence (to date) of displacement or significant gentrification.
- **Social Capital:**  
  - Strengthened networks among residents, farmers, and municipal officials.
- **Cultural Impacts:**  
  - Some tension over landscape change, but growing identification with “green pioneer” status.

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:**  
  - Green jobs in construction, operations, and agriculture.
- **Economic Activity:**  
  - New value streams from bio-based products and energy sales.
- **Property Values:**  
  - No data yet; potential for both positive (futureproofing) and negative (proximity to facilities) effects.
- **Cost Savings:**  
  - Reduced energy costs for some participants; system-level efficiencies.
- **Economic Inclusion:**  
  - Efforts to spread benefits among local stakeholders; ongoing monitoring.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:**  
  - Major contribution to city and national decarbonization goals.
- **Resource Management:**  
  - Closed-loop systems for nutrients and energy.
- **Environmental Quality:**  
  - Improved soil health (biochar); reduced air pollution; enhanced ecosystem services.
- **Waste and Pollution:**  
  - Reduced organic waste through biogas and pyrolysis.
- **Sustainability Metrics:**  
  - Project aligned with EU climate and sustainability standards; ongoing monitoring and reporting[^1][^2].

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:**  
  - Enhanced municipal capabilities in integrated, participatory planning.
- **Policy Influence:**  
  - Successful elements embedded in Aarhus’ climate action plans; co-creation now required for new energy projects[^2].
- **Transparency and Accountability:**  
  - New participatory mechanisms improve transparency and trust.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:**  
  - Significant progress on emissions, participation, and governance innovation.
- **Unintended Consequences:**  
  - Some resistance to landscape/land use changes; ongoing management of conflicts.
- **Long-term Viability:**  
  - Strong potential for sustainability, with learnings being transferred to other districts and cities.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

- **Contextual Dependencies:**  
  - Success depends on supportive policy, institutional willingness for collaboration, and a peri-urban context with mixed land uses.
- **Critical Success Factors:**  
  - Early, deep stakeholder engagement.
  - Strong municipal leadership.
  - Integration of technical and governance innovation[^1][^2].
- **Scalability:**  
  - Model already being adopted in five neighboring municipalities and designed for European replication.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

| Feature                   | Spørring CO-SHAPE   | Comparable European Projects         |
|---------------------------|---------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Governance Model          | Deep co-creation, iterative solution rooms | More top-down or consultative (e.g., Freiburg Vauban, Amsterdam Buiksloterham) |
| Technical Integration     | Multi-energy, Power-to-X, circular flows | Often single-technology or less integrated |
| Policy Context            | Aggressive climate targets, municipal leadership | Variable; often slower national or regional drivers |
| Transferability           | Explicit playbook and replication | Variable; many lack formal transfer mechanisms |

- **Best Practices:**  
  - Participatory governance, sector coupling, scenario-based planning, and knowledge sharing.
- **Cautionary Lessons:**  
  - Change is slow and requires ongoing adaptation; NIMBYism and institutional inertia are persistent challenges.

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Holistic Integration:** Technical, social, and policy innovation combined for greater impact.
- **Participatory Governance:** Deep engagement builds legitimacy and trust.
- **Replicability:** Explicit knowledge transfer and playbook development position Spørring as a European model[^1][^2].

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Complexity:** High coordination costs and slow pace due to depth of engagement.
- **Potential for Conflict:** Not all stakeholders are equally empowered; balancing competing interests is ongoing.
- **Data Gaps:** Long-term outcomes (e.g., on property values, economic inclusion) remain to be evaluated.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- **Inclusive Engagement:** Strong focus on participation and distribution of benefits.
- **Justice Risks:** Potential for unequal influence or benefit if engagement is not maintained or broadened.

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Long-term Trajectory:** Movement towards full-scale, operational circular energy park; further institutionalization of co-creation in city policy.
- **Ongoing Challenges:** Maintaining engagement, scaling technical solutions, monitoring for unintended impacts.
- **Next Phases:** Broader replication across Aarhus and other European regions; refinement of playbooks and metrics for monitoring success[^1][^2].

*This case study demonstrates how technical innovation in green energy must be matched by innovation in governance and participation to deliver sustainable, equitable urban transformation. The Spørring Circular Energy Park is a flagship example of these principles in action, offering rich lessons for cities worldwide.*
