# Executive summary## Eco-Canteen Food Waste Management Initiative (Barcelona, Spain)

---

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Eco-Canteen Food Waste Management
- **Alternative Names:** Eco-Canteen Initiative, Barcelona School Canteen Food Waste Project
- **Location:** Barcelona, Metropolitan Region, Catalonia, Spain
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 41.3851° N, 2.1734° E (Barcelona central)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Pilot launch: circa 2019–2020
  - Key milestones: Digital monitoring system installation, staff training, behavioral nudge implementation in 2020–2021
  - Status: Ongoing, with expansion to additional public canteens post-pilot
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Initial pilot: 4 public sector school canteens, ~2,900 pupils, ~10,000 meals tracked[^1]
  - Potential reach: Citywide school and public cafeteria network
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - Barcelona City Council (Ajuntament de Barcelona)
  - Center for Agri-Food Economy and Development (CRED-UPC-IRTA)
  - Fundació Espigoladors (food waste advocacy NGO)
  - Collaborating schools and catering companies[^2][^1]
- **Funding Sources:** 
  - Municipal government budget (Barcelona City Council)
  - Regional support (Catalan government sustainability grants)
  - Project-specific research funding (EU/academic grants for circular economy pilots)

#### **Executive Summary**
The Eco-Canteen Food Waste Management Initiative in Barcelona represents a pioneering city-led intervention designed to combat food waste in public cafeterias—especially school canteens—by leveraging digital monitoring, behavioral science, and real-time feedback. Through installation of plate waste tracking devices and dashboard analytics, the system quantifies leftover food and provides actionable insights to both diners and staff. The project employs behavioral nudges—such as informative signage and staff engagement—to influence meal choices, portion selection, and waste disposal habits. Regular data collection enables staff to monitor trends in waste generation, nutritional intake, and menu popularity, guiding adjustments to food offerings and portion sizes.

The initiative is situated within Barcelona’s broader commitment to sustainability, circular economy, and the UN Sustainable Development Goals (notably SDG 12.3 on food waste reduction). By focusing on institutional environments where food waste is concentrated, the project seeks both immediate reductions in waste (with pilot sites reporting up to 15% less food wasted) and longer-term culture change regarding food consumption and waste awareness. Its impact extends to economic savings (lower food purchasing and disposal costs), improved nutritional outcomes, and measurable environmental benefits in terms of reduced landfill contributions and greenhouse gas emissions. This model showcases the strategic role of data-driven, participatory approaches in advancing urban sustainability and offers a replicable blueprint for other cities.

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile:**  
Barcelona is Spain’s second-largest city, with a population of approximately 1.6 million in the city proper and over 5 million in the wider metropolitan area. It is a major Mediterranean port, a hub for tourism, commerce, culture, and innovation. The city has a diverse socioeconomic base, strong municipal leadership in sustainability, and a reputation for progressive urban planning (e.g., Superblocks, green infrastructure)[^3].

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**  
The pilot targeted public schools across the metropolitan region. These areas typically feature:
- Dense urban neighborhoods with mixed residential and institutional land uses
- Diverse demographics: various income levels, significant immigrant and multicultural populations
- Established infrastructure, but varied access to healthy food and waste management services
- Environmental constraints: limited green spaces, urban heat island effects, and pressure on waste systems[^3]

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified:**  
- High levels of avoidable food waste in institutional canteens and schools[^2][^1]
- Low awareness among users (students, staff) of the scale and impact of food waste
- Nutritional imbalances in school meals and lack of feedback on menu popularity
- Rising costs for food procurement and waste disposal

**Triggering Events:**  
- City’s adoption of circular economy and sustainability goals (e.g., 2030 Agenda, SDG commitments)
- Evidence from local food waste audits and research highlighting institutional food waste as a “quick win” for sustainability[^1]
- Advocacy and pilot proposals from NGOs such as Fundació Espigoladors

**Stakeholder Landscape:**  
- City government (policy leadership, funding, coordination)
- School administrators and catering companies (implementation, menu planning)
- Students and staff (users, behavior change targets)
- NGOs and academic institutions (research, monitoring, advocacy)

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework:**  
- Municipal authority over public schools and waste management
- Collaboration with regional Catalan government agencies for circular economy and food security policies

**Relevant Policies:**  
- Barcelona’s Circular Economy Action Plan
- Sustainable Food Capital designation (2021)
- Food waste reduction regulations (local and national)[^3]

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:**  
- Supportive legal framework for waste reduction and sustainability pilots
- Institutional inertia and data privacy concerns as barriers to rapid scaling

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals:**  
- Quantitatively reduce food waste in public sector canteens by at least 15% in pilot phase
- Improve nutritional outcomes and menu satisfaction among users
- Build awareness and change attitudes toward food and waste

**Vision Statement:**  
“To make Barcelona’s public food services a model of sustainability, where every meal contributes to health, wellbeing, and environmental stewardship through data-driven, participatory management.”

**Alignment with Broader Plans:**  
- Directly supports Barcelona’s Circular Economy Action Plan and SDG 12.3
- Reinforces the city’s role as a global leader in sustainable food policy[^3]

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change:**  
If food waste in institutional canteens is measured, made visible, and addressed using behavioral and technological interventions, then waste levels will decrease, user awareness will increase, and positive economic and environmental outcomes will follow[^2][^1].

**Target Beneficiaries:**  
- Students and staff (healthier meals, reduced waste costs)
- Municipal government (lower disposal costs, sustainability metrics)
- Wider community (environmental benefits, replicable model)

**Innovation Elements:**  
- Integration of digital waste tracking devices and real-time dashboard analytics
- Use of behavioral nudges and participatory feedback
- Data-driven menu and portion adjustment recommendations[^2]

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers:**  
- City leadership’s prioritization of sustainability and circular economy
- Public pressure to address climate change and food security

**Economic Drivers:**  
- Rising costs of food and waste management
- Opportunity to generate savings and demonstrate cost-effective sustainability[^3]

**Social Drivers:**  
- Equity concerns regarding access to healthy food
- Community advocacy for better waste management and nutritional outcomes

**Environmental Drivers:**  
- Urgent need to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from food waste
- Resource management and climate adaptation goals[^3]

**Technological Drivers:**  
- Availability of smart monitoring devices and digital analytics platforms
- Uptake of data-driven solutions in public sector management

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework:**  
Grounded in circular economy principles, sustainable food systems, and behavioral economics (nudging). Emphasizes measurable impact and participatory co-design[^2][^3].

**Physical/Spatial Design:**  
- Installation of digital plate waste tracking devices in school canteens
- Dashboard displays for staff and (where applicable) users
- Signage and feedback mechanisms as behavioral nudges

**Scope and Phasing:**  
- Phase 1: Pilot in 4 school canteens (2019–2020)
- Phase 2: Data analysis, refinement of feedback systems
- Phase 3: Expansion to wider network, integration with municipal waste data

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed:**  
- Digital plate waste tracking devices (weighing and recording uneaten food per tray)[^1]
- Analytics dashboards for trend monitoring and feedback
- Behavioral insights tools: signage, real-time feedback, staff engagement[^2]

**Innovation Type:**  
- Technological: Smart sensors, digital analytics
- Social: Behavioral nudges, participatory co-design
- Governance: Data-driven management and policy feedback

**Integration Approach:**  
- Devices and analytics integrated with existing school food service systems
- Data flows coordinated with municipal sustainability monitoring platforms

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting:**  
- Technology supplied via municipal procurement, with support from local startups and research centers
- Staff training and engagement coordinated by city and academic partners

**Stakeholder Engagement:**  
- Workshops and participatory sessions with students, staff, and parents
- Ongoing feedback cycles to adjust interventions

**Participatory Elements:**  
- Staff and student involvement in menu planning, waste tracking, and analysis
- Opportunities for users to suggest improvements

**Project Management:**  
- Coordinated by a cross-sector steering committee (city, schools, NGOs, researchers)
- Regular reporting and review cycles

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs:**  
- Initial pilot: modest investment in tracking devices, analytics software, training
- Main cost categories: hardware, software, staff time, evaluation

**Funding Mix:**  
- Municipal budget as primary source
- Supplementary support from regional sustainability grants and research funding

**Revenue Mechanisms:**  
- Cost savings from reduced food purchases and waste disposal
- No direct user fees; benefits accrue to public sector balance sheet

**Financial Sustainability:**  
- Anticipated payback through cost savings and grant funding for further expansion

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges:**  
- Ensuring reliability and ease of use of tracking devices
- Integrating data from multiple sites into unified analytics platform[^2]

**Financial Challenges:**  
- Limited initial budget for widespread rollout
- Requirement for ongoing support and training

**Social and Political Challenges:**  
- Sustaining user engagement and preventing “initiative fatigue”
- Navigating privacy concerns and data sharing among schools

**Administrative Challenges:**  
- Coordinating among diverse stakeholders (city, schools, caterers, NGOs)
- Standardizing processes across sites

**Environmental Challenges:**  
- Unforeseen variations in waste due to menu, seasonality, or external events (e.g., COVID-19 disruptions)

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

**Problem-Solving Approaches:**  
- Iterative pilot testing, refinement of nudging strategies
- Enhanced staff involvement shown to increase effectiveness, especially in fruit/healthy options[^2]

**Design Modifications:**  
- Adjustment of device placement and feedback mechanisms based on user input
- Phased rollout to address capacity constraints

**Lessons Learned During Implementation:**  
- Staff engagement is critical to success; behavioral nudges more effective when actively supported
- Real-time feedback increases user awareness and responsiveness[^2]
