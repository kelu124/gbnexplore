# Executive summary## RESPONSE (integRatEd Solutions for POsitive eNergy and reSilient CitiEs)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** RESPONSE – integRatEd Solutions for POsitive eNergy and reSilient CitiEs
- **Alternative Names:** RESPONSE Project, H2020 RESPONSE, Positive Energy Neighbourhoods (Dijon & Turku)
- **Location:** 
  - Dijon: Fontaine d’Ouche district, Dijon Métropole, France
  - Turku: Student Village (Ylioppilaskylä), Turku, Southwest Finland
- **Geographic Coordinates:** 
  - Dijon: ~47.3220° N, 5.0415° E
  - Turku: ~60.4518° N, 22.2666° E
- **Timeline:** 
  - Start: October 2020
  - Duration: 60 months (through September 2025)
  - Status: Ongoing (as of late 2025)
- **Project Scale:**
  - Dijon: Fontaine d’Ouche (large social housing district, ~10,000 residents)
  - Turku: Student Village (largest in Finland, ~5,500 residents)
  - Budget: €23.6 million (total), with €7.4 million for Dijon[^1]
- **Leading Organization(s):**
  - Dijon: Dijon Métropole, Grand Dijon Habitat, Bouygues Construction, Atmo Bourgogne-Franche-Comté, EDF, others
  - Turku: City of Turku, Turku Student Village Foundation (TYS), Turku Energia, Turku University of Applied Sciences, others
- **Funding Sources:** European Commission (Horizon 2020 Smart Cities & Communities), local public authorities, private partners[^2][^1][^3][^4]

#### **Executive Summary**
The RESPONSE project is a flagship EU-funded initiative aiming to pioneer the transition to **Positive Energy Districts (PEDs)** in urban Europe, using Dijon (France) and Turku (Finland) as “lighthouse cities.” Its core objective is to demonstrate that urban districts can produce more renewable energy annually than they consume, while dramatically reducing greenhouse gas emissions and enhancing urban resilience. RESPONSE achieves this by integrating digital twins, IoT-based smart metering, AI-driven energy management, renewable energy generation (solar, geothermal), advanced energy storage, and citizen engagement platforms into the very fabric of these neighborhoods. The project’s approach is holistic: not only does it deploy technical innovations, but it also emphasizes participatory governance, social inclusion, and innovative business models for long-term sustainability. The ambition is to develop validated, replicable roadmaps for sustainable, climate-neutral urban districts across Europe, supporting the EU’s Green Deal and climate neutrality targets for 2050. By fostering deep cross-sectoral integration, active citizen involvement, and robust monitoring and optimization tools, RESPONSE seeks to set new benchmarks for urban energy management and neighborhood-scale sustainability[^5][^6][^2][^7][^8][^9][^10][^3][^4][^11].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**Dijon:**
- **City Profile:** Regional capital, ~160,000 inhabitants (metropolitan area ~250,000); historical center of Burgundy region; economy based on public administration, education, food processing, wine, and services.
- **Neighborhood:** Fontaine d’Ouche, 1970s social housing district; high-rise blocks, underserved population, below-average income, diverse ethnic mix.
- **Land Use:** Predominantly residential (social housing), with some retail, schools, and public services.
- **Infrastructure:** Outdated building stock, district heating network, limited energy efficiency, some prior urban renewal efforts.
- **Environmental Conditions:** Urban heat island effects, air quality issues, substantial energy poverty[^1].

**Turku:**
- **City Profile:** Finland’s oldest city, ~195,000 inhabitants (urban area), major university center, port, and technology hub.
- **Neighborhood:** Student Village (Ylioppilaskylä), largest student housing complex in Finland; dense, mixed-use, young demographic.
- **Land Use:** Predominantly residential (student apartments), some commercial and community spaces.
- **Infrastructure:** Modernized but with scope for greater energy efficiency, district heating, recent investments in sustainable mobility.
- **Environmental Conditions:** Cold climate, significant heating demand, targets for carbon neutrality[^2][^12][^9].

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High urban energy demand, inefficient building stock (especially in social housing)
  - GHG emissions from heating and electricity, energy poverty, vulnerability to climate change
  - Fragmented data, lack of real-time monitoring, limited citizen engagement in energy transition
- **Triggering Events:**
  - EU climate and energy policy push (Green Deal, climate-neutrality by 2050)
  - Horizon 2020 “Smart Cities & Communities” call
  - Local commitments to carbon neutrality (Dijon Métropole, City of Turku)[^1][^7][^10]
- **Stakeholder Landscape:** 
  - Public authorities (municipalities, housing agencies), energy utilities, technology providers, local NGOs, residents, universities

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

- **Governance Framework:** 
  - Coordinated by municipalities in partnership with public housing and private sector actors
  - Strong alignment with national and EU energy and climate policy frameworks
- **Relevant Policies:** 
  - EU Green Deal, National Energy and Climate Plans (NECPs), local sustainable energy action plans
  - Local urban renewal and social inclusion strategies
- **Legal/Institutional Enablers or Barriers:**
  - Existing district heating and energy regulation
  - Barriers: complex procurement, data sharing/privacy, regulatory constraints on local energy trading

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

- **Primary Goals:**
  - Transform selected urban districts into **Positive Energy Districts** (PEDs) that generate more renewable energy annually than they consume
  - Reduce GHG emissions, improve energy efficiency, and enhance urban resilience
  - Develop replicable, scalable models for sustainable urban transformation[^5][^6][^7]
- **Vision Statement:** “Climate-neutral, resilient, and citizen-centric smart cities by 2050, enabled through integrated digital, energy, and social innovation.”
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:**
  - EU climate neutrality by 2050, national/local carbon-neutrality targets, city-level energy transition roadmaps

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

- **Theory of Change:** By integrating advanced digital, renewable, and participatory solutions at the neighborhood scale, cities can achieve radical gains in energy sustainability, emissions reduction, and social innovation.
- **Target Beneficiaries:** 
  - Residents (improved comfort, reduced energy bills, better air quality)
  - City authorities (climate targets, innovation reputation)
  - Broader urban community (replicable learning, job creation)
- **Innovation Elements:**
  - Real-time digital twins, IoT and AI for energy management, participatory platforms, business model innovation

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

- **Political:** EU and local climate targets; municipal leadership on sustainability
- **Economic:** EU funding, energy cost savings, new business opportunities
- **Social:** Empowerment of vulnerable communities, improved quality of life
- **Environmental:** Urgent need for decarbonization, adaptation to climate change impacts
- **Technological:** Rapid advances in IoT, AI, renewables, and energy storage

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

- **Conceptual Framework:** Positive Energy Districts (PEDs) as the unit of urban energy transformation; integration of technological, social, and economic innovation; “lighthouse” demonstration for replication[^13][^10]
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** 
  - Dijon: Deep energy retrofits in social housing, solar PV installations, upgraded district heating, EV charging infrastructure
  - Turku: Smart building retrofits, solar panels, smart meters, integration of e-mobility, digital dashboards
- **Scope and Phasing:**
  - Initial master planning and baseline studies
  - Progressive deployment of 10 integrated solutions (86 elements)
  - Community engagement and co-creation
  - Monitoring, evaluation, and scaling[^6][^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Digital twins:** Real-time 3D models for energy monitoring/optimization
  - **IoT sensors & smart meters:** Building-level and district data collection
  - **Renewables:** Solar PV, geothermal systems
  - **Energy storage:** Batteries and thermal storage
  - **AI-driven energy management:** Predictive control for efficiency and flexibility
  - **Citizen engagement platforms:** Digital tools for feedback and participation
  - **E-mobility:** EV charging stations, grid integration[^5][^12][^9][^10]
- **Innovation Type:** Technological (core), social (citizen co-creation), governance (new business/process models)
- **Integration Approach:** Layered with existing infrastructure; interoperable digital platforms; cross-sectoral system integration

#### **Implementation Methodology**

- **Procurement/Contracting:** Competitive processes, consortia of public/private actors
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** 
  - Dijon: Co-design workshops, living labs, local events (e.g., “kids labs”)[^6][^1]
  - Turku: Peer mentor programs, workshops, participatory digital platforms[^12][^14][^9]
- **Participatory Elements:** Residents involved in all phases; platforms for feedback, behavior change incentives
- **Project Management:** Coordinated by municipal authorities with technical and academic partners, regular monitoring and reporting

#### **Financing Model**

- **Budget and Costs:** 
  - €23.6 million total (EU Horizon 2020: principal funder; local and private co-funding)
  - Dijon: €7.4 million
- **Funding Mix:** EU (majority), local government, private sector
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Energy cost savings, innovative business models for services, potential for local energy sales
- **Financial Sustainability:** Focus on models that support post-project viability (e.g., value capture, service fees)[^11]

---

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

- **Technical:** 
  - Integration of diverse technologies (legacy systems, new IoT, digital twins)
  - Delays in asset deployment (notably in Dijon); interoperability issues[^6]
- **Financial:** 
  - Complexity of multi-source funding, ensuring long-term financial sustainability
- **Social and Political:**
  - Engaging traditionally marginalized/low-income communities (Dijon), overcoming “pilot fatigue,” ensuring inclusivity
- **Administrative:** 
  - Coordination across large consortia (53 partners, 13 countries in Dijon), regulatory bottlenecks
- **Environmental:** 
  - Uncertainties in renewable energy yield, adapting to local climatic constraints

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** 
  - Detailed baseline and technical analysis to identify/mitigate bottlenecks
  - Development of tailored business models and “Bold City 2050” visions[^6]
- **Design Modifications:** 
  - Sequencing adaptation (e.g., delayed solutions in Dijon with mitigation plans)
  - Iterative stakeholder engagement to build trust and participation
- **Lessons Learned:** 
  - Strong need for cross-sectoral coordination, early and ongoing community involvement, flexible project management to adjust to local realities

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**

- **Physical Outputs:** 
  - Retrofitted buildings, new solar capacity, grid upgrades, EV charging points
  - Digital dashboards, citizen engagement platforms
- **Service Delivery:** Enhanced energy services, real-time monitoring, participatory decision-making tools
- **Quantitative Metrics:** 
  - 11.2 GWh/year local renewable energy penetration
  - 3,090 MWh/year energy savings
  - 9,799 tons CO₂eq/year emission reduction (across both districts)[^5][^10]

#### **Social Impacts**

- **Community Benefits:** 
  - Improved living comfort, lower energy bills, better air quality
  - Social innovation via participation platforms, co-creation, local events[^14]
- **Equity Outcomes:** 
  - Inclusive focus (notably in Dijon’s social housing)
  - Empowerment of vulnerable residents, minimized risk of displacement[^1]
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** 
  - No evidence of displacement; project explicitly targets social housing and aims to avoid gentrification
- **Social Capital:** 
  - Strengthened civic engagement, increased trust in public authorities
- **Cultural Impacts:** 
  - New local identity around energy transition, innovation pride

#### **Economic Impacts**

- **Job Creation:** Direct employment in construction, retrofitting, tech deployment
- **Economic Activity:** Stimulated local businesses, innovation ecosystem growth
- **Property Values:** Likely long-term uplift, though project focuses on affordability
- **Cost Savings:** Energy bills reduced for residents/municipalities
- **Economic Inclusion:** Explicit focus on low-income/vulnerable groups

#### **Environmental Impacts**

- **Climate and Emissions:** Major GHG reductions, progress towards carbon neutrality
- **Resource Management:** Increased efficiency in energy and water use, integration of renewables
- **Environmental Quality:** Improved air quality, noise reduction, urban greening
- **Waste and Pollution:** Upgrades to waste and pollution management systems
- **Sustainability Metrics:** PED performance, alignment with EU standards

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

- **Institutional Capacity:** Strengthened public/private partnerships, improved city governance capabilities
- **Policy Influence:** Model for local/national PED policies, input to EU frameworks
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Enhanced via digital dashboards, participatory platforms

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

- **Success Metrics:** On track for key energy and emissions goals; most solutions implemented (minor delays in Dijon)
- **Unintended Consequences:** No major negative side effects reported; positive spillovers include increased innovation capacity and civic engagement
- **Long-term Viability:** Strong emphasis on business model and post-project sustainability planning[^11]

  - Local governance capacity, existing infrastructure quality, social context

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

- **Demonstrates feasibility** of PEDs in both affluent and vulnerable urban contexts (notably social housing in Dijon)
- **Integrated approach** combining technology, governance, and social participation
- **Strong replicability** focus, with clear roadmaps and “Fellow Cities” following suit
- **Robust monitoring** and transparent evaluation mechanisms[^5][^6][^7][^11]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

- **Implementation delays** (notably in Dijon, due to late asset deployment)
- **Complexity of coordination** across multiple partners and cities
- **Challenges in ensuring long-term financial sustainability** post-EU funding

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

- Strong focus on **inclusion, empowerment, and social innovation**
- Avoids gentrification by targeting social housing and vulnerable groups
- Participatory mechanisms support procedural justice

#### **Future Outlook**

- **Long-term trajectory:** Expansion of PEDs, deeper integration of mobility, digital, and energy systems
- **Ongoing challenges:** Need to mainstream solutions, address regulatory hurdles, ensure sustained funding
- **Evolution/Next phases:** Replication in “Fellow Cities,” integration with broader urban climate and digital strategies, refinement of business models for scalability
