# Executive summary## Milan Porta Romana Sharing Cities (Horizon 2020: Sharing Cities)

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**
- **Initiative Title:** Milan Porta Romana Sharing Cities (Horizon 2020: Sharing Cities)
- **Alternative Names:** Sharing Cities Milan, Porta Romana/Vettabbia Smart District
- **Location:** Porta Romana/Vettabbia District, Milan, Lombardy, Italy
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Approx. 45.4482° N, 9.2101° E (central area of Porta Romana)
- **Timeline:** 
  - Project start: January 2016
  - Core implementation: 2016–2020
  - Monitoring and follow-up: Through 2021
- **Project Scale:** 
  - Area: 14 km² demonstration district
  - Population affected: Several thousand residents in the district, with wider city impact
  - Investment: €8.6 million (local partnership), €25 million (overall Sharing Cities project)[^1][^2][^3]
- **Leading Organization(s):** Comune di Milano (City of Milan), Fondazione Politecnico di Milano, Politecnico di Milano (DASTU, DENG), Siemens, AMAT, Legambiente, A2A Smart City, Unareti, and other public-private partners[^1][^3]
- **Funding Sources:** European Union Horizon 2020 (Smart Cities and Communities), City of Milan, private sector investment, and other public/private partners[^1][^2]

#### **Executive Summary**
Milan’s Porta Romana Sharing Cities initiative is a flagship component of the EU Horizon 2020 “Sharing Cities” program, designed to demonstrate and scale innovative, digitally enabled solutions for urban sustainability. Anchored in the redevelopment of a 14 km² brownfield site bridging Milan's historic center and agricultural periphery, the project seeks to transform the district into a “near-zero emission” smart neighborhood. Through deep energy retrofits of residential and public buildings, integration of shared electric mobility (e-cars, e-bikes, shared logistics), and deployment of smart street lighting and environmental monitoring, the district serves as a living laboratory for sustainable urban regeneration.

Central to the project is the Urban Sharing Platform (USP), a digital infrastructure that aggregates data from smart meters, building management systems, mobility services, and environmental sensors. This platform enables real-time monitoring, customizable dashboards for diverse stakeholders (residents, property owners, city officials), and collaborative reporting to foster transparency and participatory governance.

The initiative’s philosophy is citizen-centric: it emphasizes co-design and behavioral incentives, notably via the SharingMi digital social market, which rewards sustainable actions. The project has achieved tangible reductions in energy consumption (up to 70% in retrofitted buildings), increased adoption of e-mobility, and improved urban air quality. Its governance, technical, and social innovations are intentionally replicable, with models already adapted in other European cities. Milan’s experience demonstrates the feasibility and impact of integrated digital-physical approaches for advancing green building neighborhoods and sustainable urban development[^4][^1][^2][^5][^3].

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**
**City Profile:** Milan is Italy’s second-largest city, a global center for finance, fashion, and design, with a population of about 1.4 million in the municipality and over 3 million in the metropolitan area. The city is characterized by a strong service economy, high urban density, and a prominent role in regional/national urban networks. Milan faces challenges typical of large European cities: aging infrastructure, air pollution, and significant socioeconomic disparities[^4][^5].

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics:**
- **Historical patterns:** Porta Romana/Vettabbia is a former industrial and railway area located southeast of Milan’s center. The site includes large brownfields and underutilized industrial spaces, which historically contributed to a fragmented urban fabric and social separation between the city core and periphery[^3][^6].
- **Land use:** Prior to redevelopment, the district featured a mix of abandoned industrial lots, low-quality housing stock (70% with poor energy ratings), and limited public amenities.
- **Demographics:** The area includes a diverse mix of incomes and ages, with a significant proportion of lower-income and aging residents living in outdated social housing[^3].
- **Infrastructure:** The district suffered from poor mobility connections, inadequate public space, and limited access to modern services[^3][^6].
- **Environmental conditions:** Air quality was suboptimal, urban heat island effects were pronounced, and green space was limited.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**
- **Problems/Challenges Identified:**
  - High energy consumption and poor efficiency in the building stock
  - Traffic congestion, inadequate public and shared mobility options
  - Environmental degradation (air pollution, lack of green space)
  - Social and economic isolation between city center and periphery
  - Outdated infrastructure and fragmented community engagement[^3][^4][^5]
- **Triggering Events:**
  - EU Horizon 2020 call for Smart Cities demonstrators (2014–2015)
  - Milan’s adoption of bold urban development, climate, and innovation policies since 2012[^4]
- **Stakeholder Landscape:** Key actors included the city government, local residents (notably social housing tenants), private developers, academia, NGOs, and technology providers. Interests ranged from improving living conditions to economic revitalization and environmental protection[^1][^3].

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**
- **Governance Framework:** Led by Comune di Milano, with multi-level governance involving national and EU institutions, private partners, and civil society[^1][^2].
- **Relevant Policies:** Milan’s smart city plan, energy efficiency standards, sustainable mobility strategies, urban regeneration and zoning codes, and national/EU climate targets[^4][^5].
- **Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers:** EU funding mechanisms (Horizon 2020) enabled ambitious cross-sectoral innovation. Barriers included the complexity of integrating new technologies with legacy systems and navigating Italy’s often slow-moving public procurement and regulatory processes[^1][^5].

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**
- **Primary Goals:**
  - Transform Porta Romana/Vettabbia into a near-zero emission smart district
  - Achieve deep energy retrofits of residential and public buildings
  - Enable shared electric mobility and reduce car dependency
  - Foster citizen engagement, transparency, and behavior change through digital platforms[^1][^2][^5][^3]
- **Vision Statement:** “Building a citizen-centered, digitally enabled, sustainable urban district that bridges Milan’s historic and agricultural areas, setting a replicable model for European smart cities.”
- **Alignment with Broader Plans:** The initiative sits at the intersection of Milan’s citywide smart city, energy, mobility, and urban regeneration strategies, as well as the EU’s climate and digital transformation agendas[^4][^5][^1].

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**
- **Theory of Change:** By combining digital platforms (for data, transparency, engagement) with physical interventions (retrofitting, mobility, lighting), the project aims to simultaneously reduce emissions, improve quality of life, and foster a vibrant, inclusive urban community.
- **Target Beneficiaries:** Residents (especially in social housing), local businesses, city officials, and the wider Milanese public.
- **Innovation Elements:** Integration of open data and digital KPI dashboards; collaborative reporting; co-design with residents; gamified behavioral incentives; cross-sector partnerships[^4][^1][^2][^3].

#### **Drivers and Motivations**
- **Political:** Strong municipal leadership and alignment with EU smart city/green deal priorities.
- **Economic:** Stimulate investment, attract new businesses, and reduce energy costs.
- **Social:** Promote inclusion, participation, and improved urban quality of life.
- **Environmental:** Address climate change, air pollution, and resource efficiency.
- **Technological:** Leverage advances in IoT, digital platforms, and smart mobility to drive systemic urban change[^2][^1].

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**
- **Conceptual Framework:** Citizen-centered smart city, urban regeneration of brownfields, integration of digital and physical infrastructures, co-design and behavioral economics[^4][^2][^3].
- **Physical/Spatial Design:** 
  - Retrofit of 20+ buildings (21,000 m² private, 4,000 m² public)
  - New mobility hubs, e-car and e-bike stations, smart street lighting, environmental sensors
  - Creation of green space and improved connectivity between urban core and periphery[^1][^5][^3]
- **Scope and Phasing:** 
  - Phase 1: Co-design process and technical planning (2016–2017)
  - Phase 2: Implementation of retrofits, mobility, lighting, and digital platforms (2018–2020)
  - Phase 3: Monitoring, evaluation, and replication (2020–2021)[^1][^2]

#### **Technologies and Innovations**
- **Technologies Deployed:**
  - **Smart meters** and building management systems for energy monitoring
  - **Digital dashboards** for KPIs and collaborative reporting
  - **Urban Sharing Platform (USP):** Aggregates and visualizes real-time data
  - **E-mobility infrastructure:** 60+ electric vehicle charging points (20 rapid), 150 e-bikes, 14 sharing stations, 10 e-logistics vehicles
  - **Smart parking:** 75+ sensors to manage parking and enforce rules
  - **Environmental monitoring:** Sensors in lamp posts for air/noise/traffic
  - **Digital Social Market (SharingMi):** Gamified app for sustainable behavior[^4][^1][^5][^7][^3]
- **Innovation Type:** Technological (IoT, data platforms), social (co-design, behavioral incentives), governance (public-private partnerships), financial (mixed funding, revenue from services)[^2][^1]
- **Integration Approach:** Interoperability prioritized via open standards; integration of new tech with legacy systems and citywide platforms[^5][^3].

#### **Implementation Methodology**
- **Procurement and Contracting:** EU and city-led competitive procurement for technology and construction; multi-partner consortium model[^1][^2].
- **Stakeholder Engagement:** Structured co-design workshops and sprints, participatory app design, ongoing feedback loops with residents and businesses[^4][^7][^3].
- **Participatory Elements:** Residents involved in selecting retrofit measures, designing mobility solutions, and shaping digital tools.
- **Project Management:** Coordinated by Comune di Milano, with working groups for technology, engagement, and evaluation. Academic partners provided monitoring and technical support[^1][^2][^3].

#### **Financing Model**
- **Budget and Costs:** €8.6 million for Milan’s local partnership (part of €25 million overall EU project)[^1][^2].
- **Funding Mix:** Horizon 2020 (majority), city funds, private investment, in-kind contributions from partners[^2][^1].
- **Revenue Mechanisms:** Some cost recovery through mobility services, potential for value capture from increased property values.
- **Financial Sustainability:** Aimed for self-sustaining mobility and energy services post-EU funding; emphasis on attracting further investment and public-private collaboration[^2][^5].

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**
- **Technical:** Integrating new digital systems with legacy infrastructure; ensuring interoperability across diverse devices and platforms[^1][^5].
- **Financial:** Managing upfront retrofit and tech investment, ensuring long-term financial viability beyond initial grants[^1][^2].
- **Social and Political:** Engaging a diverse, sometimes skeptical population; overcoming resistance to behavior change; ensuring equity in benefit distribution[^4][^7][^3].
- **Administrative:** Navigating Italy’s complex procurement and regulatory environment; coordinating a large, multi-sector consortium[^1][^5].
- **Environmental:** Remediation of contaminated brownfield sites; managing construction impacts[^3][^6].

#### **Adaptive Strategies**
- **Problem-Solving Approaches:** Iterative co-design and feedback, flexible technical integration, continuous monitoring of progress and challenges[^4][^7].
- **Design Modifications:** Adjustments to digital tool interfaces based on user feedback; technical tweaks to ensure compatibility with older buildings and infrastructure.
- **Lessons Learned:** Importance of early, genuine stakeholder engagement; need for robust data governance and privacy; value of modular, replicable solutions[^4][^2][^7][^3].

---

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

#### **Direct Outputs**
- **Physical Outputs:** 
  - Retrofit of over 20 buildings (25,000 m² total area)
  - Installation of 60+ EV charging points, 14 e-bike sharing stations, 75+ smart parking sensors
  - Deployment of 300+ smart lamp posts with environmental sensors[^1][^5]
- **Service Delivery:** New e-mobility services, car and bike sharing, real-time parking and environmental information, digital engagement via SharingMi app[^1][^5][^7]
- **Quantitative Metrics:** 
  - Up to 70% reduction in energy needs in retrofitted buildings[^1]
  - 4,500 tons of CO2 emissions saved citywide (Sharing Cities mobility measures)[^2]
  - Over 1,000 users engaged in SharingMi within first months; over 5,700 stories shared, 25,300 interactions recorded[^7]

#### **Social Impacts**
- **Community Benefits:** Improved comfort in social housing, enhanced mobility options, safer and more attractive public spaces[^1][^3]
- **Equity Outcomes:** Focus on social housing and underserved populations, though ongoing challenges in ensuring all groups benefit equally
- **Displacement and Gentrification:** No major displacement reported; project aimed at inclusive regeneration
- **Social Capital:** Strengthened community networks through co-design and digital engagement
- **Cultural Impacts:** Revitalization of district identity, increased civic pride[^3][^6]

#### **Economic Impacts**
- **Job Creation:** At least 100 new jobs created across demonstration districts (Sharing Cities overall)[^2]
- **Economic Activity:** Boost to local businesses, new mobility and data service markets
- **Property Values:** Likely increase due to improved amenities and sustainability (data not specified)
- **Cost Savings:** Significant energy bill reduction for residents; citywide efficiency gains[^1][^2]
- **Economic Inclusion:** Emphasis on affordability and access in mobility and housing interventions

#### **Environmental Impacts**
- **Climate and Emissions:** Notable reduction in building and transport emissions; increased renewable energy integration
- **Resource Management:** Improved energy and water efficiency through smart monitoring
- **Environmental Quality:** Enhanced air quality monitoring and management; increased green space[^1][^5]
- **Waste and Pollution:** Digital and behavioral strategies to reduce waste and pollution
- **Sustainability Metrics:** Targeting near-zero emissions; performance tracked via digital KPIs

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**
- **Institutional Capacity:** Strengthened city capabilities in digital innovation and project management
- **Policy Influence:** Models and standards adopted in other cities; influence on Milan’s PGT 2030 and national smart city policy
- **Transparency and Accountability:** Real-time data and reporting enhance public oversight[^4][^2][^7]

#### **Performance Against Objectives**
- **Success Metrics:** High achievement on energy efficiency, modal shift in mobility, citizen engagement, and digital innovation
- **Unintended Consequences:** Some difficulties in scaling user engagement and addressing digital divide
- **Long-term Viability:** Models designed for sustainability and replication beyond project’s EU funding window[^1][^2]

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**
- **Contextual Dependencies:** Brownfield context, strong local leadership, EU funding were important enablers
- **Critical Success Factors:** Citizen engagement, interoperable digital infrastructure, robust partnerships
- **Scalability:** Approach has been replicated in fellow cities (Bordeaux, Burgas, Warsaw) and is adaptable to other urban contexts[^2][^8][^9]

#### **Comparative Analysis**
- **Similar Initiatives:** London Greenwich and Lisbon demonstration districts (Sharing Cities lighthouse cities); smart district projects in Amsterdam, Vienna, and Barcelona[^2][^8]
- **Best Practices:** Co-design, open data, modular digital platforms, integrated mobility
- **Cautionary Lessons:** Importance of addressing digital divide; necessity of ongoing stakeholder engagement to avoid technocratic bias[^4][^2][^7]

#### **Parallels to [Your Specific Use Case]**
- **Contextual Similarities:** Urban regeneration of underused/industrial land, focus on energy and mobility transformation
- **Methodological Parallels:** Participatory planning, real-time monitoring, open data
- **Technological Overlaps:** Digital dashboards, smart meters, environmental sensors
- **Stakeholder Dynamics:** Multi-actor governance, emphasis on inclusion
- **Transferable Strategies:** Co-design, open standards, engagement incentives, digital transparency
- **Adaptation Considerations:** Tailor digital tools to local user capacities; ensure robust privacy and data governance; adapt to local regulatory context

---

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**
- Deep integration of digital and physical sustainability measures
- Genuine citizen engagement and co-design
- Significant, measurable reductions in energy use and emissions
- Robust, replicable governance and technical models[^4][^1][^2][^7][^3]

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**
- Digital divide: not all residents equally engaged with digital tools
- Technical and administrative complexity slowed implementation
- Long-term financial sustainability for some services remains uncertain
- Ongoing need to ensure equity in benefit distribution[^4][^7][^3]

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**
- Explicit focus on social housing and underserved communities
- Emphasis on behavioral incentives and user empowerment
- Potential for gentrification mitigated by inclusive design, but vigilance needed

#### **Future Outlook**
- Continued evolution as part of Milan’s PGT 2030 and wider city innovation agenda
- Ongoing scaling and adaptation in other European cities
- Further development of digital tools, particularly to bridge digital divides and ensure long-term user engagement
- Monitoring of long-term impacts and iterative policy/technology updates to sustain progress[^4][^1][^2]
