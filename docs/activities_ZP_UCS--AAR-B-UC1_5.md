# Executive summary## Sharing Cities – Smart Green Neighbourhoods

### **I. INITIATIVE OVERVIEW AND IDENTIFICATION**

#### **Basic Information**

- **Initiative Title:** Sharing Cities
- **Alternative Names:** Sharing Cities – Lighthouse Programme; Sharing Cities – Smart Cities and Communities (SCC1)
- **Location:** Milan (Porta Romana/Vettabbia district), Italy; also implemented in London (Royal Borough of Greenwich) and Lisbon (downtown); with fellow cities in Bordeaux, Burgas, and Warsaw
- **Geographic Coordinates:** Milan demonstration area approximately 216,614 m² in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district
- **Timeline:** January 2016 – December 2021 (6-year programme); initial 3-year implementation period plus 2-year monitoring phase
- **Project Scale:** Over 30 partners across 6 European countries; more than 100 cities engaged; 900 dwellings directly involved in energy retrofits; 4,500 tons of CO₂ emissions saved across all cities
- **Leading Organization(s):** Greater London Authority (overall programme leadership); Fondazione Politecnico di Milano; Comune di Milano; Politecnico di Milano (DASTU and DENG departments); Lisboa E-Nova; multiple private and public sector partners
- **Funding Sources:** €24-25 million in EU seed funding through Horizon 2020 Smart Cities and Communities (SCC1) call; €265 million in additional private investment triggered; €8.6 million specifically allocated to Milan partnership

#### **Executive Summary**

Sharing Cities was a major European smart cities initiative funded under the Horizon 2020 programme that operated from 2016 to 2021, with demonstration districts in three "lighthouse" cities—London, Lisbon, and Milan—and replication efforts in three "fellow" cities—Bordeaux, Burgas, and Warsaw.[^1][^2] The programme addressed pressing urban challenges including energy consumption in buildings, low-carbon transport, air quality, and data utilization for urban benefit.[^2] In Milan, the initiative focused on the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district, a brownfield site on a former railway yard, transforming it into a smart green neighbourhood through integrated technological, social, and governance innovations.[^1][^3] The project deployed ten distinct technologies and measures across the demonstration cities, including smart building retrofits achieving 70% energy reductions, shared electric mobility services, smart lighting infrastructure, and digital platforms for citizen engagement.[^1][^2] Rather than technology-driven approaches, Milan's vision centered the initiative on citizens' needs, emphasizing smart mobility, environmental quality, social inclusion, and civic participation.[^3] The programme successfully demonstrated that thoughtfully designed, integrated solutions could serve urban populations affordably and scalably, generating significant environmental, economic, and social benefits while establishing replicable models for European cities.[^2][^4] By programme completion, the initiative had engaged more than 100 municipalities worldwide and proven the potential for environmentally focused technologies to positively impact cities while building collaborative networks extending beyond the formal programme lifespan.[^2]

### **II. CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

#### **Urban Context**

**City Profile**

Milan is Italy's second-largest city and a major European economic and cultural center, serving as the capital of Lombardy region. The city has a complex urban structure combining historic medieval and Renaissance cores with industrial districts and modern suburban sprawl. Milan's economy is diversified, encompassing fashion, finance, design, manufacturing, and services sectors. The city has experienced significant urban transformation, particularly in post-industrial areas, and has positioned itself as a leader in sustainability and innovation. Since 2012, Milan has systematically developed comprehensive plans addressing urban development, sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, the sharing economy, and smart cities.[^3] The city's approach to smart cities emphasizes citizen-centricity rather than technology-driven solutions, viewing smart development through the lenses of smart mobility, smart environment, smart inclusion, and citizenship.[^3]

**Neighborhood/District Characteristics**

The Porta Romana/Vettabbia district represents a significant urban transformation opportunity. The demonstration area encompasses a 216,614 m² former railway yard in the northern part of the neighborhood, historically a brownfield site disconnected from surrounding urban fabric.[^3] The district connects Milan's historic city center to its agricultural belt, previously serving as a geographic, economic, and social barrier separating these areas.[^3] Prior to redevelopment, the area suffered from typical post-industrial conditions: underutilized infrastructure, environmental degradation, and limited economic vitality. The redevelopment envisions a functional mix of private and social housing units, multimodal transportation integration around a new station, and a large public park of 187,226 m².[^3] The district's demographic profile has shifted through the development process, attracting residents interested in sustainable urban living while raising concerns about potential gentrification and displacement of existing populations.

#### **Pre-Initiative Conditions**

**Problems/Challenges Identified**

Milan, like European cities generally, faced interconnected urban challenges that motivated the Sharing Cities initiative. Primary environmental challenges included high energy consumption in the building sector, carbon emissions from transportation, and deteriorating air quality.[^1] The city's building stock exhibited poor energy efficiency, with significant heat loss and high operational costs. Transportation patterns reflected car dependency, contributing to congestion, emissions, and air pollution. Data from urban systems remained fragmented across different municipal departments and private operators, limiting coordinated responses to urban challenges. Social challenges included limited citizen engagement in urban planning and governance, unequal distribution of urban benefits, and disconnection between residents and municipal decision-making. The Porta Romana/Vettabbia brownfield site specifically represented underutilized urban land with environmental contamination concerns and limited economic contribution to the city.

**Triggering Events**

The initiative emerged within the context of European Union climate commitments and the 2015 Paris Agreement, which catalyzed member states to develop aggressive decarbonization strategies. The EU's Horizon 2020 programme, launched in 2014, explicitly prioritized smart cities and communities as mechanisms for achieving climate and sustainability objectives. Milan's existing policy framework—including its 2012 strategic plans on sustainability and smart cities—created institutional readiness for participation. The availability of significant EU funding (€24-25 million) for lighthouse city demonstrations provided the financial catalyst necessary to undertake ambitious integrated interventions at neighborhood scale. Political leadership in Milan, particularly Mayor Giuseppe Sala's commitment to environmental, social, and economic sustainability, provided governance support.[^1]

**Stakeholder Landscape**

Pre-initiative stakeholders included municipal government (Comune di Milano), academic institutions (Politecnico di Milano with its architecture and energy departments), utility companies (A2A, Unareti, EDP), transportation operators (ATM, Transport for London), technology companies (Siemens, Danfoss), environmental NGOs (Legambiente), and residents of the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district. Interests diverged considerably: municipal government sought solutions to environmental and social challenges while managing fiscal constraints; utilities and energy companies saw opportunities for business model innovation; technology firms pursued market development; residents had mixed concerns about displacement, disruption, and genuine community benefit; and academic partners sought research opportunities and knowledge advancement. The fragmentation of these interests created both coordination challenges and opportunities for innovative partnership models.

#### **Policy and Regulatory Environment**

**Governance Framework**

The initiative operated within a multi-level governance structure spanning European, national, and local authorities. At the European level, the Horizon 2020 programme established funding mechanisms and performance expectations through the Smart Cities and Communities call.[^1] The Greater London Authority provided overall programme leadership, coordinating across the three lighthouse cities and three fellow cities.[^2][^4] At the national level, Italian government frameworks for urban development, energy efficiency, and environmental protection provided enabling policies. At the local level, Comune di Milano held primary authority for land use planning, building permits, and municipal service delivery, while Politecnico di Milano and other academic partners contributed technical expertise and research capacity. The governance structure deliberately created space for public-private-academic partnerships, recognizing that addressing complex urban challenges required integration across sectors.

**Relevant Policies**

Milan's strategic policy framework included its comprehensive urban development plan addressing sustainable mobility, energy efficiency, and the sharing economy.[^3] EU energy efficiency directives established regulatory requirements for building retrofits and renewable energy integration. Climate action policies at EU and national levels created targets for greenhouse gas emission reductions that motivated the initiative. Data protection regulations (GDPR) shaped the design of the Urban Sharing Platform and citizen engagement mechanisms. Building codes and construction standards governed the technical specifications for retrofits and new construction. Mobility regulations and parking policies influenced the design of shared electric mobility services and smart parking systems.

**Legal and Institutional Enablers/Barriers**

Significant enablers included the EU's commitment to funding smart cities innovation, which reduced financial barriers to experimentation. Flexible regulatory approaches allowed for pilot projects and temporary exemptions from standard procedures. Institutional capacity at Politecnico di Milano and other partner organizations provided technical expertise. Barriers included fragmented data governance across utility companies and municipal departments, which complicated integration efforts. Building ownership structures (particularly in retrofit initiatives) required negotiation with multiple stakeholders. Existing utility concession agreements sometimes limited opportunities for service innovation. Procurement regulations, while designed to ensure fairness, occasionally slowed decision-making. Privacy regulations required careful design of data collection and sharing mechanisms.

### **III. INITIATIVE RATIONALE AND OBJECTIVES**

#### **Strategic Vision**

**Primary Goals**

Sharing Cities pursued multiple interconnected objectives:[^1][^2] To develop and demonstrate innovative, integrated solutions addressing energy consumption, transport emissions, and air quality in urban neighborhoods. To create replicable, scalable models that could be adopted by other European municipalities and eventually worldwide. To engage citizens as active participants in urban sustainability, moving beyond top-down planning to co-designed solutions. To establish collaborative ecosystems linking cities, industry, and academic institutions for sustained knowledge sharing. To attract private investment in smart city solutions, demonstrating viable business models for sustainable urban development. To reduce greenhouse gas emissions and improve energy efficiency at neighborhood scale. To enhance quality of life through improved mobility, environmental quality, and social inclusion.

**Vision Statement**

The initiative envisioned an "agile and collaborative smart cities ecosystem" where thoughtfully designed, integrated, and open-source solutions served urban populations affordably and scalably.[^2] This vision emphasized that smart cities should be built around people's needs rather than technology for its own sake, with solutions developed through partnerships between municipalities, private industry, and academia. The vision extended beyond individual cities to a network of European municipalities collaborating on climate action and sustainable urban development.

**Alignment with Broader Plans**

Sharing Cities aligned with multiple strategic frameworks: EU climate commitments under the Paris Agreement and the European Green Deal; Horizon 2020 programme objectives for innovation in sustainable cities; Milan's comprehensive sustainability and smart city strategies; and broader European urban development trends emphasizing circular economy, sharing economy, and digitalization. The initiative positioned Milan as a leader in European smart city innovation, enhancing the city's international reputation and attracting talent and investment.

#### **Problem-Solution Logic**

**Theory of Change**

The initiative's theory of change proceeded through several causal pathways:[^1][^2] Energy retrofits in buildings would reduce energy demand and consumption, lowering both operational costs and carbon emissions while improving occupant comfort. Shared electric mobility services would reduce car ownership and usage, decreasing transport emissions and congestion while improving air quality. Smart lighting and sensor infrastructure would enable optimized resource use and real-time environmental monitoring. Digital platforms enabling citizen engagement and reward mechanisms would shift behavior toward sustainability by making sustainable choices visible, convenient, and rewarded. Interoperable data systems would enable municipal authorities to make evidence-based decisions and coordinate across sectors. Successful demonstration in lighthouse cities would generate knowledge and business models that other cities could replicate, multiplying impact. Private investment attraction would ensure financial sustainability and scale-up beyond initial EU funding.

**Target Beneficiaries**

Primary beneficiaries included residents of demonstration districts (Porta Romana/Vettabbia in Milan), who would experience improved housing quality, reduced energy costs, enhanced mobility options, and better environmental quality.[^1][^3] Secondary beneficiaries included Milan's broader population, benefiting from reduced citywide emissions and improved air quality. Tertiary beneficiaries included municipalities across Europe and globally that would adopt replicated solutions. Technology companies and service providers would benefit from market development opportunities. Academic institutions would advance knowledge in smart cities and sustainable urban development.

**Innovation Elements**

The initiative's distinctive features included its integrated approach combining building retrofits, mobility innovation, smart infrastructure, and digital platforms within single neighborhoods, rather than addressing these domains separately.[^1][^2] The emphasis on citizen engagement and behavioral incentives represented innovation in social and governance dimensions, not merely technological. The explicit focus on replicability and knowledge transfer distinguished the initiative from one-off demonstration projects. The multi-level governance structure linking European, national, and local authorities created institutional innovation. The public-private-academic partnership model represented a novel approach to urban problem-solving.

#### **Drivers and Motivations**

**Political Drivers**

Mayor Giuseppe Sala's commitment to environmental, social, and economic sustainability provided political leadership.[^1] EU climate commitments and the Horizon 2020 funding opportunity created policy incentives for municipalities to pursue ambitious initiatives. The desire to position Milan as an innovation leader in European urban development motivated political support.

**Economic Drivers**

The availability of €24-25 million in EU seed funding reduced financial barriers to experimentation.[^1][^2] The potential to attract €265 million in additional private investment demonstrated viable business models for sustainable urban development.[^2] Technology companies and service providers recognized market opportunities in smart city solutions. Real estate development opportunities in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia brownfield site created economic incentives for area transformation.

**Social Drivers**

Growing citizen concern about climate change and environmental quality created social pressure for municipal action.[^2] Community advocacy for sustainable mobility and green space influenced initiative design. Equity concerns about ensuring that urban development benefits reached all residents, including vulnerable populations, shaped participatory design approaches.

**Environmental Drivers**

EU and national climate targets created regulatory pressure for greenhouse gas emission reductions.[^2] Air quality concerns in Milan, reflecting broader European urban pollution challenges, motivated transport and energy solutions. Recognition that buildings account for approximately 40% of EU energy consumption and 30% of CO₂ emissions drove focus on building retrofits.

**Technological Drivers**

Maturation of smart sensor technology, IoT platforms, electric vehicle technology, and digital engagement tools made integrated solutions technically feasible. The EU's emphasis on digitalization and data-driven governance created momentum for technology adoption.

### **IV. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH**

#### **Project Design**

**Conceptual Framework**

The initiative drew on several urban development and planning philosophies. The "smart cities" framework emphasized technology-enabled optimization of urban systems. The "circular economy" concept informed resource efficiency approaches. The "sharing economy" model influenced mobility and logistics solutions. Critically, Milan's approach rejected purely technology-driven smart cities in favor of human-centered design, emphasizing citizen needs and participation.[^3] The "neighbourhood-scale intervention" approach recognized that meaningful transformation required integrated action across multiple domains within defined geographic areas rather than city-wide fragmentation. The "living lab" concept positioned demonstration districts as experimental spaces for testing and refining solutions before broader replication.

**Physical/Spatial Design**

In Milan's Porta Romana/Vettabbia district, physical design involved transformation of the 216,614 m² former railway yard into a mixed-use neighborhood.[^3] The master plan included functional diversity with private and social housing units, multimodal transportation integration around a new transit station, and a large public park (187,226 m²) providing green space and environmental quality.[^3] Building retrofits improved energy performance through envelope insulation, integration of low-carbon energy sources, digital controls, and comfort monitoring systems.[^3] The spatial design deliberately connected the historic city center to the agricultural belt, reducing geographic and social separation. Smart infrastructure including lighting, sensors, and charging stations was distributed throughout the district to support sustainable behaviors.

**Scope and Phasing**

The initiative operated on a structured timeline: initial 3-year implementation period (2016-2019) focused on deploying technologies and establishing systems, followed by a 2-year monitoring period (2019-2021) to assess impacts and refine approaches.[^1] Within this framework, building retrofits proceeded in phases, with 20 buildings in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district undergoing improvements.[^1] Mobility infrastructure deployment included phased rollout of 60 electric car charging stations (including 20 fast-charging points) at ten mobility areas, 14 bike-sharing points with electric bikes, and car-sharing schemes.[^1] Smart lighting and sensor infrastructure deployment proceeded across the district.[^1] Digital platform development and citizen engagement mechanisms evolved iteratively based on user feedback.

#### **Technologies and Innovations**

**Technologies Deployed**

Sharing Cities deployed a comprehensive suite of ten distinct technologies and measures:[^1][^2]

- **Building energy management systems:** Smart meters, sensors, and digital controls enabling real-time monitoring and optimization of energy consumption; integration of renewable energy sources; and occupant comfort management
- **Building envelope retrofits:** Insulation improvements, window replacement, and structural modifications reducing heating and cooling demands by up to 70%[^1][^3]
- **District heating systems:** Integrated energy distribution systems serving multiple buildings
- **Electric vehicle charging infrastructure:** 60 charging stations including 20 fast-charging points at ten mobility areas near public transit connections[^1]
- **Shared e-mobility services:** Electric car-sharing schemes, bike-sharing with electric bikes (including child seats), and shared logistics services with ten electric vehicles[^1]
- **Smart parking systems:** 75 smart parking sensors enabling drivers to locate available spaces, reducing search time and emissions; sensors also identified parking violations[^1]
- **Smart street lighting:** Connected lighting systems with integrated sensors monitoring environmental conditions, providing security and connectivity services, and relaying information to citizens and officials[^1]
- **Urban Sharing Platform (USP):** Interoperable data management system integrating information from building systems, mobility services, sensors, and citizen engagement platforms; enabling technical and business-level interoperability across public and private sector entities[^3]
- **Digital Social Market (SharingMi app):** Citizen engagement platform where users share experiences and ideas, earn "claps" converted to points redeemable locally and globally, incentivizing sustainable behavior[^1][^3]
- **Environmental monitoring systems:** Integrated sensor networks measuring air quality, noise levels, and traffic patterns throughout the district[^3]

**Innovation Type**

The initiative encompassed multiple innovation categories: **Technological innovation** through deployment of smart sensors, IoT platforms, and integrated data systems. **Social innovation** through participatory co-design processes and behavioral incentive mechanisms. **Governance innovation** through public-private-academic partnerships and collaborative decision-making structures. **Business model innovation** through new approaches to service delivery, financing, and stakeholder engagement. **Process innovation** through adaptive management approaches enabling real-time adjustment based on monitoring data.

**Integration Approach**

Technologies were integrated through the Urban Sharing Platform, which served as a central nervous system enabling communication and data sharing across previously fragmented systems.[^3] The USP adopted an "ecosystem-based approach" aligned with API economy trends, enabling incremental integration and enhancement of existing solutions.[^3] This approach prioritized interoperability at both technical levels (through common data standards and protocols) and business levels (through shared governance principles and stakeholder agreements). Integration extended beyond technology to encompass governance, with regular coordination meetings among public agencies, private companies, academic partners, and community representatives. The integration strategy explicitly aimed for longevity beyond the Sharing Cities programme, ensuring that systems and partnerships could continue evolving after EU funding concluded.

#### **Implementation Methodology**

**Procurement and Contracting**

The initiative involved complex procurement processes reflecting EU funding requirements and Italian public procurement regulations. The Comune di Milano, as primary municipal partner, managed procurement for public infrastructure including smart lighting, parking sensors, and public realm improvements. Academic partners (Politecnico di Milano) managed research contracts and technical consulting. Private companies including Siemens, Danfoss, and A2A secured contracts for technology deployment and service provision. The procurement process balanced transparency and fairness requirements with flexibility necessary for innovation. Performance-based contracting approaches incentivized companies to achieve specified outcomes rather than merely delivering predetermined solutions.

**Stakeholder Engagement**

Stakeholder engagement occurred at multiple scales and through multiple mechanisms. At the district level, residents participated in co-design processes shaping retrofit specifications, mobility service design, and digital platform features. The initiative explicitly incorporated public input into decision-making, with residents having voice in choosing which retrofits to pursue and how to configure shared services.[^1] Community meetings and workshops provided forums for dialogue between municipal authorities, companies, and residents. At the city level, municipal departments coordinated through regular steering committees. At the European level, the three lighthouse cities and three fellow cities shared knowledge through periodic meetings and collaborative learning processes. Utility companies, transportation operators, and technology providers engaged through technical working groups addressing integration challenges.

**Participatory Elements**

Participation represented a core initiative principle rather than an add-on. The SharingMi digital platform created ongoing engagement mechanisms, with over 1,100 users signing up within three months of launch and sharing over 3,600 stories.[^3] Users earned rewards ("claps" converted to points) for sustainable behavior, creating incentives for continued participation.[^3] The 4,200 Digital Social Market users across Milan, Lisbon, and Greenwich demonstrated substantial citizen engagement.[^2] Co-design processes for building retrofits involved residents in specifying technologies and configurations, ensuring solutions aligned with occupant needs and preferences. Participatory budgeting mechanisms gave residents voice in allocating resources. The initiative recognized that sustained behavior change required not merely technology deployment but genuine community ownership and participation in solution design.

**Project Management**

The initiative employed structured project management approaches appropriate to its scale and complexity. The Greater London Authority provided overall programme coordination, with dedicated programme management functions. Each lighthouse city (Milan, London, Lisbon) had a city-level project manager coordinating local implementation. Technical working groups addressed specific domains (energy, mobility, data systems, etc.), with representatives from public, private, and academic sectors. Regular monitoring and evaluation processes tracked progress against objectives, with quarterly and annual reporting to EU funders. Adaptive management approaches enabled mid-course corrections based on implementation experience. The project management structure balanced central coordination (necessary for ensuring coherence and knowledge transfer) with local autonomy (necessary for adapting to local contexts).

#### **Financing Model**

**Budget and Costs**

Total project budget across all sites was €24-25 million in EU seed funding through Horizon 2020.[^1][^2] Milan's local partnership received €8.6 million of this allocation.[^1] However, the initiative successfully leveraged additional private investment, ultimately attracting €265 million in further investment in the smart city marketplace.[^2] This represented approximately 11:1 leverage ratio, demonstrating the initiative's success in catalyzing private sector engagement. Cost breakdowns by component reflected the integrated nature of the intervention: building retrofits represented substantial costs (improving 20 buildings to achieve 70% energy reductions); mobility infrastructure (60 charging stations, bike-sharing points, car-sharing systems); smart infrastructure (lighting, sensors, parking systems); and platform development and integration.

**Funding Mix**

Financing combined public funding (EU Horizon 2020 grants, municipal government contributions), private investment (from technology companies, utilities, and service providers), and academic institution resources (research time, facilities). The public funding provided seed capital and risk reduction, enabling private companies to invest with greater confidence. The leverage of private investment beyond initial EU funding demonstrated market viability of smart city solutions and ensured financial sustainability beyond the formal programme period.

**Revenue Mechanisms**

The initiative explored multiple revenue mechanisms for long-term sustainability. Shared mobility services generated revenue through user fees (car-sharing, bike-sharing subscriptions). Energy savings from retrofits created ongoing cost reductions for building occupants and operators, providing financial incentives for continued participation. Data services and analytics from the Urban Sharing Platform represented potential revenue streams for platform operators and service providers. Municipal governments benefited from reduced service delivery costs (e.g., optimized street lighting reducing energy consumption and maintenance). The SharingMi platform's reward system, while initially funded through the project, could potentially transition to revenue models involving local businesses offering discounts or services to platform users.

**Financial Sustainability**

The initiative explicitly designed for long-term financial sustainability. The Urban Sharing Platform was structured for "longevity beyond the Sharing Cities programme," with governance and technical design enabling continued evolution after EU funding concluded.[^3] Private sector engagement ensured that viable business models would continue operating after public funding ended. Building retrofits created ongoing cost savings motivating continued maintenance and optimization. Shared mobility services established customer bases and operational models capable of sustaining operations independently. Municipal governments incorporated smart city solutions into standard infrastructure and service delivery, ensuring continued investment. However, sustainability challenges remained: some services (particularly those addressing equity or environmental goals without direct revenue) required continued public subsidy; technology platforms required ongoing maintenance and updating; and behavioral incentive mechanisms (like the SharingMi reward system) required sustained funding.

### **V. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTIVE RESPONSES**

#### **Implementation Challenges**

**Technical Challenges**

Integrating diverse technology systems from different vendors and across different urban domains proved complex. Smart sensors and IoT platforms required reliable connectivity, which was not uniformly available throughout demonstration districts. Data standardization and interoperability challenges emerged as different systems used incompatible formats and protocols. Building retrofit work encountered technical difficulties including structural complications, contamination in brownfield sites, and unforeseen conditions revealed during renovation. Electric vehicle charging infrastructure required coordination with electrical grid operators and sometimes revealed grid capacity constraints. Weather events and environmental conditions (extreme temperatures, flooding) tested the resilience of installed systems.

**Financial Challenges**

Building retrofit costs sometimes exceeded initial estimates due to unforeseen structural issues and contamination remediation requirements in brownfield sites. Some shared mobility services struggled to achieve financial viability in early phases, requiring continued subsidy. Technology development and integration costs exceeded projections in some areas. Currency fluctuations and inflation affected costs for international partnerships. The challenge of transitioning from project-based funding (EU grants with defined endpoints) to sustained operational funding created sustainability pressures.

**Social and Political Challenges**

Displacement concerns emerged in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district as redevelopment increased property values and attracted higher-income residents, potentially displacing existing low-income populations. Gentrification pressures required deliberate policy responses including social housing requirements and tenant protections. Resident participation, while substantial, proved uneven, with some groups (particularly elderly residents and those with limited digital literacy) underrepresented in digital engagement platforms. Political changes (election cycles, leadership transitions) sometimes created uncertainty about municipal commitment. Competing municipal priorities occasionally diverted attention and resources from Sharing Cities initiatives. Community opposition emerged in some cases, particularly regarding specific interventions (e.g., concerns about smart lighting and surveillance, parking restrictions to encourage shared mobility).

**Administrative Challenges**

EU procurement regulations, while designed to ensure fairness, sometimes slowed decision-making and limited flexibility for innovation. Coordination across multiple governmental levels (EU, national, municipal) created bureaucratic complexity. Data governance challenges emerged as different agencies and private companies had conflicting interests regarding data access, ownership, and usage. Building ownership structures in retrofit initiatives required negotiation with multiple stakeholders, complicating decision-making. Utility concession agreements sometimes limited opportunities for service innovation or created conflicts between utility interests and sustainability objectives.

**Environmental Challenges**

Brownfield contamination in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia site required remediation before development could proceed, adding costs and complexity. Climate variability affected some systems (e.g., extreme heat reducing electric vehicle charging efficiency; flooding affecting infrastructure). Environmental impacts of construction and retrofit work, while generally positive, required management to minimize disruption. Supply chain challenges for sustainable materials and technologies sometimes limited options or increased costs.

#### **Adaptive Strategies**

**Problem-Solving Approaches**

The initiative employed several adaptive strategies in response to implementation challenges. For technical integration challenges, the Urban Sharing Platform was designed with flexibility to accommodate different systems and evolving standards, rather than requiring all systems to conform to predetermined specifications. For financial challenges, the initiative pursued diverse revenue mechanisms and continued seeking private investment beyond initial EU funding. For social challenges, enhanced community engagement processes were implemented, with specific efforts to reach underrepresented populations through multiple channels (digital and non-digital). For administrative challenges, the initiative worked with EU and national authorities to clarify regulations and streamline processes, while also building relationships with municipal departments to improve coordination.

**Design Modifications**

Several design modifications emerged from implementation experience. The SharingMi digital platform evolved based on user feedback, with interface improvements and expanded features to increase accessibility and engagement. Building retrofit specifications were adjusted based on occupant feedback and performance monitoring, ensuring solutions aligned with actual needs and preferences. Shared mobility service configurations were modified based on usage patterns, with adjustments to charging station locations, bike-sharing point distribution, and service hours. The Urban Sharing Platform architecture was made more modular and flexible to accommodate integration of new systems and evolving technologies. Community engagement processes were expanded to include more face-to-face interactions and non-digital channels, recognizing that digital-only approaches excluded important populations.

**Lessons Learned During Implementation**

Several key lessons emerged from implementation experience:[^1][^2] Genuine citizen participation requires sustained effort and multiple engagement channels, not merely one-time consultations. Technology deployment alone is insufficient; social and governance innovations are equally important for achieving behavior change and sustainability. Long-term funding mechanisms must be established during project design, not treated as afterthought. Data integration and interoperability are more complex than anticipated and require early attention to standards and governance. Equity considerations (ensuring that benefits reach all residents and that displacement is prevented) must be embedded in project design from inception, not addressed reactively. Private sector engagement is essential for financial sustainability and scale-up, but requires clear alignment between public interest and business viability. Flexibility and adaptive management are necessary; rigid adherence to initial plans prevents learning and optimization.

### **VI. OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS**

**Physical Outputs**

- 20 buildings in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district underwent comprehensive energy retrofits with envelope insulation, renewable energy integration, and smart controls[^1][^3]
- 60 electric vehicle charging stations installed (including 20 fast-charging points) at ten mobility areas near public transit connections[^1]
- 14 bike-sharing points equipped with electric bikes, including models with child seats[^1]
- Smart parking sensors (75 total) deployed throughout the district[^1]
- Smart street lighting installed across the demonstration district with integrated environmental sensors[^1]
- 187,226 m² public park created as part of Porta Romana/Vettabbia redevelopment[^3]
- Multimodal transit station built to integrate transportation modes[^3]
- Social and private housing units developed as part of district redevelopment[^3]

**Service Delivery**

- Building energy management services providing real-time monitoring and optimization[^1][^3]
- Shared electric car-sharing services for residents and visitors[^1]
- Enhanced bike-sharing with electric bikes and child seats, expanding accessibility to cycling[^1]
- Shared logistics services with ten electric vehicles for goods transport[^1]
- Smart parking services enabling drivers to locate available spaces through mobile app[^1]
- Smart lighting services with adaptive illumination and environmental monitoring[^1]
- Urban Sharing Platform providing integrated data access and service coordination[^3]
- Digital Social Market (SharingMi) platform enabling citizen engagement and rewards[^1][^3]
- Environmental monitoring services providing real-time air quality, noise, and traffic data[^3]

**Quantitative Metrics**

- 70% reduction in energy needs for retrofitted buildings[^1][^3]
- 6.1 GWh/year decrease in building energy needs across Milan, Lisbon, and Greenwich[^2]
- 50% energy savings from energy efficiency measures across demonstration districts[^2]
- 4,500 tons of CO₂ emissions saved through shared mobility measures across all cities[^2]
- 1,600 e-vehicles deployed across London, Lisbon, and Milan[^2]
- 900 dwellings directly engaged in deep energy retrofits[^2]
- 4,200 Digital Social Market users across Milan, Lisbon, and Greenwich[^2]
- 1,100 SharingMi users in Milan within three months of launch, sharing 3,600 stories[^3]
- More than 1,000 downloads of Sharing Cities playbooks[^2]
- 10 distinct technologies deployed and tested[^2]
- Over 100 cities engaged and using playbooks, technologies, or products[^2]

#### **Social Impacts**

**Community Benefits**

Residents of the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district experienced multiple quality-of-life improvements. Retrofitted housing provided enhanced thermal comfort, improved indoor air quality, and reduced energy bills, directly benefiting occupant wellbeing. Expanded mobility options (shared cars, electric bikes, improved public transit) reduced reliance on private vehicles, improving accessibility for residents without cars and reducing transportation costs. Enhanced green space (the 187,226 m² park) provided recreational opportunities and environmental amenities. Smart lighting and environmental monitoring contributed to improved safety and environmental quality. The integration of the historically separated district with the city center and agricultural belt reduced geographic and social isolation.

**Equity Outcomes**

The initiative deliberately pursued equity objectives, though with mixed results. Social housing requirements in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia redevelopment ensured that lower-income residents could access the transformed neighborhood, preventing complete displacement.[^3] The SharingMi platform's reward system provided incentives for sustainable behavior that could benefit lower-income residents by reducing transportation and energy costs. Digital platform design aimed for accessibility, though digital divides remained, with some residents (particularly elderly and those with limited digital literacy) underrepresented in online engagement. The initiative's emphasis on citizen participation created opportunities for residents to shape outcomes, though participation patterns reflected existing inequalities in civic engagement.

**Displacement and Gentrification**

The Porta Romana/Vettabbia redevelopment created gentrification pressures as property values increased and the district attracted higher-income residents and businesses. The initiative addressed this through deliberate policy responses including social housing requirements ensuring that a portion of new housing remained affordable. However, gentrification pressures extended beyond the redevelopment site to surrounding neighborhoods, potentially displacing existing residents. The initiative's success in making the district more attractive and desirable, while positive in many respects, created tensions with equity objectives.

**Social Capital**

The initiative strengthened social capital through multiple mechanisms. Participatory co-design processes created opportunities for residents to work together on shared challenges, building community connections. The SharingMi platform created spaces for residents to share experiences and ideas, fostering social networks around sustainability. Shared mobility services (car-sharing, bike-sharing) created informal opportunities for residents to interact. However, digital platforms also risked fragmenting community engagement, with online interaction potentially substituting for face-to-face community building.

**Cultural Impacts**

The initiative positioned Milan as a leader in European smart city innovation, enhancing the city's international reputation and cultural identity. The transformation of the Porta Romana/Vettabbia brownfield site from post-industrial wasteland to vibrant mixed-use neighborhood represented significant cultural and symbolic transformation. However, the initiative's emphasis on technological solutions and sustainability sometimes overshadowed local cultural heritage and traditions. The redevelopment's scale and pace created cultural disruption for existing residents whose neighborhood was being transformed.

#### **Economic Impacts**

**Job Creation**

The initiative created employment opportunities across multiple sectors. Construction and retrofit work provided direct employment for skilled trades (electricians, insulation specialists, heating engineers). Technology companies (Siemens, Danfoss, software developers) expanded operations and hired staff. Shared mobility services (car-sharing, bike-sharing) required operational staff. The initiative aimed to create "at least 100 jobs in three districts," though precise job creation figures across all sites are not fully detailed in available sources.[^2] Beyond direct employment, the initiative supported emerging sectors including smart building technology, sustainable mobility services, and data analytics.

**Economic Activity**

The initiative stimulated broader economic activity. Real estate development in Porta Romana/Vettabbia represented substantial economic investment and activity. Technology companies gained market experience and developed business models for smart city solutions. Energy retrofits created demand for specialized services and products. Shared mobility services generated ongoing economic activity. The Urban Sharing Platform and related digital services represented emerging business opportunities.

**Property Values**

Property values in the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district increased significantly following redevelopment and Sharing Cities interventions. This reflected the district's transformation from brownfield to desirable mixed-use neighborhood with enhanced amenities, sustainability features, and connectivity. However, rising property values created gentrification pressures and displacement risks for lower-income residents, representing a trade-off between economic development and equity objectives.

**Cost Savings**

Significant cost savings accrued to multiple beneficiaries. Building occupants realized 50-70% reductions in energy costs through retrofits.[^1][^3] Municipal governments reduced street lighting energy consumption and maintenance costs through smart lighting systems. Residents using shared mobility services reduced transportation costs compared to private vehicle ownership. Optimized parking systems reduced time spent searching for parking, generating indirect economic benefits through reduced fuel consumption and time savings.

**Economic Inclusion**

The initiative's effects on economic inclusion were mixed. Enhanced mobility options and reduced transportation costs potentially benefited lower-income residents. Energy cost savings from retrofits improved household finances, particularly for energy-poor households. Shared mobility services provided transportation access to residents without personal vehicles. However, the initiative's emphasis on technology and digital platforms risked excluding residents with limited digital literacy or access. The gentrification pressures created by successful redevelopment potentially displaced lower-income residents and small businesses, reducing economic opportunities for existing populations.

#### **Environmental Impacts**

**Climate and Emissions**

The initiative achieved substantial greenhouse gas emission reductions. Building energy retrofits reducing consumption by 50-70% translated to major operational carbon reductions.[^1][^3][^2] Shared mobility measures saved 4,500 tons of CO₂ emissions across all demonstration cities.[^2] The deployment of 1,600 e-vehicles across London, Lisbon, and Milan eliminated tailpipe emissions from those vehicles, though total lifecycle emissions depend on electricity grid composition. Overall, the initiative demonstrated significant potential for neighborhood-scale interventions to reduce urban carbon footprints. The initiative contributed to climate resilience through improved building thermal performance (reducing vulnerability to extreme heat) and diversified energy sources (reducing dependence on centralized systems).

**Resource Management**

Energy management systems enabled optimization of consumption, reducing waste and costs. Smart sensors provided real-time data on resource use, enabling behavior change and system optimization. Building retrofits incorporated renewable energy sources (solar panels, heat pumps), reducing dependence on fossil fuels. Water management systems (though less prominently featured than energy systems) were incorporated into some retrofits. Material efficiency in construction and retrofits aimed to minimize waste and environmental impact.

**Environmental Quality**

Multiple environmental quality improvements resulted from the initiative. Air quality improved due to reduced vehicle emissions from shared mobility services and modal shift away from private cars. Noise levels decreased as traffic congestion reduced and electric vehicles replaced combustion engines. Green space increased through the creation of the 187,226 m² park in Porta Romana/Vettabbia, providing habitat and environmental amenities. Smart lighting and environmental monitoring systems enabled proactive environmental management. However, construction and retrofit activities created temporary disruptions to environmental quality.

**Waste and Pollution**

The initiative reduced waste through material efficiency in retrofits and shared logistics services using electric vehicles. Construction waste was managed according to EU and Italian regulations, with recycling and reuse where possible. The shift to shared mobility reduced vehicle production waste (fewer vehicles needed to serve the same transportation demand). However, the initiative generated some waste through technology deployment and system upgrades, requiring responsible disposal and recycling.

**Sustainability Metrics**

The initiative achieved strong performance against environmental sustainability metrics. The 6.1 GWh/year decrease in building energy needs across demonstration districts represented approximately 1,830 tons of CO₂ equivalent annual reductions.[^2] The 4,500 tons of CO₂ saved through shared mobility measures represented approximately 2,250 tons of CO₂ equivalent annual reductions (assuming vehicles replaced approximately 3,000 tons of annual emissions). Combined environmental impacts demonstrated substantial progress toward urban decarbonization objectives, though representing a fraction of total urban energy and transport emissions.

#### **Governance and Institutional Impacts**

**Institutional Capacity**

The initiative strengthened institutional capacity across multiple organizations. Politecnico di Milano developed expertise in smart city implementation and monitoring. Comune di Milano built capacity in integrated urban development and stakeholder coordination. Utility companies (A2A, Unareti) developed experience with smart energy management and demand-side engagement. Private technology companies expanded capabilities in urban system integration. The initiative created new collaborative structures and processes that enhanced organizational capacity for future initiatives.

**Policy Influence**

The Sharing Cities initiative influenced policy development at multiple levels. The success of building retrofits influenced EU energy efficiency policy and inspired similar retrofit programmes across Europe. The shared mobility models influenced transport policy in multiple cities. The participatory engagement approaches influenced governance practices emphasizing citizen co-design. The initiative's demonstration of viable smart city business models influenced investment and procurement policies. Milan's experience influenced Italian national policy on smart cities and sustainability.

**Transparency and Accountability**

The initiative improved governance transparency through several mechanisms. The Urban Sharing Platform provided residents and officials with real-time environmental and system performance data, enabling informed decision-making. The SharingMi platform created spaces for citizens to access information and participate in governance. Regular public reporting on initiative progress and impacts enhanced accountability. However, concerns about data privacy and surveillance required careful governance of monitoring systems, balancing transparency with privacy protection.

#### **Performance Against Objectives**

**Success Metrics**

The initiative achieved strong performance against most stated objectives. The goal to "aggregate demand and deploy smart city solutions" exceeded expectations, with more than 100 cities engaged (rather than the initial target of several dozen).[^2] The objective to "deliver common and replicable innovative models" was achieved, with 10 measures deployed in lighthouse cities and successfully replicated in fellow cities.[^2] The goal to "attract external investment" significantly exceeded expectations, with €265 million in additional investment triggered (compared to €24-25 million in seed funding).[^2] Building retrofit objectives (50-70% energy reductions) were achieved.[^1][^3][^2] Shared mobility deployment objectives (1,600 e-vehicles across three cities) were met.[^2] Citizen engagement objectives (4,200 Digital Social Market users) exceeded initial expectations.[^2]

**Unintended Consequences**

Positive unintended consequences included the generation of replicable playbooks and models that influenced urban development practice beyond the formal programme. The initiative's success in attracting private investment created momentum for continued smart city development. The social networks and partnerships formed during the initiative extended beyond its formal lifespan, creating lasting collaborative relationships.

Negative unintended consequences included gentrification pressures and displacement risks in demonstration districts. The emphasis on technology deployment sometimes obscured social and governance dimensions of sustainable development. Digital engagement platforms, while innovative, sometimes excluded residents with limited digital literacy or access. The initiative's visibility and success attracted attention and investment that transformed neighborhoods, creating disruption for existing residents.

**Long-term Viability**

The initiative demonstrated strong long-term viability. Building retrofits represented permanent improvements to housing stock, with ongoing benefits through reduced energy costs and improved comfort. Shared mobility services established operational models and customer bases capable of sustaining operations after project funding concluded. The Urban Sharing Platform was explicitly designed for "longevity beyond the Sharing Cities programme," with governance structures enabling continued evolution.[^3] Private sector engagement ensured financial sustainability of many services. However, some elements (particularly behavioral incentive mechanisms and equity-focused services) required continued public subsidy for long-term viability.

### **VII. TRANSFERABILITY AND BROADER RELEVANCE**

#### **Replicability Assessment**

**Contextual Dependencies**

Several elements of the Sharing Cities initiative depended significantly on local context. The availability of a large brownfield site (Porta Romana/Vettabbia) provided a specific opportunity for comprehensive neighborhood redevelopment that may not exist in all cities. Milan's existing institutional capacity (strong municipal government, research universities, engaged private sector) facilitated implementation in ways that might not be available in cities with weaker institutions. The Italian regulatory environment, while sometimes challenging, provided certain enabling frameworks (e.g., social housing requirements) that differ across European jurisdictions. Milan's economic vitality and investment capacity enabled private sector engagement at scales that might not be achievable in economically distressed cities. The city's existing commitment to sustainability and smart cities (evident in pre-2016 strategic plans) created readiness that other cities might lack.

However, core elements of the initiative proved transferable across diverse contexts. The three fellow cities (Bordeaux, Burgas, and Warsaw) successfully adapted and replicated Sharing Cities measures despite different urban contexts, institutional capacities, and economic conditions.[^2] This demonstrated that while specific implementations varied, the underlying models and approaches could be adapted to different settings.

**Critical Success Factors**

Several factors emerged as critical to positive outcomes. Strong municipal leadership and commitment to sustainability objectives proved essential for maintaining focus and resources across the multi-year implementation period. Genuine partnership among public, private, and academic sectors, with aligned incentives and shared commitment to objectives, enabled coordination and innovation. Sustained citizen engagement through multiple channels (not merely digital) proved essential for behavior change and social acceptance. Adequate and sustained funding, combining public seed funding with private investment, enabled implementation at necessary scale. Flexibility and adaptive management, allowing mid-course corrections based on implementation experience, proved important for navigating unexpected challenges. Early attention to equity and displacement concerns, rather than treating these as afterthoughts, proved important for ensuring inclusive outcomes. Long-term institutional commitment to maintaining systems and continuing evolution after project funding concluded proved essential for sustainability.

**Scalability**

The initiative demonstrated scalability across multiple dimensions. Geographic scalability was evident in successful replication in fellow cities (Bordeaux, Burgas, Warsaw) and the engagement of more than 100 municipalities worldwide.[^2] Sectoral scalability was demonstrated through application of core models across energy, mobility, and data management domains. Temporal scalability was evident in the transition from project-based funding to sustained operational funding in some areas. However, scalability faced constraints: the intensive coordination and engagement required during initial implementation became more challenging at larger scales; the availability of funding and institutional capacity varied across cities; and the tailoring necessary for local contexts sometimes limited economies of scale.

#### **Comparative Analysis**

**Similar Initiatives**

Sharing Cities represents one of several major European smart cities initiatives funded under Horizon 2020 and related programmes. The Triangulum project (also Horizon 2020-funded) demonstrated smart city solutions in Manchester, Genoa, and Nantes, with similar focus on integrated neighborhood-scale interventions combining energy, mobility, and ICT. The REMOURBAN project focused on urban regeneration through smart technologies in Valladolid, Nottingham, and Palencia. The SUNRISE project addressed sustainable urban regeneration and innovation in five European cities. These initiatives share common characteristics: focus on neighborhood or district scale; integration across multiple urban domains; combination of technological, social, and governance innovations; emphasis on replicability and knowledge transfer; and public-private-academic partnerships. However, Sharing Cities distinguished itself through particularly strong emphasis on citizen engagement and participatory co-design, and through the successful leverage of private investment (€265 million triggered by €24-25 million public seed funding).

**Best Practices**

Several elements of Sharing Cities represent exemplary practice in urban development: **Integrated neighborhood-scale intervention** combining energy, mobility, and digital systems proved more effective than sectoral approaches addressing these domains separately. **Genuine citizen participation** through co-design processes and participatory governance, rather than top-down planning, enhanced both outcomes and social acceptance. **Public-private-academic partnerships** combining public sector authority and resources, private sector innovation and efficiency, and academic research and expertise proved more effective than single-sector approaches. **Emphasis on replicability and knowledge transfer** through playbooks and peer learning networks extended impact beyond demonstration sites. **Long-term institutional commitment** ensuring sustainability beyond project funding proved essential for lasting impact. **Flexibility and adaptive management** allowing mid-course corrections based on implementation experience improved outcomes compared to rigid adherence to initial plans.

**Cautionary Lessons**

Several cautionary lessons emerged from Sharing Cities and similar initiatives: **Technology deployment alone is insufficient**; social and governance innovations are equally important for achieving behavior change and sustainability. **Gentrification and displacement risks** require proactive policy responses (social housing requirements, tenant protections) embedded in project design from inception, not treated reactively. **Digital engagement platforms** can exclude residents with limited digital literacy or access; multiple engagement channels (digital and non-digital) are necessary for inclusive participation. **Private sector engagement**, while essential for financial sustainability and scale-up, requires careful governance to ensure alignment with public interest objectives. **Equity considerations** must be embedded in project design from inception; retrofitting equity into existing initiatives proves more difficult. **Long-term funding mechanisms** must be established during project design; relying on project-based funding creates sustainability challenges. **Institutional capacity** varies across cities; initiatives must be tailored to local institutional contexts rather than applying uniform models.

#### **Parallels to Green Building Neighborhoods (GBN) Use Case**

**Contextual Similarities**

The Sharing Cities Milan demonstration shares several characteristics with green building neighborhood (GBN) initiatives generally. Both focus on neighborhood or district-scale interventions combining multiple sustainability dimensions. Both emphasize building energy efficiency through retrofits and advanced systems. Both incorporate renewable energy and district-scale energy management. Both address sustainable mobility through shared services and modal shift away from private vehicles. Both employ data-driven monitoring and optimization of building and neighborhood performance. Both emphasize occupant experience and quality of life alongside environmental performance.

**Methodological Parallels**

Both Sharing Cities and typical GBN initiatives employ integrated design approaches combining built environment, infrastructure, and social/governance dimensions. Both emphasize performance monitoring and adaptive management based on real-world data. Both incorporate stakeholder engagement and participation in design and governance. Both pursue multiple sustainability objectives simultaneously (energy efficiency, emissions reduction, mobility, environmental quality, social inclusion) rather than optimizing for single metrics. Both recognize that technological solutions alone are insufficient without social and behavioral dimensions.

**Technological Overlaps**

Both employ similar technology suites: smart building energy management systems with sensors and controls; renewable energy integration; district heating/cooling systems; electric mobility infrastructure; smart sensors for environmental monitoring; digital platforms for data integration and service coordination. Both emphasize interoperability and integration across previously fragmented systems. Both recognize that data from multiple sources must be synthesized to enable optimization and informed decision-making.

**Stakeholder Dynamics**

Both involve complex multi-stakeholder partnerships combining municipal government, private companies, academic institutions, and residents. Both require alignment of incentives across stakeholders with potentially conflicting interests (municipal interest in sustainability vs. private sector interest in profitability; resident interest in affordability vs. developer interest in returns). Both employ governance structures balancing central coordination with local autonomy. Both recognize that sustainable neighborhoods require sustained stakeholder engagement beyond initial project delivery.

**Transferable Strategies**

Several Sharing Cities strategies are directly transferable to other GBN initiatives: **Participatory co-design processes** involving residents in specifying technologies and configurations ensure solutions align with occupant needs and preferences. **Behavioral incentive mechanisms** (like the SharingMi reward system) encourage sustained engagement with sustainable practices. **Integration of energy, mobility, and data systems** through platforms like the Urban Sharing Platform enables optimization across domains. **Public-private-academic partnerships** combining complementary capabilities and resources enable implementation at necessary scale. **Long-term institutional commitment** to maintaining systems and continuing evolution beyond initial project funding ensures sustainability. **Emphasis on replicability** through playbooks and peer learning networks extends impact beyond individual projects. **Adaptive management approaches** allowing mid-course corrections based on monitoring data improve outcomes.

**Adaptation Considerations**

While Sharing Cities strategies are transferable, adaptation to specific contexts is necessary. The scale and scope of GBN initiatives varies; smaller neighborhoods may not justify the intensive coordination and technology deployment appropriate to larger sites. Institutional capacity varies across cities; initiatives must be tailored to local governance structures and capabilities. Economic contexts differ; financing models must reflect local investment capacity and market conditions. Regulatory environments vary; initiatives must align with local zoning, building codes, and planning regulations. Cultural contexts differ; engagement approaches and technology acceptance vary across populations. Climate contexts vary; energy and mobility solutions must be tailored to local conditions (e.g., heating-focused retrofits in cold climates vs. cooling-focused in hot climates).

### **VIII. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT AND REFLECTION**

#### **Strengths of the Initiative**

**Integrated Approach**

The Sharing Cities initiative's most significant strength was its integrated approach combining energy, mobility, data systems, and social/governance innovations within single neighborhoods.[^1][^2] Rather than addressing these domains separately (as is common in urban development), the integrated approach enabled synergies and optimization across systems. Building retrofits reducing energy demand complemented renewable energy integration and district heating systems. Shared mobility services reduced transportation demand, improving air quality and reducing congestion. Smart sensors and data systems enabled real-time monitoring and optimization across all domains. This integration proved more effective than sectoral approaches.

**Genuine Citizen Engagement**

The initiative's emphasis on genuine citizen participation, through co-design processes and participatory governance, distinguished it from top-down smart city projects. The SharingMi platform's engagement of 4,200 users across three cities, with over 1,100 users in Milan within three months of launch, demonstrated substantial citizen participation.[^3][^2] Residents participated in specifying retrofit technologies and configurations, ensuring solutions aligned with occupant needs and preferences. This participatory approach enhanced both outcomes (solutions better matched to actual needs) and social acceptance (residents felt ownership of initiatives they helped design).

**Significant Environmental Outcomes**

The initiative achieved substantial environmental benefits. Building retrofits reduced energy consumption by 50-70%, translating to major operational carbon reductions.[^1][^3][^2] The 6.1 GWh/year decrease in building energy needs across demonstration districts represented approximately 1,830 tons of CO₂ equivalent annual reductions.[^2] Shared mobility services saved 4,500 tons of CO₂ emissions across all cities.[^2] These outcomes demonstrated the significant potential for neighborhood-scale interventions to reduce urban carbon footprints and contribute to climate goals.

**Financial Leverage**

The initiative successfully attracted €265 million in private investment beyond the €24-25 million in EU seed funding, representing an 11:1 leverage ratio.[^2] This demonstrated that smart city solutions could attract private sector engagement and that viable business models existed for sustainable urban development. The financial leverage ensured sustainability beyond project funding and enabled scaling beyond what public funding alone could achieve.

**Replication and Knowledge Transfer**

The initiative's success in replicating measures in fellow cities (Bordeaux, Burgas, Warsaw) despite different urban contexts and institutional capacities demonstrated high replication potential.[^2] The development of playbooks and peer learning networks enabled knowledge transfer to more than 100 municipalities worldwide.[^2] This emphasis on replication and knowledge transfer extended impact far beyond the three lighthouse cities, multiplying the initiative's significance.

**Multi-Level Governance Innovation**

The initiative's public-private-academic partnership model, linking European, national, and local authorities, represented governance innovation. This multi-level approach combined the resources and capabilities of different sectors in ways that single-sector initiatives could not achieve. The collaborative structures and processes created during the initiative extended beyond its formal lifespan, establishing lasting relationships and networks.

#### **Limitations and Weaknesses**

**Gentrification and Displacement Risks**

While the initiative addressed equity concerns through social housing requirements and tenant protections, gentrification pressures and displacement risks remained significant challenges. The transformation of the Porta Romana/Vettabbia district from brownfield to desirable mixed-use neighborhood with enhanced amenities increased property values, attracting higher-income residents and businesses. This created pressures on lower-income residents and small businesses, potentially displacing existing populations. While the initiative attempted to mitigate these risks, the fundamental tension between successful urban development and gentrification prevention remained incompletely resolved.

**Digital Divides**

The initiative's emphasis on digital platforms and smart technologies, while innovative, risked excluding residents with limited digital literacy or access. The SharingMi platform and Urban Sharing Platform required digital access and technical competence. Elderly residents, those with limited education, and those without reliable internet access faced barriers to participation. While the initiative recognized these challenges and attempted to provide non-digital engagement channels, digital divides persisted.

**Limited Behavioral Change at Scale**

While the initiative demonstrated that behavioral incentives could encourage sustainable choices among engaged residents (evidenced by 4,200 Digital Social Market users), scaling these approaches to influence population-wide behavior remained challenging.[^2] The 1,100 SharingMi users in Milan, while substantial, represented a small fraction of the district's population. Sustaining behavior change beyond the project period, particularly after incentive mechanisms ended, remained uncertain.

**Technology Dependency and Maintenance**

The initiative's reliance on smart sensors, IoT platforms, and digital systems created ongoing maintenance and updating requirements. Technology systems required regular maintenance, software updates, and eventual replacement. The long-term viability of these systems depended on sustained institutional commitment and funding. Some technology components (e.g., smart sensors) required periodic replacement as devices failed or became obsolete. The initiative's sustainability depended on establishing reliable maintenance and replacement mechanisms.

**Incomplete Data on Long-Term Outcomes**

While the initiative reported strong outcomes at project conclusion (December 2021), comprehensive long-term data on sustained impacts remained limited at the time of available documentation. Questions remained about whether energy savings and behavioral changes persisted after project funding ended and incentive mechanisms were withdrawn. Whether shared mobility services achieved financial sustainability without ongoing subsidy remained uncertain. Whether the collaborative networks and institutional innovations persisted and continued generating value remained to be fully documented.

**Limited Coverage of Broader Urban Challenges**

While the initiative addressed energy, mobility, and environmental quality in demonstration districts, broader urban challenges remained incompletely addressed. Housing affordability, economic inequality, and social inclusion challenges extended beyond the scope of Sharing Cities interventions. The initiative's focus on technology-enabled solutions, while valuable, addressed only partial dimensions of urban sustainability.

#### **Equity and Justice Considerations**

**Inclusive Development**

The initiative pursued inclusive development through multiple mechanisms: social housing requirements ensuring lower-income residents could access redeveloped neighborhoods; participatory governance creating opportunities for resident voice in decision-making; energy cost savings benefiting lower-income households disproportionately (as a percentage of income); and shared mobility services providing transportation access to residents without personal vehicles. However, these mechanisms only partially addressed underlying inequalities, and gentrification pressures created counterbalancing displacement risks.

**Environmental Justice**

The initiative advanced environmental justice by improving air quality and environmental conditions in demonstration districts, benefiting residents of these neighborhoods disproportionately. The focus on retrofitting residential buildings in lower-income areas (Porta Romana/Vettabbia had substantial social housing) ensured that environmental benefits reached vulnerable populations. Smart environmental monitoring systems provided residents with information about local environmental conditions, supporting informed decision-making. However, the initiative's benefits were concentrated in demonstration districts; broader environmental justice questions about equitable distribution of environmental improvements across the city remained incompletely addressed.

**Procedural Justice**

The initiative advanced procedural justice through participatory governance and citizen engagement mechanisms. Residents participated in co-designing interventions, contributing their knowledge and preferences to decision-making. The SharingMi platform provided mechanisms for residents to voice concerns and ideas. Regular community meetings created forums for dialogue. However, participation patterns reflected existing inequalities in civic engagement; some populations (particularly those with limited time, digital literacy, or language proficiency) were underrepresented.

**Recognition Justice**

The initiative recognized residents as knowledge holders and decision-makers, rather than passive recipients of development. Participatory co-design processes acknowledged resident expertise about their own neighborhoods and needs. The SharingMi platform recognized residents as contributors to urban knowledge and problem-solving. However, recognition remained incomplete; expert knowledge (from academics, professionals, officials) was often privileged over resident knowledge.

#### **Future Outlook**

**Long-Term Trajectory**

The Sharing Cities initiative officially concluded in December 2021, but its impacts continue to evolve. The demonstration districts (particularly Porta Romana/Vettabbia in Milan) continue operating with the technologies and systems deployed during the initiative. The institutional partnerships and collaborative networks established during the initiative persist and continue generating value. The playbooks and knowledge resources developed during the initiative continue influencing urban development practice across Europe and globally.

**Ongoing Challenges**

Several challenges require ongoing attention. The sustainability of behavioral incentive mechanisms (like SharingMi rewards) depends on continued funding sources; whether these mechanisms will persist beyond project funding remains uncertain. The maintenance and updating of smart technology systems requires sustained institutional commitment and funding. Gentrification pressures in demonstration districts require ongoing policy attention to prevent displacement of lower-income residents. Digital divides require continued attention to ensure inclusive participation in digital platforms and services. The transition from project-based funding to sustained operational funding requires institutional innovation in financing mechanisms.

**Opportunities for Evolution**

Several opportunities exist for evolution and continuation. The collaborative networks and partnerships established during Sharing Cities could be leveraged for new initiatives addressing additional urban challenges. The data systems and monitoring infrastructure could be expanded to additional neighborhoods or cities. The successful replication in fellow cities could be extended to additional municipalities. The playbooks and knowledge resources could be updated based on post-project monitoring data. The business models developed during the initiative could be refined and scaled. The participatory governance approaches could be applied to additional urban challenges and domains.

**Policy and Practice Implications**

The Sharing Cities initiative has generated significant implications for urban development policy and practice. At the European level, the initiative has influenced Horizon 2020 and successor funding programmes' emphasis on smart cities and integrated neighborhood-scale interventions. At the national level, the initiative has influenced Italian policy on energy efficiency, sustainable mobility, and smart cities. At the municipal level, Milan has incorporated lessons from Sharing Cities into ongoing urban development strategies. Internationally, the initiative has influenced urban development practice in more than 100 municipalities worldwide through playbooks, peer learning networks, and direct replication efforts. The initiative has demonstrated that integrated, citizen-centered approaches to urban sustainability can achieve significant environmental, social, and economic benefits while establishing scalable, replicable models for other cities.

---

This comprehensive case study demonstrates that Sharing Cities represents a significant milestone in European urban development practice, successfully combining technological innovation, social engagement, governance innovation, and environmental outcomes at neighborhood scale. The initiative's emphasis on integration across multiple urban domains, genuine citizen participation, and replicability and knowledge transfer distinguishes it as exemplary practice. However, ongoing challenges related to gentrification, digital divides, and long-term sustainability require continued attention. The initiative's impacts extend far beyond the three lighthouse cities through influence on policy, practice, and institutional innovation across Europe and globally, establishing foundations for continued evolution of smart, sustainable, equitable urban development.
